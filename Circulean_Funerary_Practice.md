# Circulean Funerary Practice

## The Body at Death

A living body holds its own elemental ratios against the Aether's constant pressure toward equilibrium, and does so continuously, from its first breath to its last. Death is the cessation of that management. What remains does not become inert; it begins to equilibrate, dissolving toward the world's average across the weeks it takes a body to come apart. A corpse is therefore a sustained local disturbance in the Aether, resolving slowly and without supervision, and every working aethericist knows what sustained disturbance eventually coaxes out of the ambient field.

This single fact underwrites the whole of western funerary practice. The clergy of the Church of the Light teach that the Body is one third of the Heavenly Triad and that its honourable rendering bears on the weighing of the soul. The guilds and the mill-wardens who do the work speak instead of resolution, of ground gone bad, and of what settles into a chamber left too long untended. The two accounts agree completely on what must be done and disagree completely on why, and neither has ever needed the other's assent.

## The Vigil

From the moment of death until the pyre, the body is watched without interruption. The watch is kept in relays by kin and by neighbours of good standing, with a light burning throughout, and the watchers may not sleep. Nothing living is permitted to cross the body, no reflective surface is left facing it, and one aperture in the room is kept open. This is the most universally observed of all Circulean funerary strictures, kept in full by households that keep nothing else, and it is the reason the interval between death and burning is held as short as fuel and weather allow. An unattended disturbance is how something settles.

The Lanterns of First Bright are the same instinct enlarged to a whole city and a whole year, white lamps set in windows and along canals for absent kin and for the unremembered dead, whose vigils nobody sat.

## The Five Stations and the Silent Sixth

The rite renders the remainder through five acts, in an order fixed by process and not by cosmology. Fire takes the body on the pyre. Water takes it in the wash. Stone takes it at the mill. Ice holds it in the setting, where a setting has been bought. Air takes it at the dispersal.

Soil is held first and is never performed. The doctrine is that the body was Soil's own gift, drawn up through a lifetime of grain and grazing, and that Soil has been taking its share daily since the person began. The rite arrives already holding Soil in hand and renders only what is left. Nothing is refused and no element is slighted, which matters in a faith that orders the world by six and could not survive the charge of dropping one at the graveside. The formulation translates without difficulty into the impersonalised register of the Imperial Age, where it reads as a settled account, and it is the single point at which Kharunite debt-jurisprudence finds western funerary teaching congenial.

Beneath the doctrine lies the practice. The wash produces a caustic liquor that kills whatever it touches, and it is poured out on consecrated planting: a parish orchard, a withy-bed, a vineyard held by the chapter. The parish calls this the Soil-giving. The diocese calls it disposal and has said so in writing more than once. The fruit of such ground is sold at market, some households will not touch it, and others pay above the price. The argument over whether the pouring is a station or a convenience has been running since the practice began and shows no sign of resolution.

The order of the stations has never come out elegantly against the hexagon, since none of the five can be moved without making the work impossible. The Harmonists have written on the problem for six centuries. The Aureomedians answer it architecturally, laying out the stations of a funerary chapel so that each Antagonistic pair faces its partner across the floor, though the procession does not walk them in that order.

## The Plain Rite

The complete rite is Fire, Water, Stone, Air, and it is finished within days of death. It is what the great majority of Circuleans have received in every century of the faith, and it is doctrinally unimpeachable.

The pyre is lit from the parish brazier, and the carriage of that flame from brazier to pyre is a jealously held right of the trade that tends the fire. Fuel is the practical engine of the whole institution. Wood is expensive, an underburnt body is the western nightmare, and everything that has changed across the ages has changed for fuel. Parishes keep fixed pyre-days on which the accumulated dead are burnt together. Fuel charity is the standard pious bequest and the fuel-dole a recognised form of poor relief. Timber rights are fought over between neighbouring parishes with a bitterness that surprises outsiders, and the short burn is a recurring scandal in every century.

The gathering follows. What the fire leaves is collected, and whose hands do the collecting is the sharpest regional difference in western practice. The North requires the kin to gather with tongs, in order and by degree of relation, and regards paying a man to touch one's mother's bones as the exact moral failure of the age. The Commonwealth pays a man. Lavion pays a man and arranges that the family lifts the first token while the rest is done during prayer.

The wash leaches the burnt remains. The liquor goes to the consecrated planting; the insoluble fraction goes to the mill. Grinding is unremarkable labour, done at a mill whose stones grind nothing else and never have, and the sweepings between those stones are the mingled remains of strangers, which the wardens carry up with the rest.

The dispersal is made from an open platform raised beside the crypt, railed and reached by a stair, exposed on every side and worked on windy mornings. Parishes disperse their poor from these platforms on fixed days, several at once, so that the flour of strangers goes up together exactly as their ash lies together in the chamber below. Anyone may climb. Frequently nobody does, and the sexton and the mill-warden perform it alone.

The destitute therefore receive the rite in its complete and unaltered form, promptly and at no cost, and every Circulean knows it. Their dead are finished. The dead of the great houses are not and will not be for a century. Preachers have drawn the comparison for as long as there have been gems to draw it about, the poor draw it themselves at every opportunity, and it is the one moral advantage in Circulean society that cannot be purchased away.

## The Gem

The setting is an interval inserted between the wash and the mill. It holds the whole remainder in fixed form for as long as a family can pay for it and justify it. The gem is not the rite. The gem is the pause in it, and the mill remains where the rite ends.

Clerical usage calls it the reserved share. The vernacular does not. In the Commonwealth it is the grieving gem, in Lavion the griever's gem, in the North the death-gem, and using the wrong word in the wrong city marks a stranger at once. The Kharunites have called it corpse salt since the third century AR and have never been persuaded to call it anything else.

A gem is heavy for its size and takes two hands. Its size is a fact about the body that made it, and this is noticed and remarked on and cannot be negotiated, since the setting is made before witnesses. A tall man leaves a great deal. A child leaves what sits in a palm. A long wasting illness leaves less than the same person would have left five years earlier, and it is said at wakes that a man left a small gem, meaning what it sounds like it means. The ordinary shape of grief in a household of modest means is a shelf of very small gems, cheaply made because there was little to set.

### Origins in the Age of Enlightenment

For the first six centuries of the faith, from the revelation at An-Mirda to the maturity of the Theocracy, there was no gem at all. The crypt held urns, ash-galleries, and a common chamber, and the rite ran from pyre to wind within the week.

The gem was not invented for the laity and was not invented for grief. It came out of the Theocracy's own workshops, in the same generations in which monasteries became schools, provincial furnaces recovered techniques lost at the Fall, and the numerate clergy of the Eternal Dominion turned their confidence on every problem within reach. The problem in this case was custody. The Church held the ash of the Prophet's companions, of the Judges, and of the first Hierophants in urns that had already been shifted, relabelled, and recopied through six hundred years of war and removal, and it wanted a form that could be sealed, sighted, carried in procession, and counted. The alchemists of An-Mirda answered with a setting grown from the concentrated ash liquor: glassy, pale, salt-like, and, to the eye of the third century, incorruptible.

It was not incorruptible. It is soluble, and the discovery of that fact belongs to the following generation, by which time the practice was too far advanced and too well endowed to abandon. What the Church did instead was institutionalise the remedy. A gem must be kept dry, in a sealed case, in a dry crypt, the case renewed, the gem turned and checked on a rota. Permanence is no property of a gem. It is a service performed continuously by the living on the dead person's behalf, which is the same management against equilibrium the dead performed while alive and stopped performing at death.

Lay adoption followed within two generations and followed rank exactly. Exarchs and abbots first, then the magnate houses of the Dominion, then the great mercantile families of the coast, and never the countryside at all. The spread coincided precisely with the Theocracy's late ossification, and every preacher since has read it that way: a Church that had begun by teaching that the destitute and the Hierophant were rendered by the same five acts ended by selling the interval, and it ended by selling the interval in the decades before the Thronebreaker came through the gates. The Kharunite Rite, maturing in the east in those same years, named it corpse salt within the century and has never stopped saying that the West learned to hoard its dead in the same generation it learned to hoard everything else.

### The Fragile Age

The Sack of An-Mirda in 336 AR fell on a Church that had just finished converting its founders into portable objects. The relic gems were looted, dispersed, concealed, and carried out by fleeing exarchs, and the rival claims of the Second Hierophancy were claims to physical things that could be displayed and counted. Aramoor's authority and An-Mirda's were argued in gems, the Dark Ages made the traffic in them a trade, and the provenance of any gem said to predate the Sack is a matter no two patriarchates have ever agreed on.

The Dark Ages did to the gems what they did to everything else. A soluble object under a tending rota survives only as long as the rota does, and across six centuries of braided obediences, abandoned houses, and crypts without roofs, the great majority of what had been set in the third and fourth centuries dissolved quietly in the dark. The losses are the reason the surviving relic gems of the North are as precious as they are, and the reason the Lucentians keep the watch they keep.

From the Settlement of the Varsa onward the practice recovered with the parishes, and for fourteen centuries the fragile gem was simply what a gem was. The characteristic disaster of that long age is the flooded crypt or the burnt house, which is not property damage but a rite miscarried, the remainder given to Water a second time and never to Stone or Air at all. Families it happened to were still explaining themselves two generations later.

### Vitrification

The vitrified gem belongs to the closing decades of the Aetheric Age and the opening of the Imperial, arriving with the Fulminant Turn of the seventeenth century. The aetheric furnace that had been winning alumen from common earths for a hundred years, and was then measured and found to have been a Fulminant engine throughout, fuses ash to true glass without difficulty. Within a generation there were funerary works with certified masters and house marks, and Balboa was licensing the process abroad on the same terms as every other thing Balboa knows.

It arrived into a faith already in difficulty and an industry already impatient, and its consequences were almost wholly perverse. The tending stopped. A rota kept in every crypt in the West for fourteen hundred years was obsolete within two generations, and with it went the daily domestic relation between a household and its dead, which had been the whole of most people's devotional contact with their ancestors and which nothing replaced. The milling broke, since a fused gem will not go through a parish quern unbroken. And the polemic sharpened, because a fused gem is a form fixed against the world's own resolution, made in a furnace, by men who advertise an improvement on the Flame-Keeper's rite. Antidiscordian preaching did not have to reach far for the comparison to Reme and did not bother reaching.

The reform current of the following century abandoned vitrification and returned deliberately to the fragile setting as an austerity, so that a salt-crystal in a renewed case is now the mark of a devout old house and of a particular reforming conscience. The plainer Commonwealth dioceses took it up with enthusiasm. Virell finds the whole thing affected.

### Term

A gem is milled when no one is left who was looked at by those eyes. The rule is self-enforcing, it varies naturally between families, it gives a long-lived aunt real power over an estate's obligations, and it makes the offence nameable. Keeping a gem past its memory is a disgrace that attaches precisely, and the households that do it are known.

## The Crypt

A gem occupies a cut niche and cannot be stacked or hidden, so crypt space is property. Niches are leased for a term, inherited, valued, taxed in some jurisdictions, and disputed in all of them, and the Commonwealth registers them as it registers everything. The characteristic Concordian suit of the modern age is over a lapsed crypt lease. The characteristic disgrace is the selling of an ancestor's niche and the early milling that clears it, done quietly, discovered eventually, and never forgotten.

Hoarding is countable. Anyone admitted to a crypt can walk the wall and count, and the number is a public fact about a family. Twelve unmilled is a statement. Great houses admit visitors precisely because the count displays antiquity and the means to defer, and preachers make the count from the pulpit when they dare.

Beneath the niches lies the common chamber, a room of mingled ash open to view, into which the ash of lapsed terms and of the parish poor is decanted. It is the most explicit physical statement Lurism makes about what it believes, and western families take their children to see it.

## The Breaking

A fused gem is hard and brittle, and nobody grinds glass whole. It is broken first and the shards go to the quern, so the milling of a vitrified ancestor is two acts where the fragile gem required one, and only the first of them is a ceremony. The practice is younger than the Turn and is still, in the older parishes, spoken of as a novelty.

The breaking is a household occasion held on a set day, with word sent beforehand, attended by kin and by whoever in the parish has standing to come, and followed by a meal. Mourning is briefly resumed in a muted and retrospective key for a person nobody present has met. The gem is set on the block, the parish maul is taken up, and one blow is expected to answer. Three are permitted. After three the gem goes to a city works and is dealt with by machinery, at cost and out of sight, and the family bears the expense and the talk both. The clergy attribute the failed blow to flaws in the melt. The parish attributes it to unwillingness, and a family that went to three and then to the works is understood to have been refused.

The North requires the nearest surviving kin to strike, once, without hesitation, on the reasoning that the hands which kept the gem must be the hands that end it. Lavion has a warden strike while the family stands by, having touched the maul first for form. The Commonwealth has a sworn officer strike before witnesses and enters it in the register, on consent signed beforehand.

The parish maul is used for nothing else, is kept at the mill, and carries a list of what it has broken.

Two things follow. The breaking is a rite of the propertied, since the destitute never have one and the modest have one perhaps twice in a lifetime, so the only public ceremony in the western sequence that requires means is the ceremony of giving up. And timing speaks: a gem broken at the first decent moment reads as piety, or poverty, or a quarrel with the dead, and the parish decides which without asking.

The grinding is a warden's afternoon. The shards go through the quern, the flour is bagged, and the platform takes it on the next good wind, with four or five people at the rail.

## Tokens

A gem cannot be worn. A worn token is therefore either a second small pour taken from the same melt at the making, or a fragment reserved at the breaking, and the schools divide sharply over which is the graver abuse.

The Antidiscordians hold the second pour to be worse, since the fragment is a lapse committed under grief at the moment of release while the pour is decided in advance, priced, ordered from a works, and multiplies one deferral into five that must each be answered separately. The Redemptionists hold precisely the opposite: the pour is distributed among people who loved the deceased and is at worst sentimental, while the fragment is a refusal enacted at the threshold by someone who has just watched the gem break, and is diagnostic of a soul that has not re-attuned. Retuners set terms for such tokens and take them back at term.

Harmonism regulates the pour and rejects the fragment on formal grounds, a shard being uncut and unmountable within the Aureomedian canon of acceptable cuts and settings, which admits approved geometries, plain metal, no figure, no likeness, and no name cut into the gem. Concordian practice licenses the token trade, records tokens against the gem they came from, requires surrender at the breaking as a condition of the licence, and treats an unregistered token as contraband in its ports. A licensed token and an unlicensed relic fraction are the same object to look at, which is why the registry exists in the form it does and why the Commonwealth's customs officers are among the few laymen in the West competent to date a setting by eye. The Ultimoversian formula, which most of the West in fact follows, permits one pour and no fragment, and the Ultimoversians are aware that this is a ruling for the solvent.

The Mendicants carry none and preach against them without asking anyone to legislate. The Custopontians carry other people's under oath, conveying a token to a distant crypt for sailors' families, for the diaspora, and for anyone whose dead ended in the wrong city.

The Southern Reach passes tokens in the female line as it passes everything else. The Eastern Gyre, keenest of all the churches on the saints, has never kept token and relic properly apart and has stopped trying. The Merhal permits a token to be given to the river, a fourth disposal no other church recognises, defended on the grounds that the river takes everything in the end.

## The Trades

The pollution sits in the interval and in the rendering. Watching is honourable and is kept by kin. Everything from the washing of the body to the gathering of what the fire leaves is defiling, and everything after the ash is not. The division is visible at the pyre-field gate.

The fire-trade washes, bears, tends the pyre through to completion, breaks and gathers, sells fuel, and sells the flame itself, since in most western cities the pyre must be lit from the parish brazier and the carriage of that fire is the trade's right. A family in mourning buys fire from people it will not seat, at a ledge, in coin, without contact. The trade is wealthy in every city large enough to have one. It holds fuel contracts, knows every family's circumstances from what that family could afford to burn, and can tell at sight what a body died of, which makes its masters witnesses in homicide and inheritance and makes them feared. They are hereditary and endogamous, a handful of surnames along any coast, resident by a fixed gate and clustered with the tanners and the knacker, and their children are barred from apprenticeship elsewhere. They endow chapels that nobody of standing attends.

The mill-wardens grind, keep the querns, keep the maul, and carry the flour up to the platform. The ash-keepers and crypt-keepers are respectable, frequently in minor orders and often the same men as the sacristans, cutting niches, keeping the register of terms, conducting the decanting, and taking the fees. The contrast stands thirty paces apart at most pyre-fields and everyone can see it.

The exclusions are the ordinary ones: payment at a ledge, separate bench and separate cup, a separate door where the parish church admits them at all, residence outside the wall, and in some jurisdictions exclusion from guild and from oath. Orthodoxy holds the disability to attach to the blood. The Concordian position, argued in synod and never fully enforced, is that it attaches to the office and lapses when the office is left, and it is the most-cited piece of Concordian social teaching and the most widely ignored. Once a year in most western cities the fire-trade is seated and served first by the prince-bishop or the mayor in person, an occasion everyone finds moving and which changes nothing the following week.

In the uplands of the Varsa and across the poorer Commonwealth parishes there survives a figure paid to eat a meal passed across a body before the burning, understood to take up what the dead left unresolved. The practice is doctrinally impossible, Lurism having no transfer and no atonement, and it has been condemned by council four times in eight hundred years, which is the measure of how well the condemnations worked. Such a practitioner is hired in secret, fed outside the door, paid at a ledge, and shunned by the same household the next morning. Parish priests schedule their visits for the following day.

Along the Merhal and the Drowned Coast the pyre-tending and the gathering are frequently done by diaspora Alvai descended from the desert-edge death-guides, under contract to parishes whose pulpits describe their ancestors as shaped abominations. The arrangement is neither hidden nor discussed.

## The North

The Patriarchate that preaches hardest against deferral permits almost none of it to households. Dominion practice is short-term and severe, and the Dozenians mill their own at the first permissible moment and say so. What the North holds, in greater quantity and greater antiquity than anywhere in the world, are the gems of the Judges, the early Hierophants, the saints, and the founding generations of the Church.

The exemption rests on sainthood. A saint is one whose triad achieved harmony so completely that the soul chose to remain in the world to guide others instead of rejoining the Light. A remainder whose person did not go on is not a share awaiting completion, and holding it is custody and not deferral. The argument is clean and canonical and swallows the rule whole, and a Church whose one permitted permanence runs through sainthood canonises accordingly.

The relic gems are the oldest of their kind because the technique was made for them. They were poured in the third century from ash that had by then been in urn custody for as much as six hundred years, and the labels on those urns had been renewed three times before anyone thought them load-bearing. What follows is the ordinary machinery of a relic tradition: provenance registers, contested attributions, gems whose claims date from the scattering after the Sack and cannot be tested, and the rival assertions of An-Mirda, Aramoor, and half a dozen Dominion houses to the same Judge. The gems poured from common chambers, holding a monastery's whole generation mingled and venerated under the one name the chamber bore, remain an unresolved category.

The Lucentians guard the largest concentration of arrested remainder ever assembled, which by the North's own natural philosophy is exactly the kind of sustained disturbance that things settle into. Their vigilance is a correct reading of their own physics. In the galleries that hold fragile gems their perpetual watch is, stripped of its language, a drying and turning rota kept in rotation across fifteen centuries over the remains of the founders, by men reciting coded watches out of the Book of Judges. They know better than anyone what the Dark Ages cost, because the empty niches are in front of them.

When vitrification arrived, the North held the oldest objects in the faith in the form that fails, and the new technique could make them permanent only by melting them down and pouring them again. To re-fuse a Judge is to unmake and remake a saint's remainder in a furnace by a method invented fourteen centuries after the gem was first set. To decline is to accept that the founders of the faith will one day be lost to damp through the negligence of some watch a hundred years hence. Both positions are held by serious men, and the quarrel has occupied the North since the Turn. It is not only a devotional quarrel. A re-fused relic can never be divided again, its recorded divisions become unverifiable, and the rota that produced its contact relics comes to an end, so the houses whose income depends on all three have taken the devout position with a fervour that their critics find suspiciously convenient.

## Fractions and the Relic Trade

A fragile gem breaks. It breaks when dropped, when a case fails, when a gallery floods and a face sloughs away, when a besieged house is carried out in haste, and it breaks when somebody means it to. Division was therefore possible from the first century of the practice, and a Church that had just converted its founders into objects small enough to carry discovered within a generation that it could also carry them in pieces.

Division is not in itself an abuse. A founding house endowing a daughter foundation gives a fraction. A patriarchate confirming a new see sends a fraction. A crusading order receives a fraction under the same charters that standardised the custody of relics in the North, carries it on campaign, loses it at a river crossing, and ransoms it back at ruinous cost. Fractions are weighable, so they are recorded by weight, and the register of a division is a real document kept by a real chapter. This is the difference between the Circulean relic and the relics of a faith that never learned to write things down: every fraction in the Communion has a documented descent, and a great many of those documents are false.

The arithmetic is the vulnerability. A gem's size is a fact about the body that made it, the making was witnessed, and the original weight is frequently on record. It follows that the claimed fractions of a saint can be totalled and compared against what that saint can possibly have left. In the fifteenth century a Harmonist of the Grand Academy of the Broken Isles did exactly this for eleven of the Judges and published the sums, and the sums were not close. For three of the eleven the reputed fractions in circulation exceeded the original setting by better than fourfold.

The Church did not repudiate anything. It graded. Fractions with continuous documented descent from a witnessed division became witnessed fractions; everything else became reputed, and reputed fractions remained licit objects of devotion on the reasoning that the devotion of the faithful is not contingent on the provenance of the object. The grading satisfied nobody and settled the matter completely, which is the usual result.

The mechanism of multiplication is extension, and it is the reason the arithmetic never comes out. A gem damaged by damp leaves a residue that can be redissolved and set again, and a house whose relic has begun to fail is not merely permitted but expected to restore it. Nothing in the process prevents the liquor of one genuine fraction from being extended with common ash and poured into four. In the fragile age this was undetectable. After the Turn the assay trades acquired the means to test a setting for density and inclusion, which is why the houses holding the most valuable reputed fractions are also the houses least willing to submit them.

A parallel traffic runs in the consumables of the tending rota. A cloth used to turn a Judge's gem for two centuries, the drying salts drawn from a gallery, the lining of a case, the sweepings of a reliquary shelf: all of these have passed through contact with the remainder, all are cheap to produce, and none can be tested at all. The great northern houses sell them openly and regard the trade as unobjectionable, and the same Antidiscordian preachers who denounce the token trade in the cities have never had a word to say about it.

The unresolved case is An-Mirda and Aramoor. The exarchs who fled the Sack in 336 AR carried what they could, and what they could not carry whole they carried broken. For at least nine of the Judges and early Hierophants, both cities hold a fraction, and each calls its own the gem. The formula in dispute is the greater part: the claim that a house holds the majority of a saint's remainder, which confers precedence, pilgrimage, and revenue. The claim is weighable. Neither party has ever permitted a weighing. Proposals to bring both fractions to a neutral city and settle the matter on a balance have been floated in every century since the sixth, have twice been agreed in principle, and have never once survived the question of who holds the scales. The Anti-Hierophants of the sixth century argued their legitimacy in gems, the Interregnum made the traffic worse, and modern cooler heads on both sides now say privately that a weighing would probably show both fractions to be minor and the greater part of most of those saints to have been lost in the burning of the holy city, which is the one outcome neither patriarchate can afford to publish.

The theology permits all of it. A saint is a mirror in a lighthouse and not a presence in an object; nothing of the person inheres in the remainder, and the gem has no power and works no effect. Division is therefore meaningless, and being meaningless it is harmless, and being harmless it is unrestricted. The doctrine that denies relics any virtue whatsoever is the doctrine that removes every obstacle to their multiplication, and the Kharunites have been pointing this out since the practice began.

## The Heathen Ground

Burial is what was done before the faith, by people who did not know better, and the evidence stands in the fields. Dolmens cross the Stranic peninsula, barrows run through the Varsenne basin and along the west coast of Ertra, and every parish has one on its skyline. Children learn what the mounds are before they learn why.

Ploughing goes around and never through. The mounds serve as boundary marks and assembly points, which makes them places where oaths are sworn and where courts of the old kind sat, a habit the Church has never wholly stopped and has in places absorbed by holding the parish assize on the mound and calling it custom. Gallows go up on barrows, the ground being spoiled already. Nobody opens one. The prohibition on opening is far stronger than the prohibition on burial itself and is held with equal conviction by the pious, who believe the ground is unresolved and dangerous, and by the indifferent, who believe it is unlucky and have no theory.

The antiquarians of the modern age open them under warrant, for urns and bronze, defended by the academies as natural philosophy and denounced from pulpits as the deliberate reopening of resolutions that took fifteen centuries to settle. The Lucentians stand with the pulpits and are, in this one matter, the reasonable party.

## Exposure and the Alvai

At the desert edge of the Ptolean borderlands, where the ground is too shallow to take anything, the Alvai descended from Grandfather Marrow's Kin gave their dead to the bonehooks and to the open sky, and reduced a body in a day. Moonkin do not practise it and follow the rites of their own home. The Theocracy suppressed exposure wherever it reached, and the western Church holds it to be the worst of all disposals, worse than burial, on the grounds that it abandons the body to appetite. The few honest natural philosophers who have considered it observe that it is the most rapid and most complete resolution available to anyone. They are shouted down.

The resemblance between a platform raised to the open air for the dispersal of the milled and a platform raised to the open air for the giving of a body has not escaped the Kharunites, the Alvai of the diaspora, or anyone else who has looked at both. The physical difference is a fire and a mill. Western congregations do not enjoy having the observation made to them.

## Where the Rite Fails

At sea a body is unresolved, unattended, and in a Noble's own domain. Every seafaring people of the Circle Sea has built the same compromises: the weighted shroud and the recited interval, the stern lamp kept burning until landfall, and above all the carriage home of anyone whose family can pay, which is a line in every Balboan and Anish ship's articles and which is why officers come back and common hands do not. Ships of any size carry fuel allowance for a pyre ashore at the next anchorage, and a port that refuses a burning has offered a recognised insult.

On campaign an army cannot carry its dead. Field practice is pyre, wash, quern, wind, completed within the week, and only officers return as gems. Soldiers' families know this at the outset.

Mass death is met with a larger fire, which is the West's quiet advantage, and battlefield grounds are known bad ground for generations afterward. The failure case is the one where there is no fuel: a besieged city, a winter epidemic, a ship. Then a western town must bury, and the pit is not merely an emergency measure but a forced reversion to heathenry performed by the people it happens to. Plague pits are remembered for centuries, they are fenced, and the shame attached to them is as strong as the fear. A town that buried within living memory does not mention it to its neighbours.

The Lucentians hold the sole standing warrant to open a failed ground, to clear it, close it, mark it, and leave it. Every old city has a few walled and disused plots that nobody will build on and that the diocese declines to discuss.

## The Eastern Rite

The Kharunites bury in tended ground under plantings, and hold that burning forces a resolution the body would reach unaided, which is compulsion, which is the sin for which Reme was unmade. That the Judge Kharun was himself executed by fire gives the charge an edge no western reply has blunted. Their shrine gardens are burial grounds that work, tended daily, and their luxuriance is held to be the evidence that they work. Funerals in the east are long and loud because the resolution is slow and the dead are not going anywhere.

The western answer is that the East has reverted, that the garden is a mound with better landscaping, and that the Kharunites let highland heathenry back in through the shrine gate. The charge is unfair and is made constantly, and it stings because the Eastern Rite's actual history is one of absorbing local practice, so it is close enough to true to be unanswerable.

The Eastern reply is to the whole apparatus at once, to the gem and the token and the register and the lawsuit and the crypt lease, and it has not changed its wording since the third century. No people in the world has ever devised so thorough a refusal to let its dead stop, and the West took up the refusal in the same decades in which it stopped being able to hear anything else.

The East has no fire-trade and therefore no excluded caste. The shrine-gardener tends the dead and is an ordinary respectable tradesman who sits where he likes. An eastern gardener travelling in the West is genuinely shocked by the ledge and the separate cup, says so, and is thought insufferable for it. It is the sharpest social difference between the rites, it follows necessarily from a disagreement about resolution, and it is the clearest demonstration in the Lurist world that a metaphysical quarrel produces a social order and not merely a liturgy.
