When the [[Eternal Dominion|crusaders]] landed on the shores of Ertra with the ashes of Reme still warm, the thought and craft of the [[Remean Empire|Ancients]] is expunged or prescribed, including [[Shaping]]; but some Remean thought survived in practices turned well away from the body. The root was always there -- the Remeans created many household artefacts that aided and guided everyday Shaping, from combs that governed hair growth and follicle structure to more esoteric means of influencing the body. Its remaining practitioners turned the previously somatic logic fully away from living bodies and into artefacts that act upon inert matter.

Thus predisposed to preferring small but safe effects, they were the first people to realise, long before the Grand Academy wrote it up nicely, that the [[The Six Elements#The Abeyant and Antagonistic Relations|abeyant and antagonistic relations]] between the elements are where the true leverage was. Of course the early schools had neither the language nor the academic rigour to speak of the likes of "Saturation" and always mean the same thing, but early on in their craft they began to speak of "crafting a hex" and mean not a thing of witchcraft, but a way to manipulate the sixfold elemental symmetry of the cosmos through the realisation that it answers to structured intent. Indeed some treatises -- for all their mystical flavouring -- read like the grubby, artisanal ancestor of the very clean six-vector mathematics that modern [[Scalarism|Scalarism]] arrived at.

A "hex" was soon understood as a means to force the intentionality inherent in the six elements to act on a generated stimulus rather than natural one. Each element wants to express itself; that sprites might form anywhere there is elemental upheaval or saturation is testament to this. Hexcrafters learned to lean into this desire without commanding it, like a rider guiding a galloping horse with but his calves and no reins. A hex gave the elemental autopoesis both feedstock and outlet, and the best hexcrafters did it without attracting a sprite.

Were any of the fallen progenitors of the practice around to witness it, this turn would have felt like insult added to injury: from the aspirant idealism of the perfected body to the pragmatic incrementalism of an imperfect world. To the equally beleaguered [[Invoking|Invokers]], it must have seemed shockingly irreverent, even coercive: where an invoker raised voice and passion to channel the Six with an effect commensurate to his charisma and gifts, a hexcrafter quietly baked a clay token that channelled the desires of the element to act in accordance with the desired pattern as if it was natural law. Instead of invoking Maregina to keep the well sweet, they hung a hex in the shaft that strengthened Water's natural desire to dilute and diffuse, so that slime never even found a foothold. Instead of beseeching Phlogistophor to spare the warehouse, they set hexes around the beams that safely bled away Fire into the brick by shoring up its hatred of its antipodes, Ice and Water. To a modern scholar all this hocus-pocus now looks like a crude, rule-of-thumb, pre-theoretical hack of the same field they now describe as aether, but to early [[The Light|Lurist]] polemicists, all this was still deeply suspect; even if it never touched the flesh, it smelled of the same old arrogance.

Thus constantly under threat of persecution during the Dark Ages, hexcrafting techniques adapted to survive under a series of euphemisms -- "rational fortification", "harmonic guidance", or more poetically, "the Old Geometry" -- until they matured to the point that guilds formed around them, because knowledge of the proper creation and workings of hexes was already half data, half superstition, and nobody was going to hand such tricks out for free. Over time the added responsibilities and civic presentation helped the craft to step out of the shadows and into respectability.

By the time aetheric engineering became the paradigm in the 13th century, hexcrafting had quietly rebranded as just another craftsmanly arts, one guild among many, the descendants of the detested Shapers now very dull, very earnest men in ink-stained robes who argued about ratios. In fact the modern paradigm owes much to hexcrafters, who had been doing nearly the same thing for a thousand years, by hand and rote, in the dark, while being persecuted.

In contrast with later aetheric engineering however, hexcraft always remained tied to intentional resonance. The making of a hex does not merely impress a pattern into clay or brass; it also prescribes the manner in which that pattern is to be actuated. Drawing on much earlier practices -- earlier even than Shaping -- hexcrafters learned to substitute the harmonies of human throats with that of simple instruments such as bells, drums, strings, or wind-chimes, all arranged and tuned to call up a specific harmonic vibrancy in the material. The hex itself was the shaped invitation, but until it was brought to its proper tone it remained dormant and inert. Even today each hex is still made with a particular sequence of beats or pitches in mind, and part of the craft is binding that acoustic or kinetic key so tightly into the artefact that the elements will not answer to any other.

Using a hex, therefore, always entails an act of attunement. For familiar work this is routine: the mason taps a certain cadence with his chisel that lets it weaken the bond in a stone face, the well-keeper rings a little chime for diffusion of the floating silt, the boatswain leads a call-and-response cadence that makes the hull bracings thrum. Where a hexcraft device is of unknown provenance or effect, however, bringing it to heel can be an art in itself. Whole guild manuals are devoted to "unlocking" forgotten patterns, to the slow work of probing a hex by hand and ear until the resonant figure that wakes it is found, or at least guessed at well enough that it stirs. The difference between a harmless flicker of activity and a catastrophic release of elemental tensions often lies in how sound that guess truly was.

Aetheric engineering, by contrast, is built upon the 13th-century insight that resonance need not be tied primarily to such acts of deliberate attunement. Once scholars realize that certain ratios, lattices, and repeating geometries can act to sustain harmonics within their own structure, the focus shifts. Instead of crafting an artefact that waits to be "serenaded" to act, the new engineers build chambers, throats, grids, and coils whose very form fosters self-sustaining patterns whenever even a small disturbance is introduced. The rhythm may still be actuated mechanically -- by rattles, pipes, chimes, cams, or ratchets -- but the bulk of the work is done by the lattice itself, which catches and recirculates the aetheric vibration without needing a human hand to keep time.

In philosophical terms, hexcraft treats resonance as something called down from outside and held only so long as intention is maintained. Aetheric engineering instead frames resonance as something that can be trapped and farmed within matter, given the right design, with human or mechanical rhythm serving as a mere ignition. This is the main fissure between the crafts. Where the hexcraft needs someone who stands in front of the work as a necessary interlocutor, beating or ringing the world into the right mood, the aetheric engineer increasingly steps aside to let the device sing to itself.

Despite all of this, hexcraft remains a viable and indeed quite indispensable craft, for a hex will still rouse even if the groove is a little worn or the chime slightly chipped, so long as the broad pattern of intent and proportion is supplied by the person attuning it. Aetheric lattices, by contrast, are entirely self-dependent, so they will sulk or misfire if an angle drifts or a ratio wanders, necessitating precision engineering and specialized tooling that drives up the price. For most purposes in which an ordinary tool will not suffice, therefore, a charm you must hum to but can afford is preferable to an aetheric device that works in polite, self-sustaining silence yet costs a year's wages in material and another in guild fees. Thus where the high end of society favors discreet, humming-lattice marvels, everyone else keeps a battered hex-token over the door and a work-song to wake it. As usual, civilisation survives on the things the elites consider beneath their notice.
