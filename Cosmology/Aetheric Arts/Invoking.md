Invoking is the oldest and least formal of the four recognized practices of elemental manipulation, and the one whose resonance is supplied most directly of all: by the human body's own rhythm -- voice chief among its forms, but equally clapped hand, stamped foot, whistle, or dance -- addressed outward to an elemental agent rather than applied to any dead material. A drum may lend the same address a further, more formal voice, favored where a working calls for more bodies than one to hold a single rhythm together, but it is never required; Invoking alone among the four practices asks nothing of its practitioner beyond what they already carry. Where [[Hexcrafting]] and [[Aetheric Engineering]] work upon inert structure, Invoking works upon a listener -- a mote's diffuse attentiveness, a sprite's settled temperament, a courtier's near-human cunning, or, at the furthest and most perilous reach, the singular and absolute being of a [[Noble Element]]. Clerical registers still favor the fuller and more formal Invocation, particularly in [[Antidiscordian]] censure and [[Concordianism|Concordian]] canon, but the common tongue everywhere prefers the plainer Invoking, and the two names describe exactly the same practice.

## Guiding the Element

Before any relation is touched at all, Invoking's plainest and most visible expression is simpler still: a patron who consents to be guided lends the invoker something like a hand of its own, moving water to a beckoning gesture, coaxing flame to leap or crouch or curl where no strength of the invoker's own body could throw it, calling stone to shift, or wind to gather and turn in answer to breath and word together. This is not the element being commanded so much as it is the element choosing, in that moment, to move as the invoker's own body might move, were that body made of water, or fire, or stone. A drafty sprite of an open window or a wind-worn crossroads obliging a windsinger's breath with a matching gust, and a Noble Element scouring a hillside bare, are the same act by nature, differing only in the vast gulf between what each is capable of granting -- the sprite's favor barely outdoing what the invoker's own hands could manage unaided, the Noble's answer erasing the very distinction between a favor and a catastrophe. It is this plain and immediate art, more than any subtler reach into Cohesion or Tension, that the word "invoking" calls to mind for most people around the Circle Sea: the windsinger's gust, the wavecaller's rising swell, the stonewalker's shifting floor, each one simply the element moving because it was asked to, and agreed.

## The Registers of Invoking

At its smallest and most enduring, Invoking is simply the folk substrate underlying everyday life around the Circle Sea: the habit of thanking the sprite of a hearth, a threshold, or a well, requiring nothing more than the right word said at the right moment, learned exactly the way any courtesy is learned and practiced by people who would never think to call it a discipline at all. Chief among these customs, though seldom named alongside the hearth and the well, is the old tradition kept by smiths at the forge, whose courtesies are carried less in words than in the hammer's own fall against the anvil, rhythm doing the work a prayer would do elsewhere. A working forge is an unusually concentrated, unusually disturbed knot of Stone and Fire held in close relation, and precisely the kind of place where the ambient field is most readily nudged toward coherence; smiths who have never studied a day of natural philosophy nonetheless keep their own small courtesies to the forge, sensing correctly, if not always knowingly, that their trade sits closer to a spontaneous sprite's cradle than most.

Between the folk substrate and the mythic extreme lies the surviving tradition of single-element patronage -- pyromancers, windsingers, wavecallers, woodwoses, frostspeakers, stonewalkers, and their many regional counterparts -- practitioners who address one element as suits their own character and describe the result not as mastery but as acquaintance, a stoneshaper who has "Piatros' ear," a windrider whose gale-bending is credited to Morwynt knowing her name. Out of this same tradition comes a smaller, transactional habit worth noting on its own terms: a stonewalker or pyromancer hired on for a single forging session, leaning into the old attraction between Stone and Fire to coax a better weld or a faster heat-take, paid in coin or kind exactly as a wavecaller might be paid to ease a fishing fleet's passage against a contrary current, or hasten it home with one running fair. It is humble work, unremarked upon by scripture, and asks nothing of the elements beyond what any smith's own forge already invites.

At the far end of the spectrum stands choir-scale Invoking, historically the province of the Rivian Sorcerer-Kings who ruled the Merhal centuries before Reme's rise, whose ambitions required not one voice but hundreds or thousands raised together in bloodcrafted slave choirs, drums lending the mass a single held rhythm no crowd of throats alone could keep, achieving feats no one since has matched. It is remembered as coercion and blood-rite rather than acquaintance, and the register has otherwise lain dormant at that scale for the whole of the historical record since, surfacing again only where a war's desperation has proven great enough to revive it. Where it has resurfaced, the shrine-wardens and Judges massing voice against voice have found themselves drawn into an uneasy doctrinal question their own scripture has not settled: whether a lawful address, however carefully distinguished from old coercion in method and spirit, begins at sufficient scale to resemble the very overreach that scripture warns against.

## Working Through the Relations

Because the six elements are bound to one another in fixed relation -- adjacent, abeyant, and antagonistic alike -- an invoker's reach through their patron is never confined to that element's raw character alone. A stonewalker on good terms with Piatros, or with any lesser expression of Stone willing to listen, may call upon Stone's own share in every relation Stone stands within: greater resistance to being set in motion, where Stone presses against Air's claim on Motility; a firmer grip against dissolution, where Stone presses against Water's claim on Cohesion; and, through Tension, the old and mutual pull between Stone and Fire.

No patron, however humbly addressed or however richly acquainted, will ever consent to its own diminishment. Invoking can only ask an element to be more fully itself within a relation it already holds; it cannot ask that element to yield ground instead. A stonewalker may hasten Stone's grip, never its crumbling; a pyromancer may deepen Fire's heat, never its guttering. This is why offense and defense in Invoking are not two different techniques, but two ways of standing on the same relation. Cohesion binds Stone and Water together in one shared tension: a stonewalker leaning on Stone's side of it hardens a wall against a river's slow work, while a wavecaller achieves the erosion the stonewalker was guarding against simply by leaning on Water's side of that same relation, asking Maregina for nothing more than that Water be fully itself. Neither invoker ever asks their own patron to betray its nature -- they only ever choose which side of the relation to stand on. It is precisely this limit that marks the compulsion remembered of the Sorcerer-Kings as a different order of transgression: an attempt to extract from an element something no ordinary acquaintance was ever built to give.

The strength of any such working, whatever relation it draws upon, scales with the tier of the agent addressed rather than with the relation's own character: a mote or a settled hearth-sprite manages the barest nudge, a courtier a real and noticeable effect, a Noble Element something closer to catastrophe. This holds uniformly across the hexad's three kinds of relation, though the relations themselves are not all of one texture. The antagonistic relations -- Temperature, Motility, Density -- tend to read as intensifications of a substance's basic character: more heat, more inertia, more density, exactly the terms in which the elements are already understood to differ from one another. The abeyant relations, gathered into the three Parallelisms of Charges, Bonds, and Changes, run deeper, being already understood as the very source of the Charges' pull and push, gravitation, and transformation in the physical world; invoking through them tends to manifest not as a mere intensification of character but as a genuine directional force.

Tension, the Charge held between Stone and Fire, is the clearest instance of this. As in the ordinary play of lodestone and spark the Charges Parallelism gives rise to, unlike poles draw together and like poles push apart: a stonewalker leaning into Stone's share of Tension draws Fire-aspected matter toward Stone-aspected matter, while causing other Stone-aspected matter nearby to push apart from itself, the two effects arising together from the same working rather than chosen separately. This attraction is also, at the mundane and wholly unmodulated level, the natural-philosophical reason metal and forge-fire suit each other as readily as they do, long before any invoker enters the smithy at all -- the same relation that gives the smith's old forge-courtesies their unusual fertility for sprites gives the ore its readiness to take the heat.

## Reception Among the Faiths

Orthodoxy views single-element patronage with active mistrust, its censorious writings branding such practitioners warlocks and witches for confusing the bannermen for the liege. The Eastern Rite's panentheist reading of the elements as lawful neighbors of the Light makes it, by contrast, the tradition likeliest to defend the practice outright; Kharunite shrine-wardens are drilled in the old grammar of invocation and are often able, when pressed, to conjure and bind sprites, or stronger elementals still, into orderly service. Concordianism occupies a narrower ground between the two: its rites of appeasement are permitted under clerical guidance, legalistically structured and woven into civic observance without Orthodoxy's characteristic unease, yet Kharunite arguments that invoking is simply another form of prayer are firmly rejected as a step beyond measure and into peril.

## Regional Variations

The Svenish peoples have cultivated their own elaboration on Invoking, one unremarked elsewhere around the Circle Sea: Bonding, in which an elemental agent's resonance comes to key itself not to a hearth, a well, or a forge, but to a single person, carried wherever that person goes rather than left behind at any threshold. The common Svenish term for a sprite so bonded is a moling; for a courtier, an adaling.

A moling is won the same way any local invoking relationship is won elsewhere -- sustained courtesy, offered and returned over enough time for the acquaintance to settle -- and is common enough that most Svenish acquire at least one moling by the time they are grown, a few uncommonly gathering several. Little is thought of this beyond what is thought of any life properly kept; a moling marks a person as attentive and well-mannered toward the small elemental presences around them, nothing more.

An adaling is a different matter entirely. Where a moling is cultivated, an adaling is earned, and never by temperament: a courtier judges and chooses for itself, drawn not by any affinity for the person's character but by a single deed it considers proof of something worth honoring. Because this judgment answers to the deed alone, it is no respecter of age, and a child capable of one sufficiently striking act may be marked as surely as anyone grown old in reputation. Such early bonding is read among the Svenish as a recognized omen rather than a certainty, and a child so marked is watched more closely by kin and community, and expected of more than their peers for the whole of their life thereafter. To gather more than one adaling, and rarer still to gather adalings of different elements, is a mark of a destined life almost without peer, since no single-element tradition elsewhere in the Circle Sea has any path to it at all -- those traditions being bound, by their own nature, to whichever one element suits a person's character, while an adaling answers only to the deed a courtier itself has judged.

Elementals do not fall for human folly, and an adaling's bond, once given, is not undone by ordinary failure, misfortune, or disappointment; the deed that earned it stands regardless of what comes after. It can be lost only when the person becomes, unmistakably, the very opposite of whatever that deed once proved -- and even then rarely, since a courtier's judgment was never lightly given in the first place. Svenish cautionary tales of such a loss take two shapes, though one is told far more often than the other. Most commonly, the adaling remains in name but withholds its favor, leaving the marked person to carry the honor of having once been chosen while the one thing that ever answered them has gone quiet. It is this shape the tale-tellers return to again and again, since a withheld favor, unlike a clean severing, can still be sought after -- and the Svenish keep no shortage of tall tales about those who went to great ends to win a lapsed adaling back: ordeals undertaken, old wrongs set right, years of quiet, unglamorous courtesy paid again from the beginning, exactly as a moling or any lesser bond might first be won. These are, by preference, tales of reclamation rather than loss: Svenish storytelling dwells far longer on the deed that finally won a lapsed favor back than on any account of one left withheld for good, since proving one's way back into lost honor through deed is precisely the kind of story this culture likes best to tell. Less often, and harder still to reverse, the adaling departs outright, a clean and public severing, word of which travels quickly and is spoken of for a generation.
