The art and act of Shaping was an advanced and now-lost somatic discipline originating in the [[Remean Empire]], characterized by its internal focus and precision. Unlike modern [[Hexcrafting|aetheric engineering]], which acts upon the external world through machines or structures, shaping worked solely within the human body. It used specialized focus objects to harmonize with the user's bodily form, enabling permanent or semi-permanent transformations. These changes were everything from the merely cosmetic to the medicinal and the functional: hair length and color, bone setting, lessened scarification, as well as enhancements of strength, endurance, coordination, sensory perception, and even cognitive timing were among the recorded effects.

Shaping adhered to the same foundational principles that underlie aetheric science (structure, resonance, and materiality) but applied them inward. Artifacts were not general-purpose tools but personal implements, requiring intense attunement and discipline to activate. Each artifact functioned as a conduit for a carefully balanced harmonic pattern that altered the bearer, effectively rewriting how the body performed its natural functions.

Because of this, shaping allowed the Remean Empire to thrive without mechanical labor or slavery. Shaped individuals, tailored for specific physical tasks, made up the working class and the legions alike. Laborers could carry immense loads unaided. Soldiers could march farther and faster than cavalry, coordinating maneuvers with uncanny precision. This Shaping of the self into a perfected instrument became central to Remean civic and military life.

However, the art did not survive the Fall. Following the destruction of Reme and the rise of the Theocracy of [[Eternal Dominion]], Shaping came under theological suspicion. Its methods were suppressed, and artifacts designed to affect the human form were banned by ecclesiastical decree. Today, it is considered either forbidden, heretical, or mythical. No verified practitioner remains, and most surviving artifacts are inert, dangerous, or misunderstood. Despite this, scattered references in Lurist sermons and crusader accounts provide tantalizing glimpses into what Shaping once achieved, and why it was feared.

The ideal Remean form, as preserved in statuary and bas-relief, was profoundly gynoid -- refined, symmetrical, poised, and unmistakably feminine-coded in silhouette and posture, even when depicting warriors, builders, or civic functionaries. This was not merely an aesthetic preference but a cultural principle: shaping was used not to masculinize strength or exaggerate sexual dimorphism, but to refine function into beauty and beauty into harmony. The result was a society where even soldiers moved with balletic precision, where laborers bore their strength in elegant proportion, and where orators and magistrates were shaped for clarity of breath and grace of motion. While pragmatic shaping still tailored bodies to their roles -- broad backs for weight-bearing, expanded lungs for messengers -- these adaptations were carefully harmonized with the prevailing aesthetic of the feminine ideal. Strength was never made brutish; utility never at the expense of form.

This preference for feminine-coded bodies and voices may reflect more than just the sensibilities of the Remean Era. It likely preserved the memory of an earlier, pre-Remean matriarchal order, when leadership, ritual authority, and descent followed maternal lines. Even after Shaping rendered biological sex largely irrelevant, matrilineal inheritance and social lineage persisted -- not as a necessity of reproduction, but as a structure of legitimacy and historical continuity. The veneration of calm, maternal forms in art, and the use of modulated, high-register voices in civic ceremony, hint at an ancient association of the feminine with stability, wisdom, and harmony. This cultural memory endured, encoded in the very shape of the self. To Shape toward the gynoid was to align with centuries of form, lineage, and law.

The early [[The Light|Lurist]] reaction to Remean form was one of violent rejection, both ideological and physical. As the Eternal Dominion advanced, its soldiers and preachers unleashed a wave of iconoclasm against Remean art and architecture, targeting especially those statues and mosaics that embodied the gynoid ideal or blurred gender distinctions. To Lurist eyes, the Remean ideal of the body -- elegant, feminine, and functionally perfected -- represented not harmony, but hubris, a denial of the divine order in which male and female were seen as balanced yet irrevocably separate in an ontological triad: men to govern and act, women to nurture and care, their children to grow and obey, each fixed in place as part of a divinely-ordained structure. The Remean ability to remake the body, to dissolve these categories into a continuum of Shaped roles, undermined this foundation. To Lurist thought, this ability constituted not merely heresy, but an affront to the very grammar of creation, a leveling of distinctions Lurism held to be sacred and insurmountable. The result was systematic erasure: chiseled faces, torsos defaced to hide their androgyny or femininity, until what remained were only fragments and silences.


## Civic Shaping in the High and Late Imperial Age  

Any account of Remean "civic Shaping" -- the baseline imprinting performed at select lifestages, as opposed to "vocational Shaping" done upon entering a career -- must begin with a disclaimer: no comprehensive Remean sources on the subject survive. What little is known of Remean Shaping comes from hostile Lurist field reports, later theological polemic, and a handful of ambiguous diagrams and sculptural fragments that escaped destruction. On that basis alone, all reconstructions are tentative.

Even so, a consistent pattern emerges from the surviving material. It points toward a high-imperial ethos in which Shaping ceased to be considered merely corrective or aesthetic and instead became a deliberate attempt to standardise the human body into a new norm deemed more in line with the civic virtues favored by the Remean.

The principal written witnesses are early crusader accounts from the Remean frontier, composed before the fall of the city and the full imposition of _damnatio memoriae_. These texts are fragmentary and heavily moralised. They speak, with obvious disgust, of enemies "neither man nor woman," of "bodies fit only for unclean acts," and of persons "who marry man and woman in one flesh." Later Lurist theologians took these early impressions and amplified them into emblematic horrors, without concern for anatomical detail.

At the same time, the nascent tradition of Circulean archaeology has recovered from peripheral Remean sites a small number of pre-crusade tablets whose fragmentary terminology -- much of the Remean language remains untranslated -- does not map cleanly onto modern assumptions about men and women. Phrases speak of "completing the channels" and "bringing the generative humours into accord" with no reference given to recorded birth sex. 

The tentative understanding of Remean gender structures is complicated by the fact that the Remean language, insofar as it has been reconstructed, appears to have distinguished grammatically between animate and inanimate, not between masculine and feminine. Pronouns and agreement markers divide the world into "that which breathes" and "that which does not," with a few honorary exceptions such as for household sprites and higher elementals. There is no evidence for lexical gender.

Crusader contemporaries of the late Remean Empire consistently report frustration at the "confusion" of Remean reference, complaining about missives that spoke of leaders of the faith with terms of address that conveyed "baby boy" or "unbalanced person" rather than "man", as if the language itself lacked a neutral term for "person of visible maleness". It is unclear whether this linguistic structure gave rise to the Remean project of physical monosexuality, was the consequence of erasure, or whether both flowed from an older metaphysical preference to treat sex dimorphism as secondary.

Either way, the Remean conceptual frame was glossed in hostile sources as "peeling away" or "stripping off" defects. One recurring Lurist charge is that the shapers claimed "to unveil the true human from beneath the false division of the flesh" -- and while clearly intended as condemnation, the phrasing likely derived from the shapers' own observation that biological sex, in any population, is far less binary than religious doctrine often prefers. It is entirely plausible that Remean shapers, with their close attention to the body, observed such variance early and drew from it not to treat such cases as monstrosities, but as hints. The "true" human, in their eyes, was clearly a more capacious template containing the potential for either procreative roles. Civic Shaping, especially in juveniles, then functioned not as a change from one sex to another, but as an "unveiling" of that underlying pattern: adding what birth had omitted, stabilising what had developed halfway, smoothing extremes back toward a single, balanced mean.

In this way, the juvenile Shaping rite became a central institution of Remean life. The purpose was not to choose or declare a gender, but to bring every body as closely as possible into balance, with a typing that -- modelled after a long matrilineal and matriarchal preference -- remained aesthetically gynoid. In either case, the result seemed to have generally been the equivalent of a fertile hermaphrodite: an individual capable of acting as gestator or inseminator as wished. The language of what few surviving fragments speak of the practice suggests that this process was understood not as an imposition but as a completion and a rite of passage into adulthood: What was merely a body belonging to the matriline that bore it became, through its first civic Shapings, a sanctioned member of society.

While non-Remean sources writing in the earliest stages of the empire bear no significant mentions to Remean children being any different than others, texts from the high or late imperial age -- particularly crusader accounts -- do mention "even children ... being born into confusion", referring no doubt to mixed or ambiguous reproductive anatomy. In the flaring of religious strife, this was treated as evidence of widespread corruption, but a simpler explanation might well be that the long-standing practice of such civic Shaping could have shifted the distribution of sex traits in offspring toward hermaphroditic configurations.

By the late imperial period, it is entirely plausible that significant numbers of Remean children were born already close to the "civic ideal", requiring only minor harmonising adjustments at their juvenile rite. For contemporaries inside that system, such births would have seemed a natural confirmation that their art unveiled a pre-existing truth rather than imposing an artificial state.

Within this monosexual baseline, "gender" as later cultures understand it would have had limited purchase. Several texts, however, preserve Remean idioms that distinguish between those "who give" and those "who take" in particular contexts. In erotic passages -- the poetry of Zuabis of Reme particularly -- this seems to merely refer to who assumes which role in a given act. In civic texts the same verbs appear more metaphorical: magistrates "give" ratio and law, stewards "take in" grain and render accounts, legions "give" force at the frontier, granaries "take" burdens from the citizenry.

This suggests that individual identity had only weak, and moreover fluid associations with modes of action rather than fixed categories rooted in anatomy. The same person could be coded as primarily "giving" in one domain and "taking" in another, and those valences might change over a lifetime. Since every body contained the basic machinery for both reproductive roles, there was no need to anchor such patterns in unchangeable physical assignment.

If this reconstruction is broadly correct, civic Shaping in late Reme represents the most radical known attempt on Arlos to systematically adapt human bodies not just to niche-specific needs, but into a deliberate reproductive realignment, a biological retuning on a scale unheard of since the times of the First-And-Only. Yet it is entirely in character for the Remeans and their larger obsession with ratio, order, and engineered harmony, to have undertaken such a frankly transhumanist project.

From within the Remean worldview, their undertaking was morally and socially coherent. On the scale of a civilisation, a monosexual norm is flexible in reproduction, resilient against demographic shocks, and easier to integrate into a single civic identity. It is also, on the individual level, arguably closer to the actual variability of human bodies than the rigid dualism that later theology insisted upon. Given the lack of first-hand accounts, we cannot say whether this practice did allow for individual variance in a meaningful way, or forced every single citizen into a singular inviolate pattern of bodily expression as Lurist sources seem to claim.

However, we can say that from the perspective of Lurist doctrine, the practice was beyond the pale. Emerging Lurist cosmology placed strong emphasis on divinely ordained categories. A state that not only blurred but attempted to erase one such category, and that furthermore claimed to "unveil" a truer human form in doing so, could not be anything but blasphemous.

It remains conceivable that crusader accounts exaggerated or misunderstood a more modest Remean practice; given the fragmentary and partisan nature of the evidence, no definitive statement is possible. Yet the convergence of linguistic evidence, hostile testimony, and suggestive anatomical material makes the "hermaphroditic baseline" hypothesis difficult to dismiss entirely.
