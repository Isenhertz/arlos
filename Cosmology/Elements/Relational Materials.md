Relational materials, short for "Relation-active materials", are essentially elemental magnets: naturally occurring materials biased towards a certain Relation, either as stabilizing or inductive agent. Like true magnets, they are usually polarized; in the Arlosian context the poles of a relational magnet correspond to one of the two elements of the relation. Some few relation-active materials are instead in natural balance and only provide an effect if induced to tip to either end of the scale.

In the Circulean context, talk of "magnets" can refer to any of the relational materials, but is usually shorthand for "Tension magnet" unless otherwise specified, which is usually only the case in a scholarly context as most relationals have alternative terms that less prone to misunderstandings.

## Known Relational Materials - Abeyant
### Tension
Tension magnets, also called "true" magnets in the Academy's stricter speech, are among the oldest known relation-active materials. Long before any scholar could describe Tension as the weak abeyant relation of Fire and Stone, men had already found certain stones that drew pins, clung to blades, and made filings gather in orderly little beards. In village speech such stones are still called lodestone or drawstone.

For most of history true magnets were curiosities, shrine objects, or aids to specialized craft. Their practical importance rose sharply only in the Age of Corsairs, when mariners of the Circle Sea and beyond found themselves sailing farther from familiar coasts and under greater pressure to keep a reckon in haze, darkness, and open water. A floating needle taught by a sound lodestone could be trusted when stars were hidden and landmarks false. From that point onward the drawstone ceased to be a cabinet oddity and became a navigator's servant.

The naming of the poles follows the elemental relation rather than the mariner's cardinal convenience. Thus a magnet does not have a "north" and "south" pole in the scholarly style, but a Fire pole and a Stone pole. One end is understood as the more active, expressive, and seeking face of the relation; the other as the more retaining and anchoring. Like poles refuse one another, unlike poles draw, and a free magnet will turn until its inward Tension is satisfied against the greater ordering of the world. In common practice, the Fire end is usually the one marked in red lacquer, soot-wax, or cinnabar paint, though provincial habits vary and have caused more arguments in dockside taverns than any philosopher would admit in print.

Tension magnets also sit at the troublesome border of another field, namely Fulminance. It has long been observed that a vigorous lodestone may, under motion, striking, or certain patterned couplings, encourage a fulminant charge in nearby prepared matter. The converse seems true as well: strong surges may disturb, weaken, or occasionally reverse a magnet's settled bias. This affinity between magnetic Tension and Fulminance is what later natural philosophers term the Parallelism of Surges. Yet the phrase conceals as much as it explains. What is certain is only that the two relations answer one another often enough to be exploited, and capriciously enough to remain dangerous.

This uncertainty has done little to discourage experimentation. In workshops, drawstones are used to teach needles, bias grapnels, order filings in assay work, and in the more ambitious yards to build magnetic couplings and release catches. In laboratories they are paired with fulminant apparatus in an endless procession of coils, rings, plaques, and regrettable accidents, but the exact law joining the two remains obstinately incomplete. The Parallelism of Surges is therefore one of those fields that make reputations quickly and settle them poorly, and every generation produces a few savants convinced they have solved it.
### Attraction
Manipulating Attraction was a long-standing dream, which became a reality when 15th-century material advancements led to the recreation of naturally occuring neutral-buoyancy lattice materials such as found in the bones of many of the larger [[Gryphoids]]. Two forms exist, "soft" skyfoam or "hard" tractite. The former is a [[Alchemica|pliant]]-based aerogel that provides just enough above-neutral buoyancy to float naturally and is especially useful as filler material for aeronaval applications, such as gas-cell envelopes, glider padding, and other areas where weight is critical but structure is needed. The latter is a lattice material that is ordinarily of normal weight but can be "tipped" towards buoyancy or weight by selectively applied resonance and/or fulminant charge, and holds this new state as long as subjected to the original induction.

The old cautionary tale of the [[Sorceror-King|First-and-Only]]'s "Ladder", that impossible sky-reaching engine by which he [[How the Wind was Caged|sought to cage the Wind]] herself, had long been read as mere moral allegory. Yet with the advent of tractite, many scholars came to suspect that the Ladder's fabled ascent, and still more its reported ability to remain aloft as a fixed affront to the heavens, strongly suggest that it must have incorporated large quantities of an early Attraction-active structural material -- some ancient precursor to hard tractite, now lost to history along with so much else of Rivian art. If so, the modern rediscovery of tractite is one more shard of a broken and dangerous inheritance.
### Fulminance
Stormglass, called morwynite by modern natural philosophers in honor of [[Noble Element|Morwynt]] the Flash-Strider, is a rare fulminance-active vitreous material found naturally only in the Sifted Fields of [[Lu-Pto]]. Though at first glance it resembles certain pale fulgurites or sea-worn glasses, its occurrence is singular and has never been credibly reproduced in nature elsewhere, as the Sifted Fields are an aetherically charged area whose conditions the wider world has shown no inclination to imitate.

In its raw state, stormglass is a smoke-pale to faintly blue-grey glassy mineral with a waxed sheen and a dry, almost hungry feel in the hand. When properly cut, polished, and above all kept from easy grounding, it attracts fulminant discharge readily and holds the crackle for an implausibly long while before natural bleed carries it away. It does not create fulminance, but resists its immediate return to neutral abeyance better than any other conductive material. A rod tipped with stormglass, a crown set with it, or a ring bearing a facet of it, may all be charged by storm, friction, induction, or other means, then made to spend that stored ill temper in light, pain, sparks, signal, or spectacle. Upon so modest a principle many ancient tyrannies were built.

Stormglass is widely believed to have formed the material basis of the lost fulminant arts of the sorcerer-kings, whose courts could command dry chambers, trained keepers, and the patience of many terrified servants to make lavish use of stormglass in rods, plaques, lattice-cages, and ritual fittings, though little survives intact and still less survives with trustworthy commentary attached.

The great refinement of antiquity was its alloying with copper, producing a bronze that remained conductive, workable, and durable, yet inherited some measure of the parent material's appetite for fulminance. This substance was lost, but sources called it "royal bronze", as its lawful possession was reserved to the sorcerer-kings and their plenipotentiaries. A scepter of royal bronze could be charged and discharged repeatedly without the miserable fragility of raw stormglass. Plates, rods, coils, and fittings cast in it were the true backbone of the old fulminant apparatus.

In later centuries, the material returned from legend in the hands of alchemists, engineers, and, much less ceremoniously, indiscriminate graverobbers seeking ancient riches. "Royal bronze", however, never caught on as a name again, and modern workshops prefer "Rivian bronze", where the earliest surviving examples were first studied in earnest, while practical men often shorten this further to rivite. Thus what had once been a jealously guarded symbol of sovereignty became, in time, a subject for assay, standard weights, procurement disputes, and all the other minor indignities by which civilization proves its seriousness.
### Cohesion
The material known as seamtooth are the preserved radular denticles of the shearing snail, a Rim-of-Fire marine grazer whose feeding organ locally unseams Cohesion in hard substrates. In life this lets the snail rasp shell, coral, and volcanic stone. In craft, when mounted and resonantly excited, it yields cutting and scoring tools of unnerving efficacy. It was weaponized early in the Rim of Fire, but never at scale, because the material is rare, fragile, consumptive, brittle when dried out, and thus best used in specialist tasks rather than general slaughter.

Its true military value lay thus not in the melee, but in sabotage. Rimfolk free-divers, already unmatched in warm shoal water and reef channels, used seamtooth scorers below the waterline, where a ship's own weight and the sea's heave would finish the work for them. Circulean accounts from the Age of Corsairs dwell with particular emphasis (whether in outrage or schadenfreude) on the "defeat" of the Commonwealth at Red Ladder Bay, where a would-be fortified anchorage woke one dawn to find its vessels settling by the stern in calm water, their bilges opened in neat, impossible runs as though unstitched.

In the years since, seamtooth has made its way into the toolsets of specialized workshops around the Circulean, a highly sought-after and correspondingly priced import from the Rim where the shearing snail is found. Criminals, too, love the ease of it, though its price keeps it well out of reach of common cutpurses and second-story men.
### Reactivity
Wake-clay is a class of Reactivity-active clays whose natural state leans toward Ice and whose awakened state abruptly plunges into Soil. In repose it is inhibiting, preservative, and slightly deadening. Mixed into a bed, a plaster, or a mortar, it suppresses drift, slows spoilage, checks premature change, and keeps neighboring materials separate. Once the right threshold is crossed -- moisture, warmth, pressure, or the proper tone -- that it instead becomes catalytic, urging other materials to combine, set, sprout, cure, or transform. 

Potters and ditchers learned to know it by temperament rather than theory. A lump of it keeps seed and root from waking too soon. A jar sealed with it keeps better than one daubed with common clay. A paste of it laid cool on swollen flesh quiets a festering wound. What makes wake-clay useful is that the threshold can be chosen in manufacture. One lot may answer chiefly to moisture, another to warmth, another to pressure, another to tone. A farmer wants a pellet that sleeps in dry ground and wakes only after a proper rain. A mason wants a mortar that remains workable in the hod but seizes once compressed into a joint. An apothecary wants a plaster that stays shelf-stable and pliable yet turns active against the heat and damp of the body. An alchemist, being seldom content with one hazard at a time, may want beds that remain safe until a precise knock, bell-note, or change in vessel pressure wakes the mixture into activity.

In agriculture, a seed bedded in wake-clay can be held in deliberate suspension until the world gives the right sign. Dry storage is safer, transport easier, and sowing more regular. Farmers value it especially for uncertain climates, long transport, and delicate crops that would otherwise rot or jump too early.

In building, wake-clay provides self-setting mortars and grouts that are far more elegant than simple fast-curing mixes. While cool and undisturbed they remain surprisingly docile, allowing transport, daubing, and adjustment. Once packed into a seam and subjected to compression, or once wetted and then warmed, they become distinctly more eager to bind. Old hexcrafters' versions exist as well, requiring a mason's tapping cadence to set.

In alchemy, it serves as the matrix of reaction beds, filters, and catalyst pads, keeping reagents from racing one another, suppresses unwanted side activity, and gives a mixture shelf discipline. Then, once the proper condition is met, it ceases to inhibit and instead fosters exchange across its surfaces. That makes it invaluable in staged work -- fermentation beds, dye setting, resin curing, extraction filters, and multi-part compounds that must not fully "meet" until the operator is ready.

Medicine plasters are perhaps the most culturally visible use. A proper plaster of the stuff is cool, stilling, and preservative in the jar. Laid upon the body it first suppresses excess heat, swelling, seepage, and corruption. Then, as it takes in warmth, sweat, and the gentle pressures of a person's motion over the following days, it begins to encourage drawing, knitting, softening, or ripening, depending on what herbs, salts, and oils were compounded into it. Thus one plaster may be made to hold a wound quiet for travel, another to draw out corruption once safely home, another to stiffen into a support around a joint, and another to release a medicine only as the body's own heat calls for it.

### Temperature
Winterstone is a rare, naturally occurring mineral prized for its absolute indifference to heat and cold. Soft, porous, and easily worked when first quarried, it resembles a fine, pale variety of limestone with a matte bluish-white sheen. Unlike any ordinary substance, winterstone neither meaningfully warms nor cools, but maintains a fixed chill just above freezing, no matter the season, the furnace, or the snowfield, because the antagonism of Fire and Ice stands within it in perfect arrest. Temperature simply cannot advance within it, and so the stone abides at the narrow point where water remains just about liquid and frost forever threatens without triumphing.

Winterstone was known in antiquity, and its earliest uses were simple and practical. Quarried in slabs, it served as a preserving stone in storehouses, cellars, kitchens, apothecaries, and mortuary halls. Its chief inconveniences are that it sweats in warm air, gathers mist and rime, and can turn a poorly built chamber into a cold, clammy misery.

Ground to a fine powder and added to molten glass, winterstone yields winterglass, a pale, chill-bodied glass or glaze that inherits the mineral's indifference in thinner and more regular form. Winterglass does not warp in heat or craze in frost as ordinary glass does. It is therefore valued for vessel linings, alchemical ware, instrument faces, lenses, panes for cold storage, and other delicate applications where thermal steadiness matters more than comfort. It remains expensive and somewhat temperamental in the furnace.

In later centuries, powdered winterstone was worked into waxes, resins, sealants, and other compounded pastes, giving rise to hoarwax and its many cousins. Such preparations allowed the material's chill to be packed into chest linings, wall cavities, medicine jars, pipe wrappings, and transport boxes. Apothecaries, perfumers, and even confectioners all became fond of them, though not always for the same reasons. Good hoarwax keeps a chambered box cool and steady. Bad hoarwax cracks, weeps damp, and will leave a steward cursing over spoiled stock and slick floorboards.

The final and most recent refinement belongs to the Imperial age, when aetheric metallurgy learned to impress winterstone into metal as an aetherically ordered alloy. These alloys -- the rare pale-iron, the more prevalent rimebrass -- do not expand, soften, or seize with changes in weather as ordinary metal does. Their value lies not in keeping things cool, but in keeping them true. Precision frames, measuring rods, escapements, valve seats, optical housings, and delicate engine parts all benefit from such stubborn invariance. As iron takes the most readily to being alloyed in this manner, this makes an already dear material exorbitantly expensive.

### Growth
Ash of cinderbroom is a prized agricultural amendment, made by burning the dried stalks and roots of the cinderbroom shrub, a hard, fire-loving plant native to the volcanic soils of eastern Ertra. Farmers long observed that ground where it had burned would afterwards bear with uncommon vigor. The Remeans first systematized its harvest, firing, grading, and trade.

In its finished form, ash of cinderbroom is a pale grey to off-white powder, sometimes faintly glassy in the finer grades. Mixed sparingly into soil, it stirs the ground to a renewed vigor that can seem almost immodest. Used too heavily, however, it urges excessive foliage growth that strangles fruit, and can ruin the soil as it exerts itself in a single season. Like many gifts of Fire, it rewards restraint and punishes enthusiasm.

Though plainly Remean in pedigree, ash of cinderbroom survived the Scourge and the Crusades with little difficulty, for conquerors are seldom so pious as to prefer poor harvests. It remained in use across former Remean lands under a variety of pious renamings, and even in later centuries was traded widely enough that few estate stewards cared to censure it on account of its ancient provenance. Clerics might frown at the memory of the Remeans, but even in church orchards and wineyards, ash of cinderbroom continues to make plants thrive.
