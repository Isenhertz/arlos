> # Lurism
> ![[crest Lurism.png|Crest of Lurism]]
> Blazon: _Argent a sunburst or_

The Light of Lur, or Lurism, is the dominant religion in the lands surrounding the [[Circle Sea]], having largely displaced the heathen polytheistic belief in the [[Noble Element|Noble Elements]]. It is upheld by the [[Church of the Light]]; its adherents call themselves "the Communion of the Enlightened".

## Creed and Scripture

The Revelation to the Prophet Lur-Illuhan in -355 AR was not a deity's self-aggrandizing demand for worship, but a moment of unimaginable inner peace and harmonious self-awareness. Lurism, the religion born from this moment, is therefore not a community of cowed supplicants performing sacrifices to a being of great power, but a communion seeking to follow the example of the Prophet and instantiate this harmony -- in the world, in society, and most crucially in themselves. Indeed, the name in the phrase spoken by every follower of the creed -- "the Light of Lur" -- is not that of the Light, but of the Prophet who brought its knowledge into the world.

The central creed of Lurism is that "One is Three is Six is All". This creed -- represented in the chiffre •−=⨀ in its most common form, or ιͻυφ in modern handwriting -- acts as an affirmation of order in the world. In his moment of clarity the Prophet understood that "Lur" is not an entity, but the name by which the One Light, the source and measure of purity and harmony, may be known and understood.

Impersonal, ineffable, and indivisible, Lur has no form and no place, as it is in all forms and all places, singular and yet all-encompassing. Key to achieving unity with Lur is the triune harmony of Body, Mind, and Soul in a moment of enlightened balance such as the Prophet first mastered -- this is what the creed means when it is said that "One is Three".

The Heavenly Triad in turn governs how an individual relates to the world: how it is experienced, understood, and connected with. The world itself is the Sixfold Harmony of the elements, whose presence is self-evident in everyday life. This is why the creed says that "Three is Six".

Finally, then, the creed says "Six is All": What we can perceive and relate to is the basic shape of the cosmos. There is truth here, not artifice or deception -- the cosmos is as it is. And by chaining monad to triad to hexad to cosmos, the creed asserts that the Light is none other than the cosmos, a fundamental oneness only perceived as fractured through the limited lenses of mortal understanding.

Though every rite affirms the whole chain, tradition and temperament have long since settled toward one link or another. Orthodoxy in An-Mirda and Aramoor dwells longest on "'One is Three": the Light expressed and attained chiefly through the inward triumph of Body, Mind, and Soul; the Autocephalous Church of the West finds its center in "Three is Six", the passage of that inward harmony extending outward, and traces it into household and society; and the Velkirian east leans hardest on "Six is All", holding that the Light is not a destination but the saturated totality already surrounding the faithful. None of the three denies the others' clause -- the creed is, after all, one chain -- but each has made a home in a different link, and a certain amount of theological needling, good-natured or otherwise, follows from it.

In speech and theology, the Lurist ecumene is given to thinking in triads or hexads in deference to their creed. Hence it is customary for Lurist homilies and hymns alike to be structured in triplets or sextuplets, as can be seen in much of Lurist scripture, the "Lore of Light". First and foremost of these is the original _Book of Verses_, a collection of 777 canticles and psalms set down through the Prophet himself over the period of his teaching. Second in prominence and theological standing is the multi‑volume _Book of Judges_, the rulings and elaborations by the hands of the Prophet's successors up to the founding of the First Hierophancy. Thereafter follows the _Later Lore_ of the collected exegesis and commentaries of later centuries.

## Daily Practice

To align the Heavenly Triad in daily practice, clergy will exhort the faithful to follow the Triad of Harmonious Society: Right Habits, Right Attention, and Right Appetites (both literally and figuratively). From this follow simple lay precepts: speak plainly, do honest work, restrain cruelty, take no more than your due, attend your commitments with earnestness and humility, and pay respect to the relations governing your place in life.

Monastic houses stress contemplation as alignment rather than withdrawal, and quiet discipline: a morning recitation of select verses, an examination of conscience at night, fasting in certain periods or contexts, humility and mindfulness in daily craft and conduct. Household devotion attempts the same, but usually falls short in one category or another.

The reason Lurism admonishes the faithful to adhere to Right Habits, Right Attention, and Right Appetites is that bringing the Heavenly Triad into harmony is easiest when there is no discord in society. This is important because the triune harmony must be achieved so that upon the individual's death the Soul is able to rejoin Lur's Light: without triune harmony, the Soul is too weighed down by lingering appetites of the Body, or too tangled up in old habits of the Mind. 

## After Death

In the Lurist catechism, a person's judgment upon death is considered one of personal readiness and refinement, not tethered to promised rewards or just deserts. Sin, in this context, is wilful disharmony and dissonance, a rejection of harmony and overindulgence of the ego at the cost of the whole. Lurism does not teach a dogmatic concept of hell, nor of any original sin; yet all the same, early Judges characterized those opposing them as "void of harmony, mired in cacophony and catastrophe". This turn of phrase has over time given rise to a popular (though not theological) concept of the "Void" as the Light's antithesis.

In those who die without achieving triune harmony, the Soul is too weighty or too entangled to rejoin the Light, and it returns to the world in a new triad to seek more refinement. This is not reincarnation, but conservation: nothing in the Light is ever unmade, only transformed. Regarding those who rejoin the Light, common lay belief speaks of them joining the "Illuminated Choir" or "Chorus of the Light", singing in eternal harmony with all of existence, a concept quietly accepted by clergy as a helpful crutch to laypersons even though it is nowhere stated to exist in the Lore of Light.

The Lurist concept of sainthood comes from those who achieved triune harmony in life and yet opted to withhold joining the Light to guide others on their path. Their Souls are said to remain in the world, illuminated by the One Light like mirrors in a lighthouse, that the faithful may better find their way to harmony. Their guidance is readily invoked in prayer by any Enlightened, but their guidance is considered strongest near the stations of their mortal life, which are all considered pilgrimage sites for this very reason. All saints are portrayed as shining by conduct, but sculpture in the otherwise aniconic Lurist tradition is unflattering of a person's physical flaws and generally portrays without pretense.

These pilgrimages are to be held without pomp, as a sort of wandering meditation, reflecting on the lives of the saints and how best to emulate them. Extravagance in a pilgrim's means of travel or comportment is traditionally frowned upon.

## Good Behaviour and Social Mores

Modern Lurism engenders a tender suspicion of forcing outcomes -- not passivity, but the craft of finding the right channel where small nudges do the most good, as true mastery is a refusal to add noise to the world so as to maintain harmony where it may be found. This contrasts with early Lurism, which as a crusading religion was intent on eradicating disharmony. (The staunchly conservative school of Antidiscordianism retains most of this more active spirit, for good or ill.)

Lurism's appreciation for harmony extends to the idea that a working pattern can be more worthwhile than a theoretical plan: if something -- a street, a law, or an engine -- works cleanly without constant rectification, it is rightly made, even if it does not match the original intent. This extends to education and guidance: a child's bent is a grain to be followed and finished, not planed flat. Of course this is an area ripe with tension between the teachings of well-intentioned clergy and the lived reality of families.

Within the clergy, the theological turn away from forceful conversion and theocratic ambition, combined with the long-standing aniconic tradition, has given rise to repeated voices calling for restraint and right conduct within the church -- a commandment to prevent disharmony instead of commanding spectacle. This is particularly strong in the Concordianist western church in the Commonwealth. Still, spectacle has its place in the liturgical order of things (particularly in stratified societies such as the Kingdom of Lavion), and rich processions or lavish feast days to conclude periods of fasting are not seen as incongruent with this thought.

In society, Lurism teaches that hierarchy is not a ladder to climb but a choreography that keeps collisions few -- herein, too, is harmony. Societal harmony is one of the largest concerns of the modern Lurist church. Besides the Social and Heavenly Triad that govern behavior, Lurism also places high value on harmony within the family, a network of filial piety: honor your forebears, be a worthy peer, and care for those in your charge.

The creed "One is Three is Six is All" (which at its core describes the relationship between the Light and the World) is often transplanted into a more personal setting to teach mores: the one Individual is contained in the Triune Family (Father, Mother, Child), which is embedded within the reciprocal Six Relations toward Light, Liegelord, Elder, Peer, Liegeman, and Ward, from which together is formed the All of society. To maintain harmony in these relations enables a faithful Enlightened to better establish the inner harmony of the Heavenly Triad.

## Theological Schools of Thought

In the so-called Anish Rite, derived from the ancient forms codified in An-Mirda, Orthodoxy holds the center, guarding the canon and the received rites under the present Third Hierophancy and binding diversity by conciliar discipline. Maintaining societal harmony is the mainstay of the orthodox clergy, which makes for conservative thinking but also a distrust of extreme measures.

Harmonism, strong in eastern Lavion and its academies, aligns doctrine with the lawful structure of the world and exults meticulous craftsmanship and scholarly rigor as virtues; it is praised for the heights of its rhetoric and sometimes chided for the ivory tower from which it preaches.

Redemptionism regards correcting the discordant sinner as a faithful's most important charge, and sees the Light as immanent in the penitent; it values mercy and restitution, and teaches that honoring the reciprocal flow of rights and duties in the Six Relations is the most righteous expression of Right Habits.

Antidiscordianism, drawing zeal from the earliest eras of the faith, preaches with bracing candor that worldly discord must be corrected before spiritual harmony can be achieved; it thrives in the Patriarchate of the North and the holdouts of the Eternal Dominion that stand ready to cut away what resists being brought into the Light's harmony.

Aligned with the Anish Rite but independent of it, Concordianism, vigorous in the Commonwealth, denies the primacy of the Third Hierophancy while remaining in communion. It trusts synod and city over a single primate, reaches doctrine by deliberation, and prizes social cohesion and civil works as measures of holiness, concerned with the creed's "living center" -- the passage from the personal harmony of Body, Mind, and Soul outward through the Triune Family of Father, Mother, and Child into the full web of the Six Relations.

Fully separate is the Velkirian or Eastern Rite of the Kharunites, prevalent along the coasts of the Eastern Gyre and up the Mer into the lands aligned to the King of Kings in far-away Vel Kiriya. Contrasted with the Anish tradition it preaches the "Light in All" as a panentheist reality, and sees the veneration of the elements as no different from the veneration of saints -- both are acceptable intercessors and instantiations of harmony with and in the Light. Where western catechism centers the Heavenly Triad, Kharunite teaching sets alongside it the Three Natures of Beast, Man, and Saint, to be schooled and balanced rather than ranked; and where the Anish Rite settled a single Hierophant atop its hierarchy, the Velkirian Judgeship never did, remaining to this day a mendicant office exercised on the road rather than from a throne.
