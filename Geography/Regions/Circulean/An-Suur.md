An-Suur is the Circulean's north wall: a long green coast that drinks the sea's breath and wrings it into rain, then throws a hundred hurried rivers back into the Gyres. To the west the shoreline bends toward the Suurian Gates -- the straits that open the Circle Sea to the wider ocean -- while to the south, just across those waters, lies [[Stranu]]. Far inland the land climbs in broken steps toward [[Sarm]]'s mesas and high desert, and northward the canopy deepens into an unconquered jungle that has long been a living frontier rather than a mapped edge. The region is the first hearth of the Anish and, much later, the cradle of Lur's Theocracy at An-Mirda; when Ejitwe the Starkin broke that theocracy in 336 AR, he did it out of these same jungles that had sheltered his kin since time immemorial.

The coast wears a humid subtropical belt in its southern reaches and turns fully tropical northward. The shore is low and articulate -- mangrove fans, barrier bars, and tide-scoured inlets -- because no single river rules the plain. Instead the rain engine that runs on sea wind and noon heat spits out ceaseless downpours, each creek rising fast, each river descending in steps and shelves, dropping through short cataracts before it slows again to meet the tide. Mouths shift after floods; bars build and break; black-tea waters ooze from leafmold swamps beside glass-clear runs that fall out of the first hills. Sailors and pilots here speak in the old way of Morwynt's humor, because a single hard squall can turn a bar from passable to murderous in a turn of the glass.

Across the whole north the jungle thickens until it becomes the barrier the old itineraries warned of -- not a line on a map, but a breadth of green where sound dies and the sun is a story. The Starkin, Kin of Brother Bat, have lived with that canopy as house and rampart since before the Prophet's day. Their bands hold memory by song and travel by water and bough; when the Theocracy pressed out from An-Mirda they answered not with siegeworks, but with dusk strikes, cut ferries, and vanished trails. The Green War that ended in the burn of An-Mirda was learned on these rivers and creeks, in rain that turns a footstep to silence and a torch to steam. Even now, at the jungle's edge, Starkin pilots and wardens sell their knowledge of safe channels and sacred groves under strict oaths -- no preaching, no mapping, no cut of certain trees -- and a client who breaks a term finds the river empty of guides before next dawn.

Hydrologically the country reads as a fan of short, steep catchments pressed against Sarm's escarpments. The eastern sky darkens early as trade winds pile into the stone and climb; every afternoon the hills drop their water back toward the sea in braided torrents. Each river keeps a posture of three parts: a tumbling upper reach of shelves and plunge pools; a broken middle where hardstone ribs force rapids and ladders of falls; and a short tidal reach where brackish creeks finger into mangrove and backbar lagoons. Navigation follows that grammar. Dugouts, cat-prowed river craft, and later Circulean shallops can nose in with the flood to the first rapids, but inland travel has always meant ladders of portage and causeway, not a single grand trunk stream. Early Anish polities made a virtue of that reality by siting their towns on the last firm bluffs above the tide and binding them together with raised roads that run dry even when the moon tides climb the roots; the Theocracy simply reused those sites when it turned shrines and stepped pyramids into parish hearts.

The sea side of this waterworld is ruled by bars, reefs, and the slow shove of the Gyres. Along the northwestern littoral the Circulean's circulation sets up longshore drift that builds spits across lesser mouths and tangles them into back-lagoons; entrances migrate, and harbors that were safe in spring can shoal by autumn. The farther west you stand toward the Suurian Gates, the more the tide speaks with two tongues -- the Circle Sea's quiet pulse and the ocean's longer set -- and pilots time their crossings to the meeting of those breaths. Everyone along these shores knows the Gates are not just a route to the High Sea; they are a weather hinge, and every town that sends caravels and caravans beyond them tunes its markets and feast-days to the seasons of wind.

To the east the foothills steepen and the rains hit hot stone that remembers drought. Here An-Suur leans on Sarm and both show their bones: dry fans at mesa toes that bloom into brown floods when the jungle's rivers overrun their banks, slot canyons that howl in sudden storm, and high steps where grass replaces canopy and the horizon goes hard. In years when the rains outrun the coast by weeks, the wadis waking on Sarm's side can carve new cuts in a night; in lean years the same channels vanish into gravel long before they find the mangroves again. The Patriarchate that claims both An-Suur and Sarm as the North has always preached an austere gospel suited to that country -- fast judgments, strong penances, crusader habits given civic shape -- and its old stepped pyramids still draw solitary worshipers at dawn, staring into the sun to feel the Light.

An-Mirda's ruin sits in the humid middle of all this water, a lesson and a landmark both. The Theocracy built a capital among rivers that never submit for long; the Thronebreaker's bands taught the Church that a creed that forgets compassion will find the jungle itself allied against it. The fall broke the First Hierophancy and scattered its keepers; later centuries learned to govern with cooler hands and parish habits rather than crusader drums. The hydrology did not change, only the reading of it: stepped pyramids became lookouts over flood, pilgrim roads became causeways, and the jungle resumed being the country's first authority on what paths endure.

## Civilisation's Growth

Picture a region 2500 leagues wide from western ocean to eastern stone, and yet with only the first 500 leagues inwards from the coast reliably known -- a thin littoral strip stitched to the Circle Sea, and behind it a hinterland that swallows civilisation the way a mangrove swamp drinks a flood. In a land like that, the sea becomes the only road long enough, straight enough, and forgiving enough to matter. Early Anish life learned this young. The coast offered a continuous corridor where a dugout could glide for days, while every inland path was a staircase of wet stone and sudden falls. Rivers ran short and angry, their mouths shifting with each storm; trails vanished whenever the rains remembered them. A village could haul a canoe over a sandspit and be in the next lagoon by noon; the same distance overland meant a week of mud, leeches, and portages that ate the soles off your sandals.

So the first polities grew wide instead of deep, beadwork rather than blankets -- strings of harbor towns pinned to promontories and river mouths, each feeding on its own steep, small catchment, each looking outward for its surplus. The grammar of movement was water to water: goods dropped down from the nearest shelf of hills in baskets and rafts, met a coastal carrier, then slipped along the margin to the next inlet. Where a bar closed a mouth the Anish cut a canoe road behind the dunes; where a headland threw tricky eddies they posted pilots, and the pilots became families, and the families became guilds. Even in the earliest strata of myth you can hear the cadence of this coastline economy: saints who walk the tides to find a lost child, kings who claim a "day's sail" of jurisdiction, bride-prices measured in outriggers.

Vessels tell the same story. The archetypal river canoe stayed shallow and narrow to climb rapids with poles and ropes; the sea canoe sprouted outriggers and cat prows to live with cross chop and to leap bars between sets. Sails arrived as clever canvas that could be dropped in an instant when a squall hammered the lagoon, then hardened into crab-claw rigs that loved to reach along the prevailing wind. Every innovation hugs the shore. You can read the map in the timber: leeboards for the shoals, kick-up rudders for the sandbars, lashings that can be repaired with cordage when iron is scarce. The ocean beyond the Suurian Gates came later, when seamanship and seasonal knowledge were fat enough to risk it; before that, the Gyres served as offshore conveyor belts, not invitations to leap into blue emptiness.

This seaward habit did not make the interior irrelevant, only expensive. Inland was always a ladder -- climb by water until the rocks say no, portage, climb again -- and each rung hosted its own upland partners. Starkin bands sat where canopy met rock and traded silence, salt, and news for iron, resin, and the promise that no one would draw churches on their maps. Upland timber and resins came down like seasonal blood, feeding shipwrights and shrine lamps; fish, salt, and woven sailcloth went back up by marriage and pact. Because the cost gradient ran so fiercely against overland caravans, the big population centers pinned themselves to estuaries and tidal rivers. Cities faced their rivers with steps and their sea with jetties; the state imagined as a necklace of ports, not a block of territory.

When Lur's Light was revealed, it rode the same water roads. Preachers cabotaged like merchants, hopping inlet to inlet. Parish networks spread where boats could moor, and the early Theocracy's reach mirrored cabotage limits -- strong along the rim, attenuated where bars shifted fast and pilots refused passage. The Green War that finally broke the theocracy only proved the rule: the rebels severed ferries and burned boathouses rather than take field battles inland, and the Church learned the old lesson anew that a creed without compassion will find the jungle enforcing quarantine. Afterward the Communion re-rooted itself in town charters and harbormasters, folding coastal logistics into parish life. Processions carry nets and oars. Feast days mind the wind calendar.

By 1420 the world has engines, clever charts, and better hulls, but the economics have not moved much. An airship can skip the mud, yet it cannot conjure naval spirits, cordage, and spare parts out of a raincloud; without inland depots it is a mighty knight with no hay to feed his mount. Coastal paddlewheels and sail have taken the longer legs, but the fundamental tessellation remains -- a human An-Suur that is only 500 leagues deep, braided along the tide line, with a supply chain that flows downslope to salt and then coastwise from cape to cape. Inland towns still grow where the last navigable reach meets the first reliable portage, and beyond that the map grows green again.

All of this has shaped a civilization that measures distance in days under sail, honors the pilot's oath as civic bedrock, and treats land borders as rumors until a river says otherwise. Law codes distinguish carefully between bar-harbor and open roadstead, fishermen's rights in the back-lagoons and merchants' claims to canal cuts through the dunes. Even aesthetics give the game away: long houses on stilts, boat sheds as public squares, temple steps proportioned to match the river's fall. The sea is the road, the river is the stair, and the jungle is the locked door that persuades you to try the balcony instead.
