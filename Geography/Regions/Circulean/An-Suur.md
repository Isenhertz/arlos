## Definition and Character

"An-Suur" names two things at once, and Circulean usage rarely troubles to distinguish them. In its broad sense it is the whole northern continent facing Rimec-Suur across the Bight, Sarm and the Merhal floodplains included. In its narrow and far more common sense, the one this entry treats, it is the westernmost region of that continent: the jungle-fringed, causeway-bound [[Humankind#Anish|Anish]] homeland proper, distinct from Sarm's highlands and the Merhal's river-kingdoms even though all three share the continent's name.

## Geography and Hydrology

The region is characterized by its subtropical and tropical climate, leading up to a veritably impenetrable wall of jungle in the northern parts of the continent that no expedition has returned from as yet.

To the west the shoreline bends toward the Suurian Bight and the Gate of Continents -- the strait that opens the Circle Sea to the wider ocean, named for the two landmasses it very nearly joins, beyond which An-Suur turns sharply to the north. To the south, just across the waters of the Bight, lies [[Stranu]], the northwestern arm of Rimec-Suur and the nearest of its shores. Far to the east the land climbs in broken steps toward [[Sarm]]'s mesas and high desert, and northward the canopy deepens into an unconquered jungle that has long been a living frontier rather than a mapped edge.

The shore is low and articulate, consisting of mangrove fans, barrier bars, and tide-scoured inlets, because unlike the Merhal or the Varsa basin, there is no large river to rule the region. Instead the rain engine that runs on sea wind and noon heat spits out ceaseless downpours, each creek rising fast, each river descending in steps and shelves, dropping through short cataracts to meet the tide. Mouths shift and flood; bars build and break; black-tea waters that ooze from leafmold swamps are as common as glass-clear runs that tumble out of the eastern hills. Sailors and pilots here speak in the old way of Morwynt's humor, because a single hard squall can turn a bar from passable to murderous in a turn of the glass.

Hydrologically the country appears as a fan of short, steep catchments pressed against Sarm's escarpments. The eastern sky darkens early as trade winds pile into the stone and climb; almost every afternoon the hills drop their water back toward the sea in braided torrents. Nearly every river consists of a tumbling upper reach of shelves and plunge pools, a broken middle of rapids and ladders of falls, and a short tidal reach where brackish creeks finger into mangrove and backbar lagoons. All human navigation of the region necessarily falls from that. Dugouts, cat-prowed river craft, and later Circulean shallops can nose in with the flood to the first rapids, but inland travel has always meant portages and causeways, not a single grand trunk stream, even one as bisected as the Varsa. Hence we find early Anish polities on the last firm bluffs above the tide, bound together with causeways or stilted footpaths that stay dry even when the tides climb the roots.

The sea side of this waterworld is ruled by bars, reefs, and the slow shove of the Gyres. Along the northwestern littoral the Circulean's circulation sets up longshore drift that builds spits across lesser mouths and tangles them into back-lagoons; entrances migrate, and harbors that were safe in spring can shoal by autumn. The farther west you stand toward the Suurian Gates, the more the tide speaks with two tongues -- the Circle Sea's quiet pulse and the ocean's longer set -- and pilots time their crossings to the meeting of those breaths. Everyone along these shores knows the Gates are not just a route to the High Sea; they are a weather hinge, and every town that sends caravels and caravans beyond them tunes its markets and feast-days to the seasons of wind.

To the east the foothills steepen and the rains hit hot stone that remembers drought. Here An-Suur leans on Sarm and both show their bones: dry fans at mesa toes that bloom into brown floods when the jungle's rivers overrun their banks, slot canyons that howl in sudden storm, and high steps where grass replaces canopy and the horizon goes hard. In years when the rains outrun the coast by weeks, the wadis waking on Sarm's side can carve new cuts in a night; in lean years the same channels vanish into gravel long before they find the mangroves again. The Patriarchate that claims both An-Suur and Sarm as the North has always preached an austere gospel suited to that country -- fast judgments, strong penances, crusader habits given civic shape -- and its old stepped pyramids still draw solitary worshipers at dawn, staring into the sun to feel the Light.

No river here has ever been worth conquering. A city that seized its neighbor's catchment would gain a fan of steep, short, quarrelsome creeks no more useful than its own, and would gain them at the cost of a war it could not sustain against a third city waiting on the next bluff over. The jungle to the north closes off inland growth outright, and the Starkin who hold it have never been dislodged for long; Sarm's escarpment does the same to the east, at a cost in blood the Bearkin exact from anyone who tries the passes carelessly. What is left is the coast itself and the sea beyond it, and it is there, rather than upriver, that An-Suur's cities have always had to grow.

Across the whole north the jungle thickens until it becomes the barrier the old itineraries warned of -- not a line on a map, but a breadth of green where sound dies and the sun is a story. The Starkin, Kin of Brother Bat, have lived with that canopy as house and rampart since before the Prophet's day. Their bands hold memory by song and travel by water and bough; when the Theocracy pressed out from An-Mirda they answered not with siegeworks, but with dusk strikes, cut ferries, and vanished trails. The Green War that ended in the burn of An-Mirda was learned on these rivers and creeks, in rain that turns a footstep to silence and a torch to steam. Even now, at the jungle's edge, Starkin pilots and wardens sell their knowledge of safe channels and sacred groves under strict oaths -- no preaching, no mapping, no cut of certain trees -- and a client who breaks a term finds the river empty of guides before next dawn.

## Civilisation's Growth

Picture a region 2500 leagues wide from western ocean to eastern stone, and yet with only the first 500 leagues inwards from the coast reliably known -- a thin littoral strip stitched to the Circle Sea, and behind it a hinterland that swallows civilisation the way a mangrove swamp drinks a flood. In a land like that, the sea becomes the only road long enough, straight enough, and forgiving enough to matter. Early Anish life learned this young. The coast offered a continuous corridor where a dugout could glide for days, while every inland path was a staircase of wet stone and sudden falls. Rivers ran short and angry, their mouths shifting with each storm; trails vanished whenever the rains remembered them. A village could haul a canoe over a sandspit and be in the next lagoon by noon; the same distance overland meant a week of mud, leeches, and portages that ate the soles off your sandals.

So the first polities grew wide instead of deep, beadwork rather than blankets -- strings of harbor towns pinned to promontories and river mouths, each feeding on its own steep, small catchment, each looking outward for its surplus. The grammar of movement was water to water: goods dropped down from the nearest shelf of hills in baskets and rafts, met a coastal carrier, then slipped along the margin to the next inlet. Where a bar closed a mouth the Anish cut a canoe road behind the dunes; where a headland threw tricky eddies they posted pilots, and the pilots became families, and the families became guilds. Even in the earliest strata of myth you can hear the cadence of this coastline economy: saints who walk the tides to find a lost child, kings who claim a "day's sail" of jurisdiction, bride-prices measured in outriggers.

Vessels tell the same story. The archetypal river canoe stayed shallow and narrow to climb rapids with poles and ropes; the sea canoe sprouted outriggers and cat prows to live with cross chop and to leap bars between sets. Sails arrived as clever canvas that could be dropped in an instant when a squall hammered the lagoon, then hardened into crab-claw rigs that loved to reach along the prevailing wind. Every innovation hugs the shore. You can read the map in the timber: leeboards for the shoals, kick-up rudders for the sandbars, lashings that can be repaired with cordage when iron is scarce. The ocean beyond the Suurian Gates came later, when seamanship and seasonal knowledge were fat enough to risk it; before that, the Gyres served as offshore conveyor belts, not invitations to leap into blue emptiness.

This seaward habit did not make the interior irrelevant, only expensive. Inland was always a ladder -- climb by water until the rocks say no, portage, climb again -- and each rung hosted its own upland partners. Starkin bands sat where canopy met rock and traded silence, salt, and news for iron, resin, and the promise that no one would draw churches on their maps. Upland timber and resins came down like seasonal blood, feeding shipwrights and shrine lamps; fish, salt, and woven sailcloth went back up by marriage and pact. Because the cost gradient ran so fiercely against overland caravans, the big population centers pinned themselves to estuaries and tidal rivers. Cities faced their rivers with steps and their sea with jetties; the state imagined as a necklace of ports, not a block of territory.

When Lur's Light was revealed, it rode the same water roads. Preachers cabotaged like merchants, hopping inlet to inlet. Parish networks spread where boats could moor, and the early Theocracy's reach mirrored cabotage limits -- strong along the rim, attenuated where bars shifted fast and pilots refused passage. The Green War that finally broke the theocracy only proved the rule: the rebels severed ferries and burned boathouses rather than take field battles inland, and the Church learned the old lesson anew that a creed without compassion will find the jungle enforcing quarantine. Afterward the Communion re-rooted itself in town charters and harbormasters, folding coastal logistics into parish life. Processions carry nets and oars. Feast days mind the wind calendar.

By 1420 the world has engines, clever charts, and better hulls, but the economics have not moved much. An airship can skip the mud, yet it cannot conjure naval spirits, cordage, and spare parts out of a raincloud; without inland depots it is a mighty knight with no hay to feed his mount. Coastal paddlewheels and sail have taken the longer legs, but the fundamental tessellation remains -- a human An-Suur that is only 500 leagues deep, braided along the tide line, with a supply chain that flows downslope to salt and then coastwise from cape to cape. Inland towns still grow where the last navigable reach meets the first reliable portage, and beyond that the map grows green again.

All of this has shaped a civilization that measures distance in days under sail, honors the pilot's oath as civic bedrock, and treats land borders as rumors until a river says otherwise. Law codes distinguish carefully between bar-harbor and open roadstead, fishermen's rights in the back-lagoons and merchants' claims to canal cuts through the dunes. Even aesthetics give the game away: long houses on stilts, boat sheds as public squares, temple steps proportioned to match the river's fall. The sea is the road, the river is the stair, and the jungle is the locked door that persuades you to try the balcony instead.

## History

### The Ancient City-States

The old Anish cities never aspired to unite under one crown any more than the hydrology would have allowed it. Independence among them was not a failure to consolidate but the assumed order of things: a city that dreamed of ruling its neighbor was mocked for the dream as much as for the impossibility of achieving it. What passed for order between them was a competitive fellowship of equals, sisters and rivals at once, bound by shared harbors, shared cults, and shared law rather than by any single throne.

Where a city's own bluff-top ran out of room, the old Anish answer was never conquest but daughter-founding: a mother-city would plant a new port further along the coast, kin in blood, cult, and speech, but sovereign in its own right from the day its walls went up. A daughter-city owed its mother sentiment, cult, and custom rather than tribute, and more than one such daughter would eventually outgrow the harbor that founded it. This is how Anish settlement came to run the length of coastal Sarm, and by the same pattern, carried far enough south and west along the Bight, it is how Anish colonists came to the northern shore of the Stranic peninsula in the same age that Svenish clans were pressing up its southern reaches. The two peoples met in the middle, mixed in the river-mouths and borderlands between them, and the Stranic people of the Commonwealth carry both bloods to this day, the older tribes of the peninsula having left the meeting only a memory and no name, as tribes so often do wherever the people who came after also happened to be the people who wrote things down.

Anish civic and religious life were never quite separate. The tallest building in an old Anish city was reliably a temple-treasury rather than a palace, and its priesthood held real economic power alongside its cultic authority: it took deposits, extended loans, held escrow, and witnessed contracts as a matter of course, so that a merchant's dealings with the temple and a worshipper's dealings with it were never fully distinct transactions. This mattered most where two rival cities had to make an agreement stick without either city able to force the other's hand. Such deals were sworn before a courtier or Noble Element both parties recognized as impartial, never before either city's own patron alone, since an oath sworn only to one side's cult bound no one but that side. Long before any Judge or Hierophant existed, it was these mutually recognized elemental cults that served as the Circle Sea's first neutral arbiters of contract and trade, and Anish commerce ran for centuries on oaths no city could unilaterally break.

That system did not survive its own success. Wealth accumulated in temple-treasuries invited exactly the abuses one would expect: priesthoods that grew comfortable, arbiters suspected, then shown, to favor whichever city paid them best, oaths that still bound the poor while the well-connected found ways to slip them. By the time Lur-Illuhan's early preachers reached the Anish coast, the old cults' claim to neutrality had worn thin enough that a creed answering to no single city, no single treasury, and no single priesthood's ledger offered something the discredited elemental arbiters no longer could. It is this vacancy, more than any argument, that gave the earliest Lurist preaching its foothold among a people not otherwise inclined to abandon gods that had served them well.

The takeover, once begun, was thorough, because the new faith did not merely out-preach the old cults but out-administered them. The Judges who arose in the Prophet's wake inherited the arbiter's old office almost whole: witnesses to oaths, menders of disputes between cities that could not otherwise trust each other's word, and, in time, keepers of the deposits and escrows the temple-treasuries had once held, since a merchant entrusts his silver to whoever he trusts to witness his contracts. Each function the young faith absorbed left the elemental cults with one less reason to exist in civic life, until what had been the beating heart of every Anish city was reduced to household veneration and harbor superstition, tolerated, folded into Lurist habit where convenient, and stripped of the wealth and the arbitration that had made the old priesthoods powers in their own right.

### From Arbiter to Judge

Long before any Judge set foot in a temple-treasury to claim its ledgers, the young faith had already made its peace with the Six. It was Bar-Samniya, second in the line the Prophet left behind, who first wove the elemental order into doctrine and spoke, in the same breath, of an eternal dominion the Light's harmony would one day hold over the world, a claim of triumphant hope in his mouth, not yet a claim of conquest. What Lurism came to reject was never the Six themselves, who remain to this day folded into the very shape of the creed; what it came to reject was a set of institutions built in the Six's name, once those institutions gave the Anish cause to doubt them.

That doubt took generations to ripen, and the faith spread through An-Suur's cities long before it needed to conquer any of them, because the role a Judge offered was one An-Suur already had a use for. A city that had sworn its contracts before an elemental courtier held in common trust with its rivals had no quarrel in principle with swearing them instead before a Judge who claimed the same neutrality on firmer ground; the office was familiar even where the officeholder was new. This is the whole of why the earliest Judges could move from harbor to harbor among cities that would never in the same age have accepted each other's soldiers: they were not asking to be let in as conquerors, only as arbiters, and arbiters from outside the dispute were a seat An-Suur had kept warm for centuries.

The turn from welcome guest to something sharper came from outside An-Suur rather than within it. Judge Saint Illut-Kharun, scribe before he was pilgrim and Anish-born like every Judge before him, had already drafted the first charter recognizing the faith's scattered congregations as a single Communion, in the same contractual hand An-Mirda's own notaries would have used to bind two trading houses. His death under a Remean tribunal, for the crime of preaching, turned a movement Reme had tolerated into one it could no longer ignore, and no longer be ignored by. It fell to his successor, Tar-Essari the Stern, to answer that martyrdom with structure rather than only grief: the Verse-Keepers he founded were charged at once with guarding scripture and testing orthodoxy, a single office built to do exactly what a persecuted faith and a discredited priesthood both required in the same hour. Where the old temple-treasuries had held deposits, witnessed contracts, and stood surety for the trust of rival cities, the Verse-Keepers inherited all three functions in one stroke, not because the faith coveted a temple's wealth, but because a city that no longer trusted its elemental arbiters still needed somewhere to swear its oaths, hold its escrow, and shelter its ledgers, and the Verse-Keepers were built, in that same generation, to be it.

What made this handover something sharper than a change of management was the particular shape of Anish judgment. A city that finds its notaries have been selling their neutrality is not merely disappointed in its institutions; by its own oldest and harshest reckoning, it has caught an oathbreaker, and no crime in An-Suur's accounting runs deeper. The temple-treasuries were not quietly outcompeted so much as tried and found guilty in the court of a culture that had never forgiven that offense in anyone else. Once the elemental priesthoods stood convicted in that light, correcting them stopped reading as sectarian ambition and started reading as simple justice, and it is here, rather than in Bar-Samniya's gentler original meaning, that "eternal dominion" hardened from a hope into a mandate: not the Light's harmony merely prevailing in the fullness of time, but its adherents positioned, by the plainest logic of Anish law, to dominate faiths that had already broken faith.

Within An-Suur itself the work was substantially finished by the time Tar-Essari's Judgeship ended. Oremas, his successor, was the first Judge not born on Anish soil, and the Judges who came after carried the faith along desert roads and crusading sea-lanes far from any city this account has named; the fight, by then, belonged to Reme and the wider Circle Sea, not to An-Suur alone. When Maverel the Undimmed was raised as First Hierophant and the Theocracy of Eternal Dominion took formal shape, it did so on a foundation laid two centuries earlier and closer to home: cities that had traded their elemental arbiters for Judges, their temple-treasuries for Verse-Keepers, and a hopeful phrase for a mandate, long before the wider Circle Sea had properly heard the Prophet's name. That the Theocracy which rose from this foundation eventually loosened its grip again, and that no secular unifier has ever risen in An-Suur to replace it, says less about any failure of ambition than about a country built, coast and canopy and escarpment alike, to resist being held by any one hand for long.

### The Missing Fleet

An-Suur was never the crusade's target. It was the crusade's point of embarkation. The crusading fleet's ships, and by tradition most of the war-priests who commanded them, sailed from Anish harbors, an Anish-built and largely Anish-crewed fleet drawn from the very shipwright legacy the cities had spent centuries building, raised under captains the Judges themselves had appointed and sent west against Reme in the war's culminating stroke. Its bronze-and-timber hulls bore no saints on their banners, only the creed's geometry, dot, triangle, hexagon, and circle: an army of war-priests continuing a war the crusaders had only ever fought with bronze, laminate, and zeal, since the Lurist host had no Shaping to call on and no true brass, which would not be recovered for another two centuries. What left An-Suur's harbors that year was a Bronze Age fleet under a zealot's banner, not a chivalric one.

It never arrived. Whatever happened at Remea in that instant, an annihilation the Theocracy's own chroniclers would later call a righteous verdict, it scrambled the Western Gyre badly enough that the Balboa-bound fleet was carried far south of where it meant to make landfall, and An-Suur heard nothing certain in return for years. What word did eventually filter back told of a crusade fought, and won, somewhere in the Wolves' Teeth, against a people no An-Mirdan captain had ever sailed to find; whether the fleet made an unplanned landfall near the Moonkin highlands and simply turned its zeal on the nearest heathens within reach, or was redirected there by design once word of Reme's fall somehow reached it, is a question the surviving chronicles answer with far more confidence than they have actual evidence to support. What is certain is only the shape of the absence: a fleet that sailed west came home, if it can be called coming home at all, having fought an entirely different war in an entirely different country, and a great many oarsmen and marines who left An-Suur's harbors as sons of particular cities never returned to them again.

For the cities left behind, the decade that followed was less a single catastrophe than a slow, uneven erosion. The short-hop cabotage trade that had always been An-Suur's real economic backbone, grain and dye and glassware carried cove to cove along a coast no single storm could touch all at once, barely noticed the Gyre's disorder; a bluff-city trading with its neighbor three coves over has little cause to venture into open current at all. What did suffer, badly, was exactly the trade the Anish were proudest of: the long blue-water runs to Balboa and the Circle Sea's farther ports, carrying goldleaf papyrus and smoked lizardmeat further than any coastal hop could reach. Ships bound out on those routes went missing at a rate no captain could plan around, the Verse-Keepers grew correspondingly cautious in the surety they were willing to underwrite, and for the better part of that decade the Anish coast's reputation for reliable, oath-bound commerce, the very thing that had made its old arbitration worth trusting in the first place, had to be sustained on local trade alone while its farthest-reaching ventures simply stopped answering.

### Ashes and Schism

By the fourth century the Theocracy governing An-Suur's own coast had become, in its own later Church's telling, centralized, complacent, and deaf to its own cruelties, and it was not a foreign power that finally answered that complacency but a prince of the very jungle its Green War had spent a generation trying to pacify. Ejitwe the Thronebreaker gathered his coalition from 327 AR onward: Starkin exiles and oath-bands first, but disillusioned converts alongside them, meaning some of the hands that eventually broke An-Mirda's gates belonged to Anish or Anish-descended folk who had once called themselves faithful and had simply stopped believing the Theocracy still deserved the name. The siege that followed ran forty days, and the relief other Anish cities tried to send never arrived in time to matter. Ejitwe did not hold the city he took. He burned its archives, broke the golden altars on its stepped pyramids, and left the ruin standing as warning rather than conquest, a choice that meant An-Mirda was not merely defeated but permanently unusable as anyone's capital again.

What this cost An-Suur's own cities was not only a capital but the one thing that had ever made them act like a single people rather than a competitive fellowship of rivals. The cities the Theocracy had bound together were quarrelsome by nature long before any Prophet arrived to bind them, and the binding was never more than a shared creed and a shared capital laid over an older, harder default. Once An-Mirda was ash, there was no capital left to hold that fellowship together, and nothing structural could replace it: the same hydrology that had always kept any one Anish city from conquering its neighbors was equally capable of keeping any one city from inheriting An-Mirda's authority by force. Cities that had marched, tithed, and prayed as parts of one Theocracy reverted, within a generation, to exactly the fractious, independent, mutually indispensable arrangement the region had always defaulted to whenever nothing else was actively holding it together.

The clergy who might have provided a new center instead split over where it now lived. Aramoor, until then a crusader outpost on the frontier of the Theocracy's southward advance, the same push that would eventually carry the faith into Rimec-Suur itself, became the practical refuge for the exarchs who fled An-Mirda's ruin, and within a generation its bishops were arguing that the Light's authority lived wherever the Church could still speak in whole sentences, in synod, statute, and seal, rather than in any one broken city. The clergy who stayed on An-Suur's own coast, the Patriarchate of the North as later ages would name them, heard this as exactly the kind of complacent rationalization that had rotted the old Theocracy from within in the first place, and insisted that only a line unbroken from An-Mirda itself could ever be lawful again. Neither side ever fully won that argument. It is still being fought, in one form or another, every time a Northern patriarchate enthrones an Anti-Hierophant against whatever Aramoor has since settled on, and it began here, in the bitterness of clergy who stayed to bury their capital's ashes watching the clergy who had fled build a new seat of power along the distant Varsa.

An-Mirda's ruin sits in the humid middle of all this water, a lesson and a landmark both. The Theocracy built a capital among rivers that never submit for long; the Thronebreaker's bands taught the Church that a creed that forgets compassion will find the jungle itself allied against it. The fall broke the First Hierophancy and scattered its keepers; later centuries learned to govern with cooler hands and parish habits rather than crusader drums. The hydrology did not change, only the reading of it: stepped pyramids became lookouts over flood, pilgrim roads became causeways, and the jungle resumed being the country's first authority on what paths endure.
