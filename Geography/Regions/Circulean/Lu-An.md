The plains of Lu-An are often given the moniker "Lonely Plains". Between their sheer expanse and the poor quality of the topsoil making agriculture difficult on a scale beyond simple subsistence farming, settlements are few and far between, and when found tend to be little more than hamlets clustered around a water source.

But to assume the plains devoid of life would do it injustice, for the relative inhospitability to civilisations with expansionist aspirations means the wildlife thrives there, and while farming is hard, it is well suited to the roving lifestyle of nomadic hunter-gatherers. 
## Of Land and Peoples

### The Land and Its Terms

Lu-An's inland is a sun‑beaten prairie and savannah whose water comes in pulses. Rivers here are not makers of seas but makers of basins. Most courses run swift in flood, spread into fans, then sink -- lost to gravel, reed, and salt. Only short coastal gullies reach the [[Circle Sea|Circulean]], and even those are typically choked by bar, dune, or sabkha so that their mouths spill sideways into lagoons rather than out to blue water. The coast itself is tidally rich but river‑poor; the Great Eastern Gyre piles sand and iron‑heavy grit along the strand while the land offers little steady fresh to push back. Those are the facts that decide much of what follows: the sea invites outposts; the plains break them.

Moreover, the great rivers of Lu‑An do not finish at the sea -- they finish in evaporative bowls, chotts and broad salt pans, or in chains of shallow lakes that travel with the century. Beds are wide, banks are low, and channels migrate until they find a hollow large enough to die in. Out of flood, the same rivers divide, vanish, and reappear as losing streams that bleed into the subsoil and rise again as springs days or leagues away. Law and travel follow this logic: routes are named for basins rather than sources, and every path assumes that a river will lie to you in the dry.

The largest of these basins sits on the western edge of Lu‑An and marks the border with [[Karsova]]. The Deserted Sea is an endorheic sink that alternates between mirror and marrow -- years of water ringed with reed and mud islands, and years of white crusts and sandstorms. Its telescoping deltas swallow caravans that do not read the season; its exposed bars braid into roads when the wind is kind. All who dwell here treat it like a clock: when the reed‑year comes, grazing and trade skirt its rim all the way to Karsova on the far side; but when the crust‑year returns, only raiders dwell here.

Where faults cut the plains or old basalt dikes stand near the surface, water finds its way up. These are the pearls on which routes are strung -- dependable springs, seeps collected in stone‑lined pits, and palm‑nursed gardens where sands and salinity can be beaten back by patience.

Along the Circulean margin, the Eastern Gyre distributes the black iron sands from [[Ertra]]'s volcanoes in heavy bands that march with the storms. Ports, where they exist at all, cling to headlands with clean draft and take iron, salt, and cured fish out while bringing water barrels and rope in. Inland peoples approach these margins warily -- the sea is rich, but memories run long in a region where the written word carries little weight, and the peoples of Lu-An remember many feuds.

Where the savannah climbs toward [[Lu-Pto]], the grass thins and salt rises in its stead. The ground begins to break into saline depressions and supratidal flats, filled shallowly with winter water but dry‑skinned and treacherous in the summer. Basalt ridges -- landmarks when they occur in the savannah -- here rise as islands of cool in this hot country and offer anchorage for gardens, shrines, and wayhouses. Fog off the Circulean and furnace winds in summer are common, and sand‑laced squalls can scour a camp to bone in a day. Here, on the shared littoral, is where the Gyre's iron sands are richest and most fought over.
### Landmarks and Oddities

In the age of the sorcerer‑kings a city is said to have travelled the heavens and ruled by sight what others ruled by wall. Its end is written not as a single crater but as a scatter of wounds across Lu‑An -- shallow bowls now weathered to pans and fans, rings half‑lost to reed and drift. In some places the ground is fused to glass under a thin skin of dust; in others, square‑cut masonry and fired tiles turn up after storms a hundred leagues from the next shard. Travelers speak of straight lines that make no sense in wind country -- alignments of rubble, buried lintels, and angles that reappear from basin to basin as if the city fell in pieces while still trying to hold its plan. Rumor names it the true seat of the First‑And‑Only, that feared first and greatest sorcerer-king, whose city could not endure the fall; other tellings make it a chain of failed engines of the Lesser Kings, brought down by pride. No text has yet settled the matter. The [[Sunkin]] treat the shards as graves; the [[Zanori]] as markers of oath and warning; the [[Ulul-Azek|Azeki]] as unlucky, and worth a wide berth.
### Peoples of the Open: Sunkin and Zanori

The Alvai Sunkin are tall, leonine, and communal. Their basic unit is the pride: five to nine adults with dependents who share food, risk, and repute. Pride bonds are oath and hearth both; a single Sunkin is formidable, a pride moving in agreement is a hammer. Unmoved by Lurist rites and unbroken by Theocracy or crown, they answer reverence with reverence and intrusion with challenge. Their wars come as surges of poised motion, their diplomacy as sunlit straightforwardness, their mercenary service only by intact prides that refuse to be split for pay.

The Zanori travel in tight kinbands and claim the steppe not as property but as memory -- places of birth, vows, and grief to which they return in season. Humanoid but with an antelope's lower body, they ride light and fast, speak in whistles and ululation, and keep a triarchic leadership of strongest man, eldest woman, and Skykeeper who reads wind and wing to set the path. At war they loose horn-and-sinew recurves and cast long bone-headed spears from full gallop; at peace they trade with elaborate gifts.

## Timeline

### The Remean Shore and the Inland Repulse (Remean Era to 0 AR)

Reme's first touch was maritime, and it came early: the black iron sands the Eastern Gyre lays along this shore made the Lu-An coast among the first prizes of the empire's eastward reach, and for the better part of a millennium Remean emporia, light forts, and sand-works stood on its deltas and lagoons. The interior was another matter. Generation after generation the legions sent columns inland along the river lanes, and generation after generation the columns came back shorter or not at all, for Zanori kinbands -- swift, horned, and deadly from the gallop -- broke infantry screens and bled Remean columns, and Sunkin prides made every river garrison uneconomical. No campaign ever made the plains a province, and no century passed without another being tried; the shore stayed Remean and the horizon stayed the Zanori's. Shaping gave the legions a massive advantage over its human opponents; it did little on wind-flattened flats where thirst does most of the damage, and where even Shaped legs could not pursue foes adept at vanishing into the horizon.

### Withdrawal to the Shore and the Quiet Collapse (0-300 AR)

When Reme fell, its littoral works in Lu-An did not die under sack so much as under silence. Without the sea's convoys the outposts ate their stores, bargained with whatever caravans dared the estuaries, and then eroded -- stone to village quay, quay to scavenged lintel. The hinterland neither noticed nor needed them. Prides and kinbands did what they had always done: moved with water and graze, traded news for safe passage, fought when honor or trespass demanded, and named the year by storms.

### The Years Between: Silence and Sedimentation (300-800 AR)

In the centuries after the Fall, the plains wrote their own calendar. Herds and storms set routes; oath-circles set law. Sunkin prides ringed oasis-holds in the high savannahs and ranged far afield as wardens, guides, or proud enemies. Zanori bands stitched trade and raid across escarpments and reedbelts, storing wealth in hornwork, horses, and vows. Along the western edge under the Wolves' Teeth, human raider-peoples in the land called **Karsova** began to consolidate into compacts and seasonal courts, proving that riders could bind more than a single season's allegiance without surrendering speed. That Karsovan habit -- oath before wall, portable court before fixed capital -- would echo east.

### Karsova's Lesson and the First Confederacies (8th-10th centuries AR)

As Karsova hardened its compacts, Lu-An's open societies experimented with larger tents of allegiance. Early horde-bound confederacies rose from rallying songs, great hunts, and vengeance cycles settled by prize and verse. Some were coalitions that marched under shared banners to break a drought-barter gone sour; others were caravans that turned raider in the lean year and merchant in the fat. A few became famous enough that distant chroniclers gave them names -- the kind that stick to memory more than map: the Red-Banner Horde, the Seven-Horn Court, the Summer Host. What matters is not their exact borders but their pattern: leaders chosen by deed and testimonial; tribute gathered as movable wealth; law traveling with the tent and the drum.

### Traders Return to the Shore (10th-13th centuries AR)

The end of the Dark Ages drew foreign attention back to Lu-An's coast. Charters from all around the Circulean touched down as wary posts, interested less in hinterland conquest than in the black iron sands sluiced down from Ertra's shield volcanoes and gathered by the Gyre along Lu-An and Lu-Pto. Ports learned caution -- stout palisades, generous guest-right, and one hand on the hilt when hinterlanders came to trade.

### The Azek Moment (c. 930 AR to present)

From the eastern uplands came the most enduring of the horde-bound states: the Hetmanate of Ulul-Azek. The Azeki welded clans into a rotating court that rebuilds itself where omens and wind agree. Their war-ladders are meritocratic and theatrical -- plunder tallies and verse standing beside scars and seat in council -- and their moustaches are as much oath-scroll as ornament. Through the Wolves' Teeth their hordes have tested Fiurisci and Engalas more than once; Lavion's marcher lords learned that snow, feast, and flattery win more seasons of peace than walls alone. The Hetmanate governs without a fixed city, yet it governs -- patrols across the eastern plains are regular, omens are calendared, guest-right is codified, and oathbreakers are made examples of with memorable ceremony.

## Spheres and Boundaries in the 1420s

Today Lu-An is many sovereignties layered. Sunkin oases and pride ranges anchor the high savannahs; Zanori circuits claim the riverlands and escarpment edges by memory and rite; Azek patrol belts sweep the east. Between them are corridors of courtesy where messengers ride under visible sign and ports that answer inland signals with lantern code. The coast is outward-looking, tied to the Gyre's iron and salt; the inland is inward-hearted, tied to hoofbeat and kin.

### Why No Kingdom Took the Plains

Three facts recur. First, **logistics**: graze and water do not favor fixed garrisons in depth. Second, **mobility**: riders choose when to be seen, and the pursuit that cannot close is a pursuit that bleeds itself. Third, **legitimacy**: on the plains, right is spoken aloud in oath and deed, not inscribed once in stone. Reme learned it, the Theocracy ignored it at cost, the marchers of Lavion respect it now by necessity.

### Continuities Across the Ages

From legion skirmishes to hetman rides, Lu-An's story is not a vacuum between coasts but a long continuity of motion. Prides and kinbands remain the root of order; compacts and courts appear, travel, and dissolve; and the greatest states adapt to that rhythm rather than erase it. The iron on the shore buys tools and hulls and makes distant furnaces glow; the hoof on the inland makes law and memory and keeps the sky wide and owned by none.
