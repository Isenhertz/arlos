Between the green coasts of An-Suur in the west and the slow-moving silver ribbons of the Mer and Hal in the east rises a region of steps. Sarm is a country lifted into the sun -- a broad, old plateau flensed by wind, baked by still air, and carved ragged where rare water remembers its strength. From a distance it reads as level. Up close it is nothing but edge: scarps and shelves, benches and breaks, slot canyons that turn noon into evening, and long stone ladders where each rung is a cliff.

Sarm is dry because the winds that matter spend themselves before they arrive. Moisture off the Circle Sea and the western coasts climbs An-Suur's low rim, rains promptly, and leaves only heat to cross the inland. What survives travels as high summer thunder -- slow-building anvils that burst over the plateau in brief, hard convulsions. The rest of the year is pale skies, chilled dawns, and afternoons that crackle with dust. In that rhythm the land keeps water hidden in stone, and moves it violently when it must.

The plateau's bones are cleanly layered -- redbeds and buff sandstones, the odd pale limestone bench, capped here and there by tougher dark flows from ancient fire. Where caprock endures, the surface holds broad, empty tables. Where it breaks, the softer beds beneath weather into badlands and labyrinths. Slot canyons chew down along joints, sinuously narrow and cold even at noon, with walls varnished black by slow mineral tears. Arches span dry gulches like shrugged shoulders. Hoodoos with white hats of cemented gravel stand in ranks as if the land remembered a parade and kept the formation.

Hydrology here is paradox with a straight face. Surface water is mostly absent, yet water writes every line. The ordinary state is dust, salt, and stone; the exceptional state is roar. For most of the year the washes are merely instructions -- sinuous grooves for the eye to follow. Then the high-sun storms arrive and the instructions are obeyed. A mile of dry stair suddenly becomes a brown, frothing ribbon, rising from trickle to wall without any intermediate mood. A canyon floor that will carry a caravan for months becomes a chute fit to flense a herd in minutes. The country breeds a precise fatalism: if the clouds stack over the western rim, do not be in the slots.

Because surface water cannot be trusted, Sarm's true rivers run inside the rock. Perched aquifers sit where a porous bed meets a tight one; along these contacts the plateau “weeps”. Gardens hang from orange walls where a green ledge seeps steadily, nourishing maidenhair and thorn, staining the cliff with high tide marks of copper and lime. In the deeper beds, older aquifers move as slow pressure, and along the eastern escarpment that pressure breaks daylight as artesian mounds. There the Merhal Front shows its special trick: scores of springs bubble through saltgrass fans to feed the Merhal's outermost channels even when the plateau itself has forgotten rain for a year. Caravan calendars are written to those springs, not to the sky.

Drainage divides Sarm into three manners of country. On the west the An-Suur Rim falls in a staircase of short, violent canyons that point like knives toward the jungled lowlands; their flows are flashy and brief, their mouths choked with coarse fans. In the heartland between rims lie the endorheic basins -- broad, shallow sags where water never finds the sea. These are crusted with white and ochre pans, brittle to the step. After a thunderburst they turn to mirrors that look navigable until they take a cart by the hub and a mule to the belly. As they dry, gypsum dunes creep away in low, featureless swells, marching slowly along the prevailing wind. On the east the land breaks at the Merhal Front -- a tall escarpment whose feet are braided by seasonal tributaries. Here the plateau's rare generosity meets the floodplain's patience and makes oases worth naming.

Soils are thin and frank. On the tables: desert pavements and bronze-varnished gravels that shrug off both hoof and plow. On the benches: gritty loams that can cradle low thorn and silver scrub if left unbothered. In the slot shadows: pockets of black earth where leaves collect and rot in slow privacy, feeding small groves that never show from above. When the high-sun storms pass, the country answers with its one riot -- a fortnight when every gutterline wears flowers and every flat carries a pale haze of seed.

Wind is Sarm's other river. It sorts, scours, and sings. At the caprock edges it shears into steady laminar lanes that whistle like taut cord; in the broken lands it eddies and loads dust until the air becomes a moving wall. Yardangs -- long, streamlined ridges -- align to that stress and point the way across otherwise trackless reaches. Wind also lifts fine salt from the pans and deposits it as fragile crust along leeward benches. Any road that forgets this will crack its wheels on invisible glass and throw sparks where none are wanted.

Life, where it insists, follows water's habits. Gallery groves climb the weeping ledges. The deeper canyons stitch themselves with shade and host green threads of spring to spring. On the Merhal side, reed and saltgrass take lines of flow and write them in permanent ink. Cliffs host colony nesters whose guano stains can give away hidden seepage from half a mile. Out on the tables, resilient scrub keeps the wind close to the ground and throws seed long, betting on the next impatient hour of rain.

Travel is possible, but only once you accept the plateau's grammar. The safe lines run on benches between the big steps, not on the canyon floors; the sane crossings of slots are where the walls are still talking to each other. Roads that last hug caprock and avoid pans, switchback with humility, and arrive at springs rather than at destinations named by optimists. True countryfolk move at dawn and dusk, lie up at noon, and keep a weather eye on the western rim even when the sky is polite. Where the great steps meet the east, caravan towns sit on stone islands surrounded by salina flats -- dependable ground above wet seasons, close enough to the artesian points to thrive.

The Dvergr mark Sarm in a way stone approves of. Many of the grander cliff faces carry neat mouths at sensible heights, tucked into alcoves above known flood lines and aligned with benches that a loaded cart can actually reach. The entrances interrupt the monotony of varnished wall just enough to draw the eye, and their apron ledges are worn by ages of feet from the plateau side and the floodplain side both. You do not need to ask what lies within to know that whoever holds those doors reads water as scripture and cliff as covenant.

The hazards are honest. Flash flood is the swift one. Dust is the slow one; it will get into your food, your joints, your temper. Salt pans punish the proud and reward those who test with a staff. Heat hammers less in the slots than on the flats, and cold will come at dusk even after a day that felt like a kiln. Thunder makes rules and then enforces them. Lightning hunts the caprock. When the weather is clear the land pretends to be asleep, but it is only counting down to violence.

Seen whole, Sarm is a library of the obvious made sublime. Water falls rarely, thus the land remembers it fiercely. Wind comes daily, thus the land listens to it and takes its shape. Stone is layered, thus the land is stepped; stone is jointed, thus the land is cut. Between An-Suur's green breath and the Merhal's patient spread, the high dry keeps its counsel -- a place of edges where endurance is a craft, and where every door cut into a cliff tells you that someone long ago took the time to learn the country's grammar and decided to stay.
