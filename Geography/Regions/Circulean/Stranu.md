The peninsula of Stranu sits between two very different waters, the Open Ocean to the west and the steady Western Gyre to the east, and its rivers and plants show that split. Winter storms arrive from the Open Ocean and drop rain on windward coasts and the first mountain ridges. Summer brings a dry high that locks in for months -- heat builds, thunder sticks to the hills, and many channels shrink to stones and pools. Along the Gyre rim, the sea is calmer and often cooler at the surface, so summer starts with morning stratus and sea breezes rather than big rains. Fogs add quiet moisture even when totals stay modest.

The Ocean-side northwest is the wet quarter. Short, steep streams tumble into drowned valleys and small estuaries. Banks carry alder and willow; backwaters fill with reeds and cattails. On the hills above, broadleaf woods grow on deep, humid soils: oak, chestnut, birch. Leached crests hold heather and gorse where wind and rain bite hard.

South and east, central sierras catch the last of the Ocean-side moisture. Cool north faces keep beech and fir in protected folds; higher belts grade into pine before giving way to juniper scrub and tough bunchgrasses near the top. In colder years, snow stores water for spring, stretching flow in rivers that would otherwise flash and fade.

Beyond the mountains, the interior has big seasonal swings. Winter rain fills banks, spring snowmelt adds another pulse, and summer draws rivers down to a trickle. The vegetation fits that rhythm: open oak wood pasture over spring meadows; pigs clean up acorns, flocks rest in shade when the heat comes on. In the driest basins the canopy thins to steppe with esparto and needlegrasses. Endorheic pans crust white in drought, and saltbush and glasswort mark the edges where minerals collect.

The Gyre coast is different again. Limestone lies near the surface, full of sinkholes and caves. Winter freshets vanish into the stone and travel underground, then return as cold springs at the foot of the hills. Some surface streams dry in summer while the spring runs below stay clear. Wide closed plains flood in winter and turn into hay fields by midsummer. Thin soils and strong sun favor evergreen shrubs and trees: holm oak and other hard-leafed oaks, wild olive, mastic, phillyrea, juniper. Open rock carries low, aromatic scrub of rosemary, thyme, lavender, and sage. Where the Gyre brings cool water up, fog belts and steady sea breezes soften the heat -- less flood, more dew, and plants that drink from air as much as soil.

River mouths tell the coast apart. On the Ocean-side, longer rivers wander across broad lower valleys and build sand bars, lagoons, and marsh. Reeds, rushes, and sea lavender spread across the mid-marsh; tamarisk fringes salty edges; alder woods occupy backswamps that seldom dry. On the Gyre side, short catchments make small deltas behind barrier spits or end in linked lagoons where the gentle fetch lets the shore grow intricate. Salt pans work where evaporation beats inflow, edged by glasswort and saltbush. Offshore, clear bays host meadows of seagrass that pin the sands and soften waves -- most robust on the quieter Gyre rim, but present anywhere the water stays clear.

Season rules, and fire enforces the rule. Summer drought, resinous shrubs, and lightning make burns inevitable at intervals. Many shrubs resprout from deep bases after fire; pines seed into the ash and take hold on rock and dune where little else can start. Soils steer communities -- acidic uplands lean to heaths and pine, limestone favors thyme turf and low scrub. On bare karst, tight shrub canopies shed water fast and sharpen flash floods. In the interior, open oak pasture slows runoff, traps silt, and deepens rich topsoil under old trees.

People have shaped water and plants for a long time. Ocean-side rivers with predictable winter rises support coppiced poplar and willow, pollarded ash, flood meadows, and orchards on levees. Gyre slopes are stacked with dry-stone terraces for olives, vines, figs, almonds, and carob; where springs are reliable, irrigated gardens stay green through the dry months. Resin comes from pines, cork from cork oak, cordage from esparto, tanning from sumac, red dye from madder, and oils and perfumes from the hill herbs. Salt, fish, and reeds make a second economy in lagoons. Herds move with the seasons -- up to cool pastures on the spine in summer, back under the oaks when the first rains wake the grass.

Seen from above, the map reads as belts. The wet Ocean-side northwest is chestnut and oak above ria estuaries ringed with alder. Windward sierras hold beech and fir in shaded folds and feed clear springs. The interior is a pale mosaic of oak pasture and grain, with saffron in airy basins and long, seasonal tributaries stitched between. The southeast breaks into steppes, badlands, and salt pans, with dwarf fan palm in the hottest pockets. Along the Gyre rim, karst peninsulas and quiet coves carry maquis on limestone, short rivers fold into lagoons and dunes, and the calmest bays show dark bands of seagrass through glassy water. Across it all the same rule holds: Stranu lives on winter rain stored in snow, stone, and soil, then spends it carefully through the long bright months that follow.
