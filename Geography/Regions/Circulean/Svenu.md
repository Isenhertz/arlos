## Definition and Character

Svenu is both the peninsula and the people upon it: a long southwestern arm of land running out of the Wolves' Teeth into the Open Ocean, and the indigenous folk who have held it since before any Anish foot touched Rimec-Suur. In Circulean scholarship the name refers first to the outer, fjorded coast -- the historic heartland of the raiding and long-distance seafaring for which Svenu is best known abroad -- but it stands just as readily, pars pro toto, for the whole peninsula, the Gulf it encloses, and the people of both shores alike.

Where Varngard, its cousin and offshoot across the Gulf, has hardened over centuries of river-wealth and Circulean contact into something the wider world recognizes as a state, Svenu has not, and by its own reckoning has no wish to. It remains a confederation of clans bound by custom, kinship, and the rotating gathering of the All-Thing rather than by council, charter, or crown -- conservative by conviction rather than by isolation, and increasingly so as the centuries have shown it, in Varngard, exactly what the alternative looks like.

## Geography

The Svenu peninsula runs southwest from the skirts of the Wolves' Teeth into the Open Ocean, and its climate runs with it: temperate in the north where it shoulders closest to Stranu, boreal through the long mountainous spine of the Wolves' Teeth and their foothills, and arctic at its farthest southern reach. In its folding, the peninsula encloses the Southern Gulf, separating that gentler sea from the Open Ocean beyond -- Svenu is not merely adjacent to the Gulf, but the very landmass that makes it a gulf at all.

The two coasts could hardly differ more. The outer, western shore faces the Open Ocean directly and is cut deep with fjords, a hard coast that bred the deep-water seafaring Svenu is known for. The inner, eastern shore, folded around the calmer Southern Gulf, is gentler by far, and it is here -- in reach of Varngard across the water -- that Svenu's agricultural heartland lies, feeding the peninsula that its harder, outer shore could never feed alone.

Trade with the Circulean is modest, mostly overland through the Lupines or along the coast through Stranic ports, and often limited to rare timbers, ores from subarctic mines, smoked fish, and hard liquor.

## People

The Svenish are tall, broad, and light in hair and eye, indigenous to the peninsula and its coasts since long before Anish settlement reached Rimec-Suur. Where the two peoples met and mingled, in the borderlands and river-mouths that would become Stranu, their blending gave rise to the distinct Stranic people of the Commonwealth -- so that Concordian Stranu's own softened customs, an inheritance custom descended from hlutasiðr itself and an ease with women in authority alike, are not late borrowings from a foreign neighbor but inheritances from Svenish stock at the root of Stranic blood itself.

## Faith

Svenu is heathen country by any Circulean accounting, and the plain, daily fact of Bonding does more to keep it so than any missionary has ever managed to undo. The Autonomous Church of the Southern Reach holds nominal writ over the peninsula from its seat across the Gulf at Hjalmsvik, and has held it for centuries without ever converting more than a thin, coastal fraction of the population in anything but name. What Orthodoxy elsewhere brands heresy or superstition, the Southern Reach has simply learned to live beside here rather than uproot: the Svenish venerate the Noble Elements outright as the real and tangible powers behind the world -- demiurges, in the reckoning of a foreign theology, though no Svenish tongue reaches for so grand a word for what feels to them like plain fact -- and hold every lesser sprite and courtier besides as those powers' own court and kin, owed the same ordinary courtesy any neighbor is owed. Where an Orthodox sermon warns elsewhere against confusing the bannermen for the liege, a Svenish clan-hall sees no confusion there to warn against: a moling felt at one's own hearth, or an aettling that answers a child's coming-of-age rite, is proof enough, carried on the body rather than argued from any pulpit, that the elements themselves are owed devotion and not merely careful handling.

The Southern Reach has made its peace with this rather than fight it, and the accommodations it is known for elsewhere in its writ -- ordaining women as deaconesses, abbesses, and chapterwardens without contest, preaching a plain, hearth-centered Fire's warmth rather than a sun-gazing enlightenment -- read, in Svenu specifically, less like doctrine freely given than like the standing price of being tolerated at all. Hjalmsvik keeps its seat, its chapterhouses, and its handful of genuinely converted coastal congregations, and calls that a going concern; it does not call it conversion in any fuller sense, and after this many centuries knows better than to try.

### The Noble Six as Kin

Where Circulean theology names the Noble Six as remote, singular powers, Svenu speaks of them the way it speaks of its own aettlings: as kin, called by titles that read as family rather than sovereignty. Sæmóðr, the Sea-Mother, is Water; Dauðisamma, Granny Ice-Death, is Ice; Styrisystr, Sister Storm, is Air; Arnvörðr, the Hearthkeeper, is Fire -- an epithet Svenu shares, unknowingly, with distant Reme's own old name for the same Noble; Sprotafaðr, the Sproutfather, is Soil; and Járnibróðr, the Iron Brother, is Stone. None of these names implies worship of a stranger power granted from on high: a Sea-Mother, a Granny, a Sister, a Father, a Brother are addressed the same unremarkable way a Svenish household addresses its own dead and elder kin, and the same logic that makes a clan's own aettling a mark of belonging rather than command extends, for the Svenish, all the way up to the Noble Six themselves -- family first, and power only ever incidental to it.

## Society and Governance

### The fyrstinna and the reckoning

Authority in Svenu runs through the fyrstinna (singular fyrstinne), the senior matriarchs of the clans, whose judgment stands behind both the peninsula's oldest customs and its ongoing encounters with the wider world. It is the fyrstinna who judge the fittingness of a hosting-clan's gifts at the All-Thing, and it is the fyrstinna, by extension, who judge everything a returning captain, a foreign bride, or a foreign good brings home with it -- a standing, informal process the Svenish call simply the reckoning. Nothing is refused on principle alone; a thing is asked whether it concentrates power in hands the fyrstinna cannot check, and whether it demands the kind of open display Svenu's honor-custom has always named as its opposite. Aetheric alumen, choir-engines, and the academy prestige that comes wrapped around them have, so far, failed that reckoning consistently; a foreign captain's trick of knot or sail, kept within a household's own hands, more often passes.

### Dugsiðr and hlutasiðr

Svenu keeps leadership and property apart by deliberate design, through two customs that work in tandem rather than as variants of one another. Headship of a clan, and captaincy of a ship, passes by dugsiðr -- or the "custom of capability," often calqued as "ducidry" outside of Svenu -- to whichever eligible adult proves most capable, never automatically to the eldest child or to whoever last held the office. The person so proven is a fyrstingr, or, as is overwhelmingly the ordinary case in Svenu, a fyrstinne -- the same title already used for a clan's senior matriarch, since in practice a fyrstinne simply is her clan's own dugsiðr-proven leader, the two words naming one and the same standing rather than two different things. Property passes by hlutasiðr instead -- the "custom of allotment," falsely rendered as simply "lottery" in Circulean sources -- partitioned among all heirs, sons and daughters alike, rather than concentrated in whoever happens to hold office at the time. The name says plainly what the custom does: a hlutr is a lot, a portion, a share, and what a Svenish heir gains is what was literally allotted to them in the dividing, not what they were simply given. The two together mean a clan's leader and a clan's wealthiest member are, as a rule, two different people entirely, and Svenu does not regard that as an oversight to correct.

The effect runs the same guardrail as the reckoning and the boastfulness taboo, only built into inheritance law rather than judged case by case: property cannot pool into a single dynastic line the way strict primogeniture would allow, and headship cannot be bought or simply inherited by whichever house happens to be richest, since wealth and office are kept from reinforcing one another generation after generation. A fyrstinne's own standing follows the identical logic in miniature -- earned by age, deed, and reputation, never a title a daughter is owed by birth alone.

Raunni names the process by which dugsiðr is actually carried out, and it is not a single act of proving so much as a protracted one: a candidate's early promise is watched, then tested over time against her character and her deeds, and only once that promise has held is she formally acclaimed to the headship in question. It unfolds in two rather different registers depending on how the vacancy arises. Where a sitting fyrstinne dies, raunni runs as the quieter of the two: a natural process of evaluation among the clan's eligible women, unfolding over whatever time the clan needs, and customarily brought to formal acclaim at the next Allþing, where any change of headship in any case wants recognition before the wider gathering. Where a sitting fyrstinne is challenged rather than simply succeeded, raunni instead runs as open contest -- a formal challenge mounted by whatever means the clan and the circumstance judge fitting, tested along the same lines of character and deed, but compressed into public view rather than left to unfold quietly over seasons.

Hlutasiðr carries a plainer, less comfortable consequence besides. Partitioning farmland on the gentler inner-Gulf coast among every child fragments a holding fast; a family of three or four children can see one workable farmstead become three or four much smaller ones within a single generation. This is very likely no small part of why so many young Svenish take to a ship rather than stay and work a shrinking plot -- the raiding and Rim-trade tradition is fed as much by an inheritance system that makes the sea the practical alternative to a land-share grown too thin to live on, as by temperament or custom alone. A child who takes to the sea does not simply forfeit a share owed by law and custom; it is given over outright to a sibling who stays, or traded away for passage, gear, or a stake in a ship, in some settled arrangement between them. The former sits very comfortably alongside Svenu's own gift-economy -- a share of land handed to a staying sibling is exactly the kind of well-fitted, rightly-judged generosity the fyrstinna already prize, and a sea-bound child who gives their share freely rather than merely abandoning it loses nothing in standing for going, and often gains something in the giving. A captain's berth, unlike a strip of inherited field, remains something a capable but landless younger child can still rise to on dugsiðr's own terms.

The oldest layer of hlutasiðr, by report, took the word's literal sense further than any clan still practices today: shares not measured and assigned by agreement, but drawn blind, lot for lot, leaving even a favored or capable heir no more say in what fell to them than luck itself allowed. Vanishingly few clans keep this fuller, harsher form now -- most hlutasiðr in living practice is negotiated and agreed rather than drawn -- but the memory of it persists, and a clan said to still cast lots for its dividing is spoken of as holding to the old way in full, for better or worse.

### Honor and its opposite

Svenish honor is built on generosity rightly judged: a gift, a hosting, a season's hospitality that fits its recipient so naturally it seems to have cost nothing to give. Its opposite is not stinginess alone but boastfulness -- generosity performed for its own glory rather than the gathering's sake, which the fyrstinna are said to recognize at a glance and to punish more surely than any outright refusal to give. It is this same root concept of honor-as-earned-standing that Varngard's warband culture carried south and gradually reshaped: a Varngardi house may openly fund a warband or endow the Southern Reach to buy the means of earning stature, a transaction no Svenish clan would attempt without shame, for in Svenu standing that is visibly gone and gotten is standing already spoiled.

### The Allþing

The Allþing -- the All-Thing or Great Gathering, as it is generally glossed -- is Svenu's confederal gathering, and unlike Varngard's fixed-seat Hall it holds no permanent capital. Hosting rights for the coming year's gathering are contested at the close of the current one: a clan stands and declares its intent to host, backed by a promise of what will be given, and the fyrstinna of the assembled clans judge, the following year, whether that promise was kept and kept well. Hosting confers real prestige -- precedence in seating and speech, first right to bring a matter before the moot -- at the real cost of feeding, housing, and gifting every clan that travels to attend. A clan judged to have given generously and well builds credit that can carry it toward future hosting even in a lean year; a clan judged boastful in its giving loses standing the same season it thought to gain it.

The institution has not stood entirely still. Centuries of contact with the Southern Reach and, through Varngard, the wider Circulean world have pushed the All-Thing toward slow, incomplete formalization -- a gathering that keeps better account of itself, that increasingly answers a Circulean visitor's expectation of "who is in charge here" with something closer to an actual name -- without yet becoming the kind of settled institution Varngard's Hall has become. Whether it ever will is, by most fyrstinna's own reckoning, very much the point being resisted.

## Bonding and the Sea-Bound Elementals

Where Circulean folk practice keeps its courtesies to a hearth, a well, or a threshold, the Svenish practice samasiðr -- the "custom of sameness," generally glossed simply as Bonding -- an elemental relation that travels with its bonded charge rather than staying behind at any fixed place. The custom, and its two elder terms, are Svenu's own -- Varngard inherited them during the boat-clan era before its own people settled south, but kept only the narrower, person-to-person form; Svenu alone still practices it in full, and always as a bond to a particular element rather than to the person, ship, or clan as such -- a moling or adaling is an Air-bond, a Water-bond, an Ice-bond, and so on, never a bond to "a person" in the abstract.

A moling is a sprite bonded to a single person, and is named for whichever element first took notice: an Air-moling rides a person's own breath and the flutter of loose cloth or sail-cord; a Water-moling clings to rain on skin or the wake left wading ashore; an Ice-moling lingers in the frost that forms first on a favored blade or doorstep; a Fire-moling in the coal that catches quickest at one hand's striking; a Stone-moling in the give of a familiar footpath; a Soil-moling in the particular richness of a well-kept garden bed. It is won the same patient way any local invoking-relationship is won elsewhere -- sustained courtesy, returned often enough for an acquaintance to settle -- and common enough that most Svenish adults carry at least one, tied to whichever element their own life has kept closest company with.

An adaling is a courtier's own bond, never cultivated but earned outright: the courtier alone judges a single deed worth honoring, without respect to the age or standing of whoever performed it, and the bond, once given, survives ordinary misfortune and is lost only where a person becomes the plain opposite of the deed that earned it. Where a moling is felt more than seen, an adaling is a thing that answers a summons in the flesh, and Svenish tradition holds, almost without exception, that a bonded courtier takes the shape of a beast proper to its own element rather than anything resembling a person -- though no two tellings agree on exactly which beast, only that it is always some beast and never a face. An Ice-adaling might be told of as a hart of hoarfrost and antlered ice in one hearth's story and a lean snow-wolf in the next; a Water-adaling as a breaching shape of living wave in one telling and a great eel of running current in another; a Fire-adaling as a coal-eyed hound here and a stag of banked embers there; an Air-adaling as some swift, shadowless gryph of an old mountain breed, its exact kind never agreed on; a Stone-adaling as a boar of bare granite or, just as often, a ram of the same; a Soil-adaling as a badger or a hound of black loam. Tales of a courtier wearing a human face are told only in the oldest and least trusted stories, and most Svenish find the notion faintly ridiculous -- a courtier, in this telling, is a beast that has learned cunning, not a person who has learned to burn or freeze.

Svenu alone extends the practice past the person, and does so along the same elemental lines rather than to "the ship" as an undivided whole. A skipling is a sprite bonded to whichever element sustains a vessel's passage -- an Air-skipling in the wind that fills her sail, a Water-skipling in the water parting at her keel, an Ice-skipling in the floes of the fjord she was launched and wintered among -- and a single ship may gather more than one, each tended by whoever currently crews her and carried into every water she sails: an Air-skipling filling a slack sail, a Water-skipling easing a keel through chop, an Ice-skipling easing passage through skim-ice or warning of a floe not yet fit to trust.

An aettling is rarer and graver still, and reaches wider than any ship: a courtier bonded, along the same elemental lines as any adaling, to a crew, and just as often to a whole clan, as a totemic marker of belonging rather than any one office or command -- earned only by some deed the crew or clan did together, and answering afterward to that continuity rather than to whoever currently leads it. Like any adaling, it takes some beast-shape of its own element, whatever that telling settles on, and a clan is often known afterward, informally and half-affectionately, by the shape its own aettling happened to take -- one clan's folk called the Ice-Hart's kin, another's the Stone-Boar's folk, purely as that clan's own history settled it -- long after the deed that won the bond has passed out of living memory. Clans that keep an aettling treat it as living proof of who belongs and who does not, closer in weight to a shared name or a shared hearth than to any personal honor, and it has been known to outlast several changes of captain or clan-head alike, provided enough of the old temper survives among the living for it to still recognize.

Where a clan keeps an aettling, a child's coming of age is often marked by a rite in which the child is set to "command" it -- word chosen deliberately, since no bond of this kind answers to command at all, and asking one to obey would itself be the very transgression Invoking has always refused. What the rite actually tests is recognition: whether the aettling accepts the child as truly belonging to what it was bonded to, however the courtier judges that for itself, and a rite that ends in silence rather than accord is remembered, and quietly worried over, for a long time after.

Bonding carries real weight in Svenish raiding and voyaging alike. A captain who gathers more than one adaling in her own name, of more than one element, is read as marked for something exceptional, but the fyrstinna are quick to remind any crew that the bond only ever promises -- it is the captain's own command and character that must supply what the elementals merely judged possible, and more than one tale is told of a many-bonded captain who lost her crew's faith regardless. In the ordinary run of a voyage, moling and skipling see to the small, constant work of their own element: wind for a slack sail, calm water for a landing, a thinned fog for an unseen approach. Where a raid turns to a fight, an adaling or aettling answers the summons in earnest, loping or breaching or striding to its bonded warrior's side in whatever shape it long ago settled into, and fights there as much with tooth, horn, and weight as with any local mastery of its own element -- a steel-bull goring and trampling as readily as it hardens the ground beneath a foe's own feet, a fire-gryph raking with burning claws as readily as it sets a foe's banner or shield ablaze. This is exactly what makes a bonded beast so formidable a thing to face: precious few human weapons, and bronze least of all, bite reliably on a creature that is, in some real sense, its element made momentarily flesh, and a warrior without steel of her own, or a bond of her own to answer it, has little but flight or luck to fall back on.

Where two Bonded Svenish parties come to blows, the fight reliably splits along this same line: the humans engage each other, weapon to weapon, while each side's bonded beast moves to pin or press the other's, since it is only an elemental, in the end, that can meet another elemental on anything like even terms. The result is a fight fought on two fronts at once, human against human and beast against beast, and it is commonly decided not by whichever pair wins first, but by whichever side wins a front first and can then turn its freed hand or freed beast against the other -- a warrior who dispatches her human opponent while her bonded beast still holds its own foe pinned suddenly presses a two-to-one advantage that the losing side rarely survives. Formidable as a bonded beast is against ordinary steel and flesh, none of this exceeds a courtier's ordinary reach -- a real and noticeable effect, never the catastrophe only a Noble Element commands -- and it is precisely because the fight stays on that human scale that pinning, not annihilating, is what one bonded beast can generally do to another.

## The Svartalfr of the High Fells

High in the Wolves' Teeth, in a fastness remote enough that few Circulean travelers have ever laid eyes on it, lives the svartalfr -- a Svenic name, and an outsider's one at that, for the black-haired hair and bearing Svenu first saw and never asked further about; kin of Lumal-Nâri the Wolf Mother, and the last enclave of the Moonkin never swept up in the diaspora that scattered their Alvai kin from the range at large after the Theocracy's crusades. What they call themselves, in their own dialect of Ngerhemäir, is kept as closely as any Moonkin keeps a family rite-name from the world outside it -- more closely, if anything, given how fiercely this enclave in particular holds to old ways its lowland kin let slip. Many svartalfr no longer count themselves part of that wider scattered diaspora at all, holding instead that they alone kept the old ways whole while their city-settled kin traded custom for coexistence -- kin and strangers both, even to a Moonkin born in a Circulean port.

Trade with the lowlands brings bone and horn inlays, plaited work, smoked meat, and, rarest and most prized, an axe or blade of their own forging down from the high fells, for the svartalfr learned the secrets of steel before any lowland smith did. Their long Alvai lives have made them without rival as smiths anywhere in Arlos, and it is the svartalfr who take Varngard's bog iron -- carried across the Gulf for exactly this purpose -- and return it as steel with no equal in the known world. Circulean merchants credit the blades to Svenu rather than to Varngard's marshes, not from ignorance of where the ore is dug, but because it is Svenu's mountains, and no one else's smiths, that finish it.

It is from the svartalfr, and not from any Svenish root of its own, that Svenu takes the custom of the exibrúðr, the axe-bride: a warrior woman who forswears clan and kin outright to spend her long training on the mastery of the axe alone. Svenu holds the svartalfr in real, if wary, respect for this and other old ways, and the borrowing runs only the one direction -- a svartalfr axe-bride is spoken of as the custom's truest form, a Svenish one as an honored imitation of it.

## Historical Overview

Little is attested of Svenu in the **Rivian Epoch**; the peninsula lay, as it has lain through every age since, outside the reach of the great riverine civilizations of the north and east, its own history beginning, so far as anyone can trace it, on its own terms.

By the **Remean Era**, Svenish boat-clans were already raiding and trading out of the southwestern fjords, delving past the Suurian Gates and into the Gyres themselves. Reme knew of them -- knew them rather better, in fact, than most northern powers of that age knew their own hinterlands -- and met the odd raiding clan with the same polyreme patrols that kept the rest of the Circulean rim in order; but Svenu lay far outside the Circle Sea proper, and never entered the scope of empire, whether as province, tributary, or serious ambition. It was in this same era that the first lasting Svenish settlements appeared south of the Wolves' Teeth, in the country that would in time become Varngard.

The **Age of Enlightenment**, proclaimed by the victorious Theocracy in Reme's ashes, passed over Svenu as thoroughly as the empire it replaced; whatever upheavals convulsed the Circulean heartland in those centuries, they had no purchase this far from the Circle Sea.

It was the **Dark Ages**, following the Sack of An-Mirda, that made Svenu's name abroad. With Circulean shipwrighting itself diminished from Remean heights and not yet recovered, Svenish dragonboats commanded a rough-water seamanship no Circulean hull could then match, and Svenish raiders ranged deep into the Western Gyre and beyond it into the Open Ocean proper, striking coasts no Circulean captain of the age would have dared approach. It is from this period that Lurist clerical accounts, encountering a matrilineal, raid-hardened people they could scarcely credit, coined the enduring epithet "the matriarchy of the ice-sea" -- a phrase born of equal parts fascination and unease, and one that has followed Svenu, in one tone or another, through every age since. It was in these same centuries that raiding clans first began wintering over on the Keystone Isles rather than braving the Open Ocean's return crossing twice in one year, a practical accommodation that hardened by slow degrees into settlement outright; marriage and kinship with the islands' own fisherfolk followed, and by the time the Age of Corsairs opened the Rim of Fire to Circulean shipping in earnest, Svenish blood, speech, and marriage-custom had already worked its way into island life deeply enough to persist in the Keystones' rites and reckonings of lineage to this day.

The **Age of Corsairs** saw Svenu's seamanship turn from raiding toward a longer-reaching consequence: it was Svenish captains who first made reliable contact with the Rim of Fire, and Svenish charts and navigational lore that gave the Circulean powers both the incentive and the means to follow where Svenu had already gone. Svenish houses secured trade footholds in the Rim in this era that endure, in one form or another, to the present day.

The **Aetheric Age** brought choir-engines, aetheric alumen, and academy prestige to the Circulean heartland, and brought all three, in turn, before the fyrstinna's reckoning -- which has, on the whole, refused them. Svenu's Rim trade deepened across these centuries into real and quietly held wealth, unevenly distributed between the cosmopolitan, Rim-facing outer coast and the more conservative inner Gulf and highland interior, and it became a recognized pattern for captains and factor-families to return from long years in the Rim carrying foreign habits, foreign goods, and sometimes foreign kin for the fyrstinna to weigh, clan by clan and case by case, rather than for the peninsula to resolve once and settle.

In the **Imperial Age**, Svenu remains outside the ambitions that have remade so much of the Circulean world -- Roburic II's southern campaigns reached Varngard and Karsova, never Svenu itself -- though its Rim of Fire holdings and its captains' skill have not gone unnoticed by the powers now contesting that theater. The full shape of Svenu's place in the present century's gathering storm is a matter for later drafting.

## Names

"Svenish" names the people and the peninsula alike; "Svenic" names their tongue alone, and the two are not interchangeable, no more than a person and their speech are the same thing. Svenic, the tongue of Svenu, retains the thorn and eth that Varngardi's speech has long since worn smooth in the writing, if not always in the saying -- Svenic spelling keeps Þ and ð where a Varngardi scribe would render the same sound in plain letters, and treats y as its own vowel rather than a spelling variant of i, carrying stress and root-meaning on equal footing with a, í, and ú. Aspirated onsets -- hr-, hl-, hn- -- are native and unremarkable to a Svenish ear where Varngardi has long since worn them down to plain r, l, and n. Given names: men called Hnýrvik, Sigvýrð, Þagnarr, Vinnulfr, Freðmar, Korðvaldr, Sæstyn, Ysbrandr; women called Brynhrist, Hmelðr, Yskarðr, Sigrýn, Æsvý, Hryfriðr, Bjargunndr, Ysveig, Ulfrún. (Hmelðr and Yskarðr are also attested, in a Circulean mouth's inevitable smoothing, as Emelda and Iskanda -- the forms by which the same two women are known to history outside Svenu, their names having lost the aspirate and the fricative that a settled Circulean household never learned to say.)

A Svenish name proper runs three parts: the given name, a matronymic drawn from the mother or, where some other ancestor's memory looms larger, from her instead, and last the clan name, which as often as not is simply the clan's own informal name for itself where it keeps a remembered aettling. The matronymic carries no suffix of its own any longer -- the mother's name in the bare genitive is signal enough, a fuller formant once presumed to have marked it having worn away through everyday use until the case ending alone was left to do the work. The genitive is built as -ar, with a bare trailing -r on the mother's own name trimmed first so the case ending does not double against it, and a linking glide bridging any name that ends on a bare vowel; so Sigrýn, daughter of Brynhrist, is styled in full as Sigrýn Brynhristar, of the Ice-Hart's kin, while a daughter of Yskarðr would carry the matronymic Yskarðar -- the mother's name carrying the child's identity forward exactly as descent in Svenu is reckoned to run.

## Open Threads

The terms skipling (ship-bonded) and aettling (crew- or clan-bonded) are freshly proposed in this session and not yet confirmed as settled canon; moling and adaling are the only terms attested prior to this drafting. The endpoint (if any) of the All-Thing's slow formalization is undecided, as is any name for the potlatch-style hosting contest itself beyond the descriptive "reckoning" and the plain "All-Thing." The svartalfr's own relationship to the reckoning -- whether their smithcraft and any foreign contact of their own is subject to the fyrstinna's judgment, or stands wholly apart in their mountain remoteness -- is unaddressed. Svenu's founding grievance, if any, behind its deep distrust of male leadership remains deliberately unexplained per prior decision. The precise texture of Svenu's entanglement in the Great War -- which Rim anchorages sit secure under old custom and which are contested, and the terms of the Commonwealth's courtship of Svenish captains -- is left for the Great War material proper rather than resolved here. Finally, the Dark Ages settlement of the Keystone Isles by Svenish raider-clans -- first as winter quarters, then as residents absorbed by marriage into the islands' fisherfolk -- is now sketched above; its fuller influence on Beniotti's maritime culture and ruling character remains banked as a cross-reference for a future Keystone Isles or Beniotti entry, not detailed here.
