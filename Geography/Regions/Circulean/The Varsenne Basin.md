## Nomenclature

The Varsenne is the name given to the whole basin of the Varsa: the river itself keeps the plainer form, while "Varsenne" is the broader, regional name applied to its entire watershed, covering every people and polity that has ever occupied it. This lorebook uses "the Varsenne Basin," or simply "the Varsenne," as the umbrella term for the region across all its periods, reserving "Lavion" and its adjective "Lavienese" specifically for the feudal kingdom founded by Genovrast "the Great" de Lavion in 998 AR, and for the dynasty, polity, and people bound to it. Lavion is the Varsenne's current and longest-lived political chapter, not a synonym for the basin itself -- a distinction earlier drafts of this lorebook blurred and this one does not.

Within that narrower sense, "Lavion" further specifies one of three overlapping things depending on context: the ancestral holding of House de Lavion, the small ducal seat from which Genovrast rose; the kingdom itself, founded in 998 AR and still ruling the greater part of the basin at the time of writing; or, informally, the whole of the Varsenne as it currently stands under that kingdom's rule. This lorebook avoids the third, loosest usage wherever precision matters, in favor of "the Varsenne Basin" for the region and "Lavion" or "the kingdom" for the polity specifically.

## Geography

The Varsenne Basin is defined by the Varsa, a broad and fertile river whose scale rivals the greatest of the Circulean basin's waterways, and whose floodplain is counted among the richest soils anywhere in the region. It rises in the spine of the western Lupines and runs east, gathering the drainage of that range's southern slopes, until it reaches the White Falls -- the largest waterfall by volume anywhere in Arlos. Above the Falls the Varsa is broad, gentle, and easily navigable for its entire length; below them it turns northeasterly, running down toward the Circle Sea and meeting the Western Gyre by way of a wide estuary on the Circulean southern coast.

The Falls are the basin's great structural fact. A cargo bound from the upper Varsa to the sea, or the reverse, cannot simply ride the river's current the whole way; it must be carried around the drop, first by portage and in later centuries by a system of locks, before continuing. The settlement that grew up to manage this crossing -- Virell -- became in time the largest city on the continent since the Fall of Reme, and, under the current political era, the capital of the kingdom named for the region. Above Virell the river country is a patchwork of grain, orchard, and vineyard; below it, toward the estuary, the land turns to marsh, weir, and fishery before opening onto the Gyre itself.

The basin's climate is not uniform but graded, shaped by both its remove from the Circle Sea's colder southern shore and its long climb from tidewater to mountain spine. Along the coast and the tidal reaches of the estuary, winters run mild and wet while summers stay warm and dry, a climate that has made the lower Varsa's orchards and vineyards some of the richest in the basin. Inland, across the broad grain, orchard, and vineyard country that makes up the greater part of the watershed, the extremes soften into a milder, more evenly-seasoned climate -- warm rather than parching, wet rather than sodden -- and it is this temperate heartland, not the coast, that has fed the basin's cities for centuries. Only as the land begins its long rise toward the western Lupines does the weather turn markedly cooler and wetter still, the foothills and high valleys ringing the basin's western and southern edge holding a climate closer kin to the mountains above them than to the river plain below.

Fish are abundant in the Varsa's reaches at every point along this gradient, the plains along its banks are among the most fertile in the region, and the basin's wealth in grain, wine, and river trade has underwritten the political weight of whatever power has held it since long before Genovrast's day.

Taken as a whole, the Varsa's watershed is the single largest contiguous drainage north of the Lupines -- a basin broad enough that its upper grain country and its estuary marshes can feel, to the people living in each, like different countries entirely, bound together only by the river running between them.

## The People of the Varsenne Basin

### Origins

The people of the Varsenne Basin belong to the broad ethnic envelope historians call Rimecian -- a term that is less a tribal name than a historian's shrug, gathering together the descendants of the old local tribes, ancient Anish littoral colonies, Remean remnants left behind after the Fall, successive waves of Svenish migration over the mountain passes, and Lu-An raiders settled from the southeast, all folded into a single broad stock over the centuries. The same envelope covers Varngard and Karsova as well as the Varsenne and its dependencies, and the citizens of a dozen lesser polities besides, each holding its own dialect, law, and local snobbery inside the wider kinship.

Lurism itself did not arrive in the Varsenne Basin by conquest in the crusading sense, but its spread was no gentle or organic thing either: it was carried in by roughly a century of hard missionary work, militant orders preaching and, where preaching failed, compelling by the sword, moving from valley to valley rather than sweeping the basin at a stroke. Because no single basin-wide authority existed to convert wholesale, the campaign dragged on deep into the Dark Ages, tracking the same fractured warlordism that defined the era -- one petty prince's conversion did nothing to bind his neighbor's -- and it was not until Genovrast's own campaigns, a full century after that missionary work began, that the last holdouts were folded in alongside his political conquest of the basin. Genovrast's Settlement of the Varsa is, in this light, as much the conversion's final chapter as it is the Kingdom of Lavion's founding one.

### Faith Beneath the Faith

Officially, the basin has been proudly and continuously Orthodox since the Settlement of the Varsa, and its people take real pride in five centuries spent as safekeeper of Aramoor. That state religion, though, was laid over a population whose Lurism had been won by generations of missionary militancy rather than by anything resembling the conciliar, localist temper that would later define Concordianism among the Commonwealth's own founders; if the basin's early faith leaned toward any later-named current, it sat far closer to the hard fervor that would eventually crystallize as Antidiscordianism than to any spirit of consensus. What Genovrast tamed at the Settlement of the Varsa, folding the old missionary bands into the budgeted Church Militant and brokering the deliberately conciliatory Third Hierophancy over the top of them, was not a gentle rural custom but a genuinely zealous inheritance -- tamed and institutionalized rather than erased. The realm's later reputation for proud, orthodox devotion is accordingly no thin veneer over indifference; it runs as deep as the conquest that first planted it, and the old fervor it was built from has never been entirely bred out. Whether that vein of zeal shows itself again in how readily the realm's people accepted Roburic II's rupture with Aramoor -- a crown discarding the Church's blessing being received, perhaps, less as heresy than as the same old fervor simply changing its object -- is a thread worth pulling further.

### Physical Character

Rimecians are, by their own historians' admission, extremely variable in appearance -- a natural consequence of an ethnic envelope stitched together from so many contributing strands rather than descended from any single stock. A tawny cast to the skin, dark hair, and hazel or green eyes are common enough to be called typical, set on features that run sharp rather than soft but rounded rather than angular; height across the basin trends toward the medium rather than either extreme. Even within the Varsenne proper the variation runs wide: pale, light-eyed types reflecting old Svenish and Southland ancestry turn up alongside darker, Anish-leaning faces, freckling is more common toward the west and the Lupine foothills, and complexions run darker toward the coasts and trade roads reaching toward Ertra and the Merhal beyond. Lavienese nobles, as a class, are notoriously preoccupied with proving the purity of their own bloodlines -- and notoriously unable to agree among themselves on what a pure Rimecian bloodline would even look like, given how thoroughly the basin's own ancestry defies the exercise.

### Regional Character

The basin's character shifts with the river along its length, much as its economy and settlement pattern do. Above Virell, the grain, orchard, and vineyard country toward the Duchy of Cendrelac and the Lupine foothills keeps the oldest and most conservative temper in the region: farming households closest in daily rhythm to the feudal order the Peerage still nominally represents, their dooryards kept in Yardheeler and Tallowback, their woodland fringes worked for game against wild Brackenfowl and Coppertail. Virell itself and the lock-and-portage corridor around it are the basin's cosmopolitan heart, home to the fairs and bourses that trace back to Héruard's reign and, in the present Imperial Age, to the aethermaton yards and the guild disputes that come with them; the free city's own old habit of chartered self-governance, granted under Genovrast II and expanded under Édmun, gives Virell's townsfolk a genuine and long-standing tradition of civic self-rule that sits apart from rural feudal subjecthood, and the Gutterjacks, Nick o' Silvers, and Tower Rooks that work its rooftops and quays are as much a fixture of the city as its guildhalls. Below Virell, the marsh, weir, and estuary country is the most insular part of the basin, its fisherfolk and weir-workers sharing their reed-beds and mudflats with Canalets, Paddletails, Reed-Stalkers, and Jeweldarts, and holding, by most accounts, the basin's most idiosyncratic and least examined religious practice of all -- furthest as it is from both throne and cathedral alike.

### Language and Legacy

Rimecian speech belongs to a family the Learned call, in their own more technical register, Middle Suurese, though in everyday use across the Varsenne and its dominions the family goes simply by Virellic -- a naming that says something about where the basin's people believe their own tongue is centered. The finer structure of Virellic, its relationship to whatever the Commonwealth speaks, and how far Lavienese itself diverges from the wider family are all matters for later drafting rather than settled here. What can be said now is that the Rimecian legacy to the wider Circulean world runs through statecraft and rite more than through any single invention: feudalism in its Lavienese form, the courtly tradition that grew up around the Peerage, the centralized statecraft Genovrast's line perfected over five centuries, and, in the more technical register, some of the earliest formal codification of aetheric practice anywhere in the West.

## The Kingdom of Lavion

*The following covers the Varsenne's current and longest-standing political era -- the feudal kingdom, later empire, founded by Genovrast "the Great" de Lavion in 998 AR. It is one chapter of the basin's history, not the whole of it.*

### Founding

The Kingdom of Lavion was forged by Genovrast "the Great" de Lavion (r. 998-1022; 970-1022), who by the age of twenty-eight had subdued the last of the petty princelings contesting the Varsa basin and crowned himself king in 998 AR. He did not stop at the river: campaigns carried his peace as far as the Ertrese Peninsula before he turned to the matter of the Church. In that same founding year he entered the basilica on the Varsa with charters in hand, recognizing the Patriarch of the Varsa as Hierophant over all lands under his peace -- and only then knelt, not to be crowned, for he already was king, but to receive the Church's sanction upon a rule he had already seized by the sword. This is remembered as the Settlement of the Varsa, and it is the founding act of both the kingdom and the modern, conciliatory Third Hierophancy.

The Church settlement was an early act of the reign rather than its capstone: Genovrast spent the following twenty-four years still building and still campaigning, marked throughout by fast building and faster war-making, and died in battle in 1022 AR securing the same borders he had spent his reign drawing. He left behind a young dynasty with hard edges and a harder task, one that the next several generations of his line would spend consolidating.

The Duchy of Lavion, from which Genovrast's line and the kingdom both take their name, is held directly by the crown rather than granted out as a subject fief, and remains so to this day. Crucially, it does not include Virell: the capital is counted among the realm's free cities, self-governing in its own civic right and not a ducal possession. The duchy instead takes its name from the much smaller township of Lavion proper, which sits some leagues north of Virell along a minor tributary of the Varsa -- an unassuming market town that gave both the ruling house and the kingdom itself their name, and which has long since been eclipsed in every practical sense by the capital that shares its river but not its title.

### The Crown

Lavion is a feudal monarchy, and the Peerage -- the kingdom's aristocracy, ranked in the familiar gradient of duke, marquis, count, viscount, and baron -- forms the realm's senior chamber: a hereditary body of great dignity and old right, holding much of the realm's practical power in its own hands and deliberating collectively on matters of law, tax, and war in a manner the newer, elected Commons' Chamber cannot match in standing, however loud its debates. Succession to the crown itself runs by male-preference primogeniture, and minorities are common enough in the dynasty's history to have produced a standing vocabulary for them -- Queen Dowager regencies, Marshal-Regencies, and the like -- but a Queen Regnant holding the throne in her own right has happened exactly once in five centuries of the line, when Héruard "the Steady" died without a blood heir and named his niece Lurie-Mavrelle successor over a contested field of rival claims. Her reign is remembered as settled and successful, but it was a testamentary exception rather than an established alternate line of succession, and no Lavienese jurist treats it as having set a standing precedent one way or the other.

Actual governance is concentrated less in the Peerage's collective voice than in the Privy Council, a small standing body of the crown's chosen ministers where most decisions of state are in fact made. The Peerage deliberates and consents; the Council rules.

### Heraldry

The arms of House de Lavion are blazoned gules, a rose or en soleil: a golden rose, rendered radiant with sunburst rays, upon a red field. Reduced to its plainest civilian form -- gold rose, golden rays, red ground -- this device is the "rose-on-red" that Lavienese ships have carried on every sea since Genovrast's own day, still flying from the masts of Edramar II's fleets in the realm's present century.

The full achievement adds a royal helm of gold, crested with a sunburst, and supporters of two royal griffins passant, rendered argent and armed, langued, and winged gules -- their bodies silver-white, claws, tongues, and wings picked out in red. As with any gryph in Arlos, these are living animals rather than heraldic fancy, the same creatures that fill the sky's raptor niches continent-wide, chosen as royal supporters for their bearing rather than invented for the purpose. The mantling falls in ribbons of royal or and sable, gold and black.

Beneath the achievement runs the house motto, I Rise In Splendour -- a line that does double duty, read equally as Genovrast's own boast of a self-made crown and as a plain description of the rose device it accompanies, wreathed as it is in its own sunburst. The solar imagery is doctrinal rather than merely decorative: Genovrast chose it deliberately to echo Lur's Light, the same radiance the Patriarchate of the North's faithful still seek to glimpse in their sun-gazing devotions, and in doing so cast his own house as an unofficial defender of the faith -- a role claimed by implication and heraldry rather than by any charter or title. This stands in deliberate contrast to the Commonwealth's High Principate, whose defense of the Church of the West is explicit and constitutional, written into the office itself; Lavion's kings have never needed the title spelled out, since their arms have made the claim for them since the founding. Later chroniclers have not resisted reading Roburic II's self-crowning at Virell as the house finally living up, in the boldest possible terms, to a claim its own device had been making for five centuries.

### The Commons' Chamber

In 1277 AR, under Wulfric de Lavion (r. 1257-1293), a widespread revolt -- remembered simply as the Revolt of the Commons -- forced the crown to concede a lower house, the Commons' Chamber, seated alongside the Peerage. Its shape was the familiar one such revolts tend to take: a string of bad harvest years had left the countryside and the towns both squeezed, and the crown's demands for tax and muster on top of failed yields proved to be the spark rather than any single grievance of principle. Wulfric is credited with bending rather than breaking: trading a portion of royal prerogative for the promise of steady revenue and quiet streets, he is judged by later chroniclers to have left the crown stronger for being better balanced. Its legislative powers are narrow by design: it can propose, protest, and occasionally delay, but it cannot on its own bind the crown or override the Council. Its true influence runs instead through petition -- the Chamber's principal and most effective tool is the formal petitioning of the Privy Council, which is where Commons pressure, when it succeeds at all, actually translates into policy. What the Chamber lacks in formal power it makes up in temperament: it has a reputation, cultivated and defended by its own members, for a combative and litigious spirit wholly out of proportion to its narrow remit. Lavienese commonly and only half-jokingly compare it to the Commonwealth's Parliament; Commonwealthers, for their part, are quick to insist there is no comparison at all, and that the Chamber's "combative spirit" is what a body without real power inevitably cultivates in its place.

### Faith and the Third Hierophancy

Lurist Orthodoxy has been the state religion of Lavion since the Settlement of the Varsa, and the kingdom has stood as safekeeper of Aramoor -- seat of the Hierophant, and formally an independent, canon-governed city-state within Lavion's own southwestern heartland -- for the roughly five centuries between Genovrast's settlement and its ending under Roburic II "the Iron Crown" (r. 1498-1524).

Roburic II inherited a standing soldiery built by his predecessor Carlorus "the Soldier Scholar" and put it to conquest rather than rest: swift campaigns along the Lu-An and Lu-Pto littoral and across the Lupines into the Swicca basin won him protectorates and tributary ports stretching Lavion's reach far beyond the western Circle Sea. Flush with triumph, he crowned himself Empereur of the Lavienese and Protector of the Circulean at Virell in a ceremony that pointedly excluded Hierophantic blessing. The rupture with Aramoor that followed was decisive and one-sided: the Patriarchate of the Varsa -- the traditional seat of the Hierophant since Genovrast's own day -- was brought under the crown as the Imperial Patriarchate, its Archprelate now appointed and dismissed at the Empereur's pleasure, while the Hierophant himself was forced into an unacknowledged exile on the Keystone Isles. Aramoor, as a city-state, endures, but stripped of the office that had been its reason for primacy; it can protest the nationalization of its old core Patriarchate, but cannot reverse it. Roburic styled himself thereafter as "Crowned Servant of Harmony," a mediator between the Light and the Realm whose authority he claimed flowed from sword and creed alike -- and so, five centuries after Genovrast knelt to receive the Church's sanction, his line's most ambitious heir dispensed with asking for it at all.

### The Imperial Turn

If the Settlement of the Varsa is Lavion's founding bargain -- a sword-won crown kneeling to receive a Church's blessing it did not strictly need -- the Imperial Turn is that bargain's mirror and undoing five centuries later: a crown that no longer asks. The two moments bookend the kingdom's whole history as a Lurist realm, and the road between them runs through the Peerage rather than around it.

The turn begins, in practice, a generation before Roburic II ever wears a crown. When Carlorus "the Soldier Scholar" opens commissions to untitled cadets of good record and sells lesser patents to fund the reform, he is not creating a rival to the old nobility so much as opening a door the nobility itself immediately walks through: the younger sons of Peerage houses, who stand to inherit no seat and no land under primogeniture, find in the officer corps an ambition their birth had otherwise foreclosed. New Gentry patents and noble cadet commissions become, within a single generation, the same career -- so that by Roburic II's own reign, "professional soldier" and "wellborn second son" are very often the same man wearing the same uniform. The Peerage does not lose ground to this new class; it feeds it, and absorbs it back, changed.

This is what lets Roburic II wage his rupture with Aramoor without breaking the nobility as an estate. He does not need to. What he needs is to isolate the minority of Peerage houses whose fortunes are genuinely bound to the old settlement -- an advowson here, a cousin seated as exarch there, lands adjoining Aramoor itself, or simply generations of family identity built on having stood at Genovrast's side in 998. Against these Orthodox holdouts, the fused service aristocracy becomes his leverage rather than his replacement for the Peerage: every great holdout house has its own rising Imperial cadet within it, a younger brother or ambitious nephew who will take the commission, the estate grant carved from the new Swicca protectorates, and the Emperor's favor that the senior line, clinging to Aramoor, will not. The pressure this exerts is domestic before it is political -- a wedge driven through family dinners as much as through Parliament halls -- and it is far more effective for being personal rather than confrontational. No duke need be attainted when his own brother is quietly given every reason to wish he would step aside.

Imperatrice Ardelise's Charter of Service, two reigns later, is the closing move rather than the opening one. Professionalization of arms was settled business by her time; what the Charter does is sever commission and preferment from religious standing specifically, so that an Imperial officer's loyalty runs to the throne alone and owes nothing to Aramoor, Orthodoxy, or the old settlement his great-grandfather's generation had been born into. It is this, rather than the fact of a standing army as such, that draws the sharpest objection from the old nobility in her reign -- the Charter closes off the last argument the Orthodox-loyalist rump had for claiming that service to the crown still implied deference to the Church the crown had once knelt before.

The end state, a generation or two into the Imperial Age, is not a Peerage defeated by a New Gentry, but a Peerage transformed into one largely fused Imperial service aristocracy -- landed, martial, and loyal to Virell rather than to Aramoor -- alongside a shrinking, increasingly ornamental rump of Orthodox-loyalist old houses who kept their seats, their dignity, and steadily less of anything else. The nobility does not lose its relevance in this telling; it simply stops mattering for the reasons it used to, and starts mattering for reasons the crown itself has arranged.

## Sovereigns of the House de Lavion

The reign-list below runs from the founding to the present day. Reign dates precede birth-death dates in each entry. Several early sovereigns are attested only by name and dates in the sources so far assembled; their fuller character is left for later drafting rather than invented here.

1. **Genovrast "the Great" de Lavion (r. 998-1022; 970-1022).** Founder of the realm, marked by fast building and faster campaigning. He died in battle securing those same borders, leaving a young dynasty with hard edges and a harder task.
2. **Édric de Lavion (r. 1030-1058; 1014-1058).** Crowned after the regency of Queen Dowager Aldira, Édric consolidated what his father won but, like his father, died on campaign, leaving his widow Ismara to lead a regency for his heir.
3. **Godwine de Lavion (r. 1067-1099; 1051-1099).**
4. **Oswine de Lavion (r. 1099-1115; 1084-1115).** Young and unseasoned, Oswine entered a crown ringed by seasoned captains and sharper courtiers. A run of poor harvests and southern raiding wore his reign thin, and factional knives flashed in Virell's corridors while outposts were gnawed to splinters.
5. **Roburic "Ironfist" de Lavion (r. 1115-1142; 1079-1142).** Ironfist, uncle of Oswine, gave the realm its first taste of iron order after claiming the crown to prevent upheaval following Oswine's untimely death. The War of the Three Kings (1117-1121) pitted him against the Prince of Cendrelac -- who styled himself the self-declared "King in the South" as leader of a loose anti-Lavion coalition standing, in one form or another, since the resistance to Genovrast's original campaigns -- and against the independent Principe of Balboa, Giancarle di Balboa. Roburic broke the southern coalition in the field, its old alliance collapsing with the defeat of its self-styled king, and Cendrelac was absorbed outright into Lavion as a duchy rather than left as a tributary or vassal state. Balboa, by contrast, was brought in on gentler terms, entering the realm as a privileged Crown Vassal rather than as a prize; by the end of Roburic's reign, Lavion spread from the coast to the mountains, and from Ertra to Stranu. He leaned heavily on the sophistication of the realm's warriors and its orders of chivalry, not least the long-established Order of the Silent String.
6. **Wulfstan de Lavion (r. 1142-1175; 1120-1175).**
7. **Genovrast II "the Young" de Lavion (r. 1175-1196; 1161-1196).** A fourteen-year-old heir under the oversight of the Council of Virell until his sixteenth year, Genovrast II grew into his crown while veterans kept the marches quiet. When his hands were finally free, he granted town charters and market protections that traded garrison costs for civic loyalty, and his small wars were the sort that ended at a table. He died young but left behind towns that could govern themselves and roads that paid for their own repairs.
8. **Cyneric de Lavion (r. 1196-1216; 1176-1216).**
9. **Éthelmound "the Lesser" de Lavion (r. 1216-1224; 1204-1224).** Crowned at twelve under a Marshal-Regency that lasted four years, Éthelmound emerged into rule amid competing court factions. His death at twenty left more questions than answers, proving that a regency can linger even after it is pronounced finished.
10. **Lurvric de Lavion (r. 1224-1257; 1205-1257).** Éthelmound's younger brother, Lurvric steadied the helm with diplomacy and law. He cut trade compacts with Stranu and codified conduct with his Crown Vassal at Balboa, which in turn calmed the Circle Sea's quarrelsome piers and rang in the end of the Age of Corsairs. Under his seal, maritime law began to look like law rather than a list of bribes, and the realm's coin moved with fewer daggers following behind it.
11. **Wulfric de Lavion (r. 1257-1293; 1237-1293).** Wulfric's reign carries the thunderclap of 1277: the Revolt of the Commons and the clear air that followed. Wulfric bent before he broke; by trading a portion of prerogative for the promise of revenues and quiet streets, he left the crown stronger for being better balanced.
12. **Édmun "the Rich" de Lavion (r. 1293-1331; 1269-1331).** Édmun's longer, steadier years saw towns fatten, guilds knit their own law, and the Commons' Chamber grow from emergency measure to fixture. He reworked coinage and weights, patronized practical scholarship at home and the Grand Academy abroad, and proved that a crown can rise by lifting others -- provided its books add up.
13. **Osric de Lavion (r. 1331-1358; 1300-1358).**
14. **Héruard "the Steady" de Lavion (r. 1358-1401; 1328-1401).** Héruard presided over four decades of quiet strength, during which Lavion edged away from mercantilist feudal habits toward early modern commerce. He backed fairs, tolerated bourses, regularized customs, and made it possible for credit to cross a county without needing a convoy. By the end, his peace felt ordinary -- the surest sign of success. Dying without a blood heir, he left a clear preference for his niece Lurie-Mavrelle as successor, trusting stability to outlive him.
15. **Queen Lurie-Mavrelle de Lavion (r. 1401-1469; 1385-1469).** Crowned at sixteen after a hard succession that saw factions contest the will of Héruard, Lurie-Mavrelle governed as the first Queen Regnant of a realm that had known but two Queen Dowagers as regents before her, and found an ally in the Principessa Serenissima of Balboa of her time. The "Lurienne age," more than half a century of steady compromise, drew crown, commons, and church into a workable rhythm, if never quite a tranquil one. She tamed the great marches by charter, balanced the Privy Council against the growing Commons' Chamber, and fostered the learned arts through royal colleges patterned after the Grand Academy and its schools. Her reforms left Lavion civil and solvent, yet her caution bred a generation of younger nobles restless for sharper purpose. When she died, she left a prosperous realm -- and an army that had learned organization better than war.
16. **King Carlorus "the Soldier Scholar" (r. 1469-1498; b. 1438).** Grand-nephew and chosen heir of the childless queen, Carlorus had worn harness before crown. His early years on frontier inspection tours taught him discipline and the worth of precision. He reformed the officer corps, opening commissions to untitled cadets of good record; the sale of lesser patents to fund these reforms birthed the New Gentry, a class of title-holding but land-poor nobles who found advancement only through arms. Under him the Royal Ordinance Corps replaced feudal musters with a permanent soldiery. Yet Carlorus loved order more than conquest; his reign closed with Lavion a barracks polished but at rest, its sword newly honed and waiting.
17. **Empereur Roburic II "the Iron Crown" (r. 1498-1524; 1471-1524).** Roburic II inherited that sword and forged an empire with it. A general before he was a king, and a conqueror before he was an emperor, his rise marked the rebirth of Lavienese ambition; his reign and its rupture with Aramoor are detailed above under Faith and the Third Hierophancy. His armies brought the translupine Southlands to heel, tribute from Ulul-Azek, and dread to the Commonwealth and Vel Kiriya, while foundries and academies flowered under royal command, their inventions marching in step with his troops. Yet his empire, though brilliant, burned hot and brief; exhaustion, intrigue, and the strain of unending war dimmed its splendor. When he died of fever returning from campaign, he left behind a realm richer in glory than peace -- and a throne that now claimed its authority not from the Church, but from itself.
18. **Imperatrice Ardelise de Lavion (r. 1524-1541; 1499-1541).** Daughter of Roburic II, Ardelise saw clearly the cost of her father's victories and strove to reconcile empire with conscience. Her sponsorship of the Charter of Service defined the soldier as servant of the realm rather than of the old feudal order, a gesture that pleased the Commons but nettled the nobles. Industrial patronage flourished under her, but her peace policy faltered when mercantile guilds, fattened on southern trade, demanded new concessions. A failed expedition to Ulul-Azek ended her authority; illness and intrigue finished her.
19. **Empereur Edramar "the Thunderer" (r. 1541-1556; 1509-1556).** Edramar, cousin of the late Imperatrice, had been a soldier for a decade before he became Empereur. He embodied parade-ground precision gilded with operatic pomp. Under him Lavion's standing army dwarfed every levy; uniforms shone like enamel, and the officer corps could read a map as easily as they could quote doctrine. He chartered the Companies Royal, mercantile ventures in the Rim of Fire granted crown monopoly over trade with distant coasts in exchange for a share of the take -- half-merchant, half-naval concerns in their own right, keeping private fleets and private soldiery for their own ventures rather than serving as any arm of the realm's standing army. Against the Commonwealth he waged a paper war of tariffs and spies that remained cold in the Circulean but turned hot in the Rim of Fire, fought through proxy by pitting the Lavienese Companies Royal against the United Chartered Companies of the Commonwealth. Expeditionary corps of the crown's own army, separately, crossed the eastern Lupine passes toward the Hetmanate of Ulul-Azek in Lu-An, while others garrisoned the Southlands in the name of order. He died ignobly of a lung infection, though sentiment in the realm was quick to implicate supposed Commonwealth agents.
20. **Empereur Edramar II (r. 1556- ; b. 1532).** The son who succeeded his father inherits not peace but momentum. The army is the state; industry its choir; faith its drum. Lavion's ships bear the rose-on-red on every sea, and the Principe of Balboa calls himself an ally still, though whispers say otherwise. The Commons murmur of overreach, yet the forges of Virell sing night and day. The century of soldiers has begun, and the next war is on the horizon.

## Character

Lavion's self-image turns on the same figure twice over: a war-leader who made himself a king by the sword, and who -- in the same founding year, though far from his last campaign -- turned just as deliberately to the basilica, securing from the Church the legitimacy that conquest alone could not supply. Genovrast's path from warlord to crowned sovereign is not unique to Lavion -- Varngard's own memory reaches for the same shape in its Yxafyring who rises to acclaim in the field, and Varngardi courts are quick to note that no Lavienese dynasty can dismiss such a rise as mere irregularity without undercutting its own founding myth. What sets Lavion's version apart is the second act: Genovrast did not merely win his crown, he went on to remake the Church that would bless it, bending a fractured post-Interregnum communion into the tidy, conciliatory shape of the Third Hierophancy on his own terms. Lavion is, in its own telling, a realm proud of having made its own king, and prouder still of having made its own primate to crown him -- fertile, well-fed, and confident in a way that river-wealth and a tame Church both tend to produce.

The rose-en-soleil and the house motto I Rise In Splendour are royal heraldry first, but they describe a temperament that runs well past the crown: an ambitious, self-made confidence that the basin's people apply to themselves as readily as to their sovereigns, paired with a real touchiness about being taken less seriously than the Commonwealth despite belonging to the same broad family of western Lurist polities. The Imperial Turn's fusion of New Gentry and noble cadet lines into a single service aristocracy is beginning, a generation on, to change what advancement looks like for common families too, not only for the nobility: a tradesman's or yeoman's son with a good record now has a plausible path into the officer corps that did not meaningfully exist before Carlorus, giving the basin's common people a distinctly martial and institutional route to advancement that has no real equivalent in the Commonwealth's own guild- and charter-company-based mobility.
