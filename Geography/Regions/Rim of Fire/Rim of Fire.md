For hundreds of years, the edges of the map were of little concern to anyone but the most devoted cartographers -- the [[Open Ocean]] seemed a blue wall, curtailing the peoples of the [[Circle Sea|Circulean]] to the gentle waters of that predictable sea, while the ocean made their shallow ships the plaything of the waves. Yet it was always known that beyond the horizon, there lay entire regions where humankind had likewise bloomed, as the Ukili'ata always had a rich oral tradition speaking of their origins: the Rim of Fire.

It became what cartographers drew at the far western edge, in the margin just before they ran out of paper. In reality it is not so much a line at all, but a wide, broken patchwork of islands and shoals, several thousand leagues beyond where the [[Circle Sea#^113153|Suurian Gates]] enclose the Circle Sea. Beyond the Gates the trade winds rule; only after weeks of blue water and long swell do the soundings climb again, and the first volcanic peaks of the Rim rise out of the depths like dark teeth.

The Rim lies atop a fractured seabed where the world's skin is so thin that [[The Six Elements|Fire]] and Stone clash here, and frequently. Nowhere here is there a landmass approaching something like a continent -- the largest islands are no bigger than a single duchy in Lavion, most far smaller, and they all rise as conical volcanoes or shattered calderas, with windward slopes drowned in clouded rainforest, and the leeward side all dry grass and scrub falling to black-sand shores. Between them stretch long gaps of deep ocean; then, suddenly, a shoaling -- a reef-ringed lagoon, a chain of low coral atolls where tide and time wore some earlier mountain away, to leave only its skirts. This pattern repeats across thousands of leagues: deep blue void, a tangle of islands around a shallow platform, then blue again.

Because the Rim is a tug of war between Fire and Stone, the land never quite sits still. Some peaks smoke lightly for generations, shedding ash that keeps their soils young and unnervingly fertile; others sleep for centuries and then wake in a single frightful night of red sky. Lava fields freeze mid-flow into black glass ridges; pumice rafts drift for leagues before crumbling into sand. People remember old catastrophes in place-names and building habits, putting their houses on stilts, building above known flood marks, and -- on larger islands -- constructing sea walls not for the everyday lap of gentle waves but for the long, sly claws of tsunamis. Hot springs and fumarole fields are common enough that some islanders joke that if a pool is not steaming it is only because the local sprites are lazy.

The sea around the Rim does not have the Circulean's tame, circular pulses. Swell trains march in from the west and south, unbroken by continents; the dominant winds blow steady for half the year, then swing in a monsoon-like turnabout so predictably that every pilot along the Rim can recite its signs in their sleep. Storms breed in the warm open reaches between the island groups and run along the arc like a child's hand sweeping along a row of wind charms. In some stretches the cyclone season is a fifth time of year, acknowledged in law and custom; yet in calmer months, the same steady trades and long swell make for splendid voyaging, provided one knows the star paths, the temper of Belana in that season, and the secret signs of cloud, gryph, and water that Rim pilots prize as their inheritance.

Hydrologically, each island or archipelago is its own small world. On the younger islands, knife-back ridges catch the trade wind and wring out rain into a radial fan of short, steep streams that cut deep, fast gorges and then simply stop at the sea, tossing their burden of ash and boulder straight onto the reefs. On older, subsiding islands the streams have gentled into looping brown belts around the remaining high ground, feeding alluvial flats where taro, river-root, and cane thrive. Atoll nations live instead by the thin, floating lens of fresh water that forms in the porous coral beneath each islet. Wells must be shallow, waste carefully sited, as one careless generation can easily salt the prospects of its grandchildren.

The peoples of the Rim are as various as its islands, but strangers can still tell when they have left the Circle Sea and come into another genealogy. Skin runs from warm copper to near black, seldom pale; eyes tend toward the long, soft almond shape that Circulean painters romanticize; hair, perversely to Circulean expectation, is very often light. Ash-blond and sandy copper are common, bronzed and bleached to paler streaks by spray and sun; darker curls occur, but the archetypal Rim sailor has skin like polished wood and hair like dried grass or firelight. The Ukili'ata, who in Lavion are met as tireless pilots and mercenary sailors, are counted among these Rim peoples themselves and claim descent from one of the old “mother islands” of the chain, lost now or simply very far away depending on the one telling the tale. Their light hair and ocean-bred ease are unremarkable here, and only became a subject of comment when those same traits appeared on the quays of [[Principality of Balboa|Balboa]] and [[Stranu]].

The Circle Sea with its generous steadiness teaches people to think in terms of coasts and capes, but the Rim teaches them to think in wakes and waylines. Polities tend to run long and thin: a "realm" is more likely to be a group of harbors and reefs strung out along a sailing route rather than a sizeable chunk of land. Genealogies reckon descent by voyages as much as by parentage; an ancestor is remembered not only as "child of so-and-so" but as "who first crossed to the western shelf" or "who carried our clan from the red mountain to the blue lagoon". A chief may rule only one island, but his or her word carries farther if allied canoe houses will answer the call.

Culturally, three broad patterns recur, repeated and recombined. High-island kingdoms live off their steep rivers and volcanic soils, terracing slopes, diverting streams, and tying their livelihoods tightly to mountain and forest. Lagoon-realm confederations treat the reef rim as both wall and highway, prospering on fish, pearl, and salt, the atoll islets little more than low, tree-pinned caps for water lenses and canoe sheds. In the deep gaps between, true blue-water peoples ply double-hulled voyaging craft that think nothing of a thousand-league reach, provided the season is right and Belana shines true. The Ukili'ata belong to this third habit, which is why they, and a scattering of [[Svenu|Svenish]] madmen who fancied themselves their equal, were the only outsiders habitually seen along the Rim until the last two centuries.

Materially the Rim is poor in bulk metals and stone compared to the continents, rich instead in things that grow or condense there and almost nowhere else: rare resins and oils, brilliant dyestuffs from reef and forest, dense hardwoods veined like smoke, shell and bone and gryph featherwork that Circulean collectors pay for by the chest. The volcanic belts do hold ore, sometimes in spectacular concentrations around old calderas, but working these deposits has always meant making terms with a mountain that can, at intervals, erase the mine and the miners in a single hot evening. For most of history, Rim polities chose to dig lightly and trade cleverly instead.

Gryph life along the Rim is loud and omnipresent. Colorful variants of cliffscreams build their colonies on sea-facing rock and serve fishermen as living charts of where the shoals run. Spraywings and Gyre Runners ride the swell lines out beyond the reefs, and serve as reliable indicators of nearby land. Inland, forest-edge demigryphs and reef-stalking waders keep rodent and crab in constant check, and a visitor learns quickly that anything left unsecured on a veranda will be inspected, tasted, and probably dragged off by a curious beak before morning.

To [[The Light|Lurist]] orthodoxy on the far side of the world, the Rim of Fire long occupied the same place in imagination as antiquity's sorcerer-kings: half legend, half warning. The idea of a whole region of the world where Fire walks so openly and people give it shrines on craters made more cautious preachers mutter about [[Noble Element#^798992|Phlogistophor]]'s wrath and the scars left by Reme. In practice, Rim peoples have usually treated their volcanoes with a mix of piety and pragmatism. Offerings and tabu zones keep the worst sacrilege at bay; water channels and emergency terraces keep the ash where it can do most good and least harm. Local rites often name the Noble Six as an ancestor or an elder sibling, not as a god -- something to be respected, soothed, and worked around, rather than adored. Interestingly, the Rim universally holds [[Noble Element#^467185|Maregina]] in much less acclaim than the Circulean; here, the sea is an unavoidable and at times terrible sovereign, not a love affair. Soil, Stone, and Fire inform more positive cults, though they may at times be unrecognizable to a traveler from the east.

Contact with the Circulean world has come in hesitant pulses rather than a steady tide. In ancient days Reme never pushed far along the Open Ocean; its thalassocracy was content with coasts and the Gyres. The Ukili'ata made the crossing in both directions long before Lavion was a thought, but they guarded the star-paths jealously. Svenish voyages of the Dark Ages reached some of the easternmost groups and came back with tales of “Fire Isles” and boatwrights who sailed by the feel of the sea. Only with the Age of Corsairs and the advent of dependable bluewater hulls did [[Kingdom of Lavion|Lavienese]] and [[Commonwealth of Principalities|Commonwealth]] captains begin to visit on purpose rather than drift there by accident. By the late 15th century, chartered companies were established in many Rim harbors, trading bronze and aetheric toys for spices, fibers, and pilotage, and writing very confident reports to very distant boards that did not have to live with the cyclones.

At the farthest known end of the chain, where the region of fiery plumes peters out, lies [[Hyanatao]], the great high island that Rim pilots treat as the hinge of the west. To Circulean minds it is a realm in its own right, home to Great Clans and careful etiquette. To the islanders scattered along the sea, Hyanatao is just one more knot in the net woven by the Rim of Fire, the place where their voyaging maps end and someone else's begin.
