### History

An-Mirda is among the most ancient of Arlosian cities, revered as the cradle of the Revelation of [[Lur's Light|Lurism]] in -355 AR. It was here, in the midst of plague and famine, that the Prophet raised his voice and the Lore of Light first took shape, uniting a fractured people under the promise of divine harmony. From its temple precincts arose the First [[Hierophancy]], which extended the authority of the Hierophant over the [[Eternal Dominion]]. For nearly seven centuries An-Mirda stood as the beating heart of Lurism, its judges and hierarchs shaping the faith into both a spiritual covenant and a temporal power.

The fall of An-Mirda in 336 AR marked the end of that age. Ejitwe the Thronebreaker and his Starkin host tore down its citadels and shattered the temporal dominion of the Hierophancy. Though rebuilt and reconsecrated in later years, An-Mirda never regained its former political preeminence. Instead it became a holy city, revered for its association with the Revelation, its monuments rising from ruins like prayers cast in stone. Pilgrims have continued to walk its streets for more than fifteen centuries, even as its power has dwindled.

### Geography & Hydrography

An-Mirda lies inland, at the meeting place of rivers descending from the [[Sarm]] mesas and the humid forests of the north. Its climate is heavy with subtropical rains, feeding fertile lowlands and irrigated terraces. Seasonal floods bring silt from the highlands, enriching the soil and sustaining orchards of citrus, groves of palms, and fields of millet and cane. The city was first founded on a rise above the floodplain, its acropolis overlooking both the river-crossings and the wide expanse of verdant plain. To the east, the high mesas cast a long shadow, their wind-carved escarpments forming a natural bulwark and a source of stone for the city’s monumental works.

Water is at once gift and peril. Great canals and cisterns were cut in the earliest days of settlement, ensuring both irrigation and protection from the floods. The River Merida, one of the few deep rivers in the region which skirts the southern edge of the city, remains the lifeline of An-Mirda, its quays lined with steps where pilgrims bathe before ascending to the temple precincts.

### Architecture & Urban Form

An-Mirda is a city of contrasts, where ancient temple-pyramids rise alongside crumbling palaces and narrow, crowded streets. The monumental style of its earliest age -- massive platforms of stone, steep stairways climbing toward sky-facing sanctuaries -- still dominates the skyline. These are joined by the sweeping courtyards and columned halls of later eras, when foreign artisans left their mark. Walls of glazed brick and painted plaster carry stylized images of Light bursting forth from darkness, the earliest representations of the otherwise aniconic faith.

The early pyramids of An-Mirda remain the most iconic symbols of the faith’s first centuries. Their broad staircases rise toward sanctuaries open to the heavens, where the sun itself was honored as the surest sign of Lur’s enduring Light. Ceremonies at dawn and dusk crowned these heights, marking the union of heaven and earth in radiant beams. In later centuries, however, the architectural focus shifted. The cathedrals and chapels of the maturing faith abandoned the singular gaze skyward, and instead expressed harmony through proportion and geometry. Their vaulted interiors and tripartite naves embodied the dogma of Mind, Body, and Soul in sacred unity. In stone tracery, in resonant halls built for chant and procession, the faithful experienced a theology translated into form: not the solitary brilliance of the sun, but the balanced harmony of human devotion.

The urban form remains dense and layered. Markets cluster near the river, noisy with sellers of fruit, woven cloth, and relics for pilgrims. Beyond them lie the residential quarters, a labyrinth of narrow alleys, shaded patios, and communal wells. Rising above all stands the ruins of the Palace of the Hierophant, enclosed within walls as old as the faith itself, and yet brought low by the Sacking of the city in 324 AR. It remains as a remainder of the faith's overreach, despite voices calling for its removal or renewal throughout the centuries. From here, the stony steps up the barren outcropping begin, where the Revelation is said to have been received, and where the pilgrims filter past the Eternal Flame and the masterful lenswork that ensures that during every hour of daylight the light of the sun shines down onto the spot where the Prophet sat.

### The Eternal Dominion

Though the First Hierophancy fell with Ejitwe’s conquest, the Eternal Dominion never entirely relinquished An-Mirda. To this day it is governed not as a worldly capital but as a theocracy, ruled by a council of hierarchs who answer to the Patriarch of the North. The city is one of the few remaining true theocracies in Arlos, a living remnant of an earlier age. Secular law bends before canon law; magistrates serve at the pleasure of priests; guilds and markets pay their tithes not to a crown but to the Basilica.

This enduring authority has lent An-Mirda a peculiar role. It is a place of pilgrimage rather than of power, of memory rather than of conquest. Yet its influence is keenly felt in theology. The Antidiscordianists, with their insistence on excision of all that could corrupt Lur's harmony as the highest form of devotion, hold An-Mirda as their chief city, and from here their teachings hold sway across the Circle Sea. The architecture of An-Mirda is copied in the design of chapels and cathedrals all around the Circulean, its chants echo in village shrines, and its imagery of geometric precision adorns manuscripts even in heathen lands, from [[Svenu]] to [[Vel Kiriya]].

In this way, An-Mirda persists. Once the heart of an empire of faith, it now beats as a relic and a beacon, faded yet luminous, its streets filled with the footsteps of pilgrims who believe that to walk where the Prophet walked is to glimpse the Light anew.
