## Setting and first impressions

Aramoor sits where the Mevrille pours itself into the Upper Varsa, on a low rise of limestone that lifts the city just far enough above the floodplains to feel deliberate. From the east, along the royal road out of [[Virell]], the first sight is not walls but bells: a crown of pale towers and lantern turrets, each hung with bronze that answers the hours. From the river, arriving on a broad-beamed barge, one sees instead the stern geometry of the quays and the great basilica that commands them, its façade like a stone sermon on order and proportion.

Several leagues downstream, the thunder of the White Falls guards the passage to the sea. Deep-keel hulls can come no farther than Virell; their bellies are emptied there and their cargoes lifted onto the river barges that nose their way up to Aramoor with measured strokes. The city is therefore a hinge rather than a mouth: not the marketplace of an empire, but the place where charters are read, tithes are counted, and the [[Church of the Light]] keeps watch over the heartland that feeds [[Kingdom of Lavion|the realm]].

The first impression is of pale stone, water, and restraint. Roofs are tiled rather than shingled, façades limewashed or left bare, and the streets are paved to the width of processions, not armies. On ordinary days the air smells of riverweed, incense, and wet vellum. On feast days one can taste beeswax, brass polish, and pilgrims' dust.

## Rivers, harbor, and airs

The confluence defines the lower city. The Varsa runs fast but steady here in its course due east, constrained between dressed embankments; the Mevrille comes in shallower and more wayward from the north, its inner bank stitched with gardens and wash-stairs. The union of the two is marked by the Bridge of the Creed, a five-arched span running east to west across the mouth of the Mevrille, whose piers are faced with carved geometry: circle, hexagon, triangle, and the dot at their heart. Bargemen swear that if you run beneath all five arches without speaking, the Light will overlook your next lie. The clergy are careful never to comment.

On the western bank of the Mevrille the Sanctuary Quays run in a disciplined line, broken by broad water-stairs where pilgrims and delegations climb straight up from the barges toward the basilica. Warehouses are low and thick-walled, more granary than counting house, their doors stamped with seals of the dioceses and abbeys that store their tithes here. Harbor offices bear both royal and Hierophantic arms: the Crown keeps a discreet customs house, but the harbor masters wear the white sashes of lay brethren over their civic coats.

Across the confluence, downriver, the quays run looser. Here are cooperages, ropewalks, dye sheds, and the fish market that serves the whole upper valley. By custom, this bank lies under the town's lay council rather than the Sanctuary chapter, and the difference shows in the bawdier taverns and the painted signs advertising river pilots, drovers, and cheap beds.

The riverfront also includes the [[Arlosian Aeronautics|aeroship]] harbor, a masked concession to modernity. Aside from the stolid jetties where pontoon craft can alight, a cluster of moorings towers -- not so tall as the basilica but plainly kin to its bell-towers -- lifts rigging and gangways to the level of aeroships unwilling or unable to set down. Choir engines hum in a substructure of ceramic vaults, coupling the heat of carefully watched furnaces to winches and lifts that haul passengers and freight up from the river road. The Church has taken to calling the pylons "sky-stairs", as if to insist they are merely another form of access to the Sanctuary; engineers mutter that the real blessing is that the Hierophant has permitted any engines at all within the city walls.

Airships do not throng Aramoor as they do [[City of Balboa|Balboa proper]] or Virell, but their presence has become routine: slow-bellied liners of the pontoon lines that tie the major ports together disgorge pilgrims, dignified embassy craft idle at the guarded Ambassadors' Quay, and the occasional gondola from Virell in royal colors, on business the chancery will not name.

## Sanctuary and synod hill

Above the quays and the aeroship harbor rises the Sanctuary proper, occupying the crown of the hill like a citadel turned inward. This is the canon-governed heart: an independent city-state on parchment, equal in rank to a barony in Lavion's rolls yet answerable in practice only to the Synod and the foculier.

At its center stands the Basilica of the Varsa, the forward-chapel-turned-cathedral whose stones were first laid in the [[Church of the Light#^97845f|Age of Enlightenment]], when Aramoor was a crusader outpost watching the heathen hinterlands. Its lower walls still show the blunt language of that time: thick bastions, arrow slits bricked up, a west front that leans forward like a shield. Above, later centuries have added tiers of galleries, traceried windows, and a lantern dome that catches the sun even on cold days. The great doors bear the date of [[de Lavion#^071a53|Genovrast]]'s visit, when he laid his packet of charters on the altar and raised the [[Church of the Light#^7a2033|Patriarch of the Varsa]] to the dignity of Hierophant, ending the [[Church of the Light#^3be3ba|Hundred Years' Vacancy]]. The carvings show only the foculier and the creeds of unity, but the spot in the Basilica where the founder of the realm knelt to receive the blessing of the Church is cordoned off with thick ropes of red velvet, a pilgrimage site of the civic kind.

To the north of the basilica sprawls the Palace of the foculier, which despite the name is less a palace than a formerly fortified cloister. Its riverward face towards the Mevrille is a sober curtain of windows and recessed loggias; its courtyards are inward-looking, ringed by colonnades where Exarchs, canonists, and visiting prelates walk in pairs beneath frescoes of [[Church of the Light#^fb2523|Judges]] and saints. Offices line the inner corridors: chancery rooms where scribes sit three to a desk copying decrees; the Hall of Illuminations where new doctrinal reflections are read aloud before they are sent to the Patriarchates; the House of Appeal where supplicants from across the Communion queue beneath lists of cases like passengers in a well-ordered station.

West of the basilica, on slightly lower ground, lies the Synod Quarter. It is a maze of residences and guesthouses, each bearing the arms or geometric badge of some far-flung church: the stepped pyramids of the North, the river glyphs of the [[Riv-î-Tep|Merhal]], the austere circle of the [[Concordianism|Church of the West]], the occasional discreet [[Eastern Rite|Kharunite]] slate that signals a tolerated Kiriyan observer. In their courtyards stand citrus trees in tubs and stone benches worn by centuries of argument. On synod days the bells of the Basilica of the Varsa ring a measured peal, and the streets fill with processions: threads of color and rite that converge on the great west door to become, for a brief moment, a single river of silk and incense.

Aramoor's Orthodoxy is famous for running cool and exacting, tempered by Redemptionist habits in its schools and chaplaincies. One sees this in the Sanctuary hill's daily life: catechism schools where children recite the creed alongside multiplication tables; chaplains of the [[Eternal Dominion|Church Militant]] drilling recruits in both sword and psalm; quiet houses of penance where the doors are never locked, because those who enter are meant to leave again, differently tuned.

## Pilgrims, colleges, and everyday life

Below the Sanctuary walls, wrapping the hill like a second, looser rampart between the docks and the holy center, lies the Pilgrims' Ring. Here the streets slope gently down toward the quays, lined with hospices, confraternity houses, hostels, and the inevitable taverns that have learned to keep one side of the room decorous and the other less so. Establishments advertise themselves not only by signboards but by the dialects on their benches: a Balboan house with card games and loud toasts, a Stranic inn where the talk runs to law and trade, a Rivian hospice whose courtyard fountain is carved like a levee.

On the eves of the [[Calendars of Arlos#^ad4f6c|great feasts]] -- the High Day of the Light, the Night of Harmony, the Ashen Remembrance of Reme -- the Pilgrims' Ring never quite sleeps. Vendors hawk candles, prayer cords, and pocket diagrams of the Six; choristers from distant dioceses rehearse in alleyways; [[Lurist Theological Currents#^92d973|Custopontians]] and members of the Church Militant patrol in pairs, their lanterns hooded, to keep enthusiasm from tipping into riot. After the processions finish their climb toward the basilica, the streets are swept and the city returns to its usual, quieter hum.

To the north of the Sanctuary hill spreads the College Quarter, a district of cloisters, lecture halls, and modest gardens. Aramoor has no Grand Academy, but it maintains several houses of higher learning for theologians, canon lawyers, and aetheric moralists who concern themselves with the ethics of modern craft. Here lies the Grand Archive of the Libraferentians, the High Wardens of the Lucentians convene here, and the main workshop of the Conclave of the Golden Mean experiments with acoustics and light in chapel designs that make doctrine tangible without lapsing into distraction. Lecture bells punctuate the day; students in plain black, grey, or brown gowns spill out into the lanes, carrying slates covered in dozenal numerals and marginalia of saints.

Everywhere, the sense is not of a great engine of commerce, as in Virell, but of a machine of habit and precedent. Copyists' shops do brisk trade in charters and commentaries; notaries sit just inside the doors of their houses with desks facing the street, ready to witness a vow or a will for a few Gros and a blessing. Bakers time their ovens by the basilica bells; workshop masters schedule apprentices' hours around Lurday sermons. Pilgrim money keeps half the city fed, but the true currency is paper and seal.

## Quarters and character

Further north of the Sanctuary, upriver along the Mevrille, lie the Mevrille Fields: market gardens, orchard belts, and smallholdings that blur into the wider countryside. Farm carts crowd the western gate at dawn, bringing vegetables, fruit, and fresh straw for the city's stables. Many of the garden walls hide small, discreet shrines to [[Noble Element#^467185|Maregina]] as Lady of Dew and to [[Noble Element#^7ef156|Kuapakalipa]] as the Seed-Raiser, offerings carefully couched in orthodox language so as not to offend the Lightkeepers' sensibilities. The local clergy, being practical, prefer to read these as metaphors rather than start arguments they cannot finish.

Downriver, past the quays, the old barracks of the crusader age have become the Precinct of the Militant. Here the Church Militant drills under stricter rule than any royal garrison: paved yards painted with geometric drill-diagrams, armories kept in impeccable order, chapels whose walls bear the names of campaigns and, more tellingly, of relief expeditions along pilgrimage routes. What began as forward command for holy war has settled into a disciplined service of wards and waystations; the furor of the faith's crusader times may be a thing of the past, but it can still be glimpsed here.

Further downstream still on the Varsa bank a newer suburb has sprung up along the royal road in the last century: the Crown's Reach. Here live the lay officials who serve as bridge between Aramoor and Virell: royal envoys, Commons' clerks seconded to watch the Church's finances, guild agents who track pilgrim trade. The streets are wider, the houses more balconied, the conversation more openly political. From here, processions from the Crown to the foculier and back again set off in carefully scripted order, their sequence of banners and liveries visibly embodying the concordats that keep Lavion's faith and monarchy in awkward but enduring harmony.

At night Aramoor quiets sooner than Virell. There is river traffic still -- barges slipping through the dark with their lamps hooded, night-soil boats making their patient rounds, the occasional aeroship drifting up with its running lights turned to the river like low stars -- but the city above them withdraws into cloisters and parlors. Lanterns burn in clerks' windows as they balance accounts or draft opinions for the next day's hearings; somewhere in the Basilica a choir rehearses the antiphons for the coming Span; somewhere else a pilgrim, sleepless, counts the slow strokes of a bell until worry yields to prayer or exhaustion.

Aramoor is not a city of sudden revelations. It is a city of accreted habit: crusader camp turned synod seat, fortress-church softened by centuries of charters. If Virell is Lavion's mind at work upon the world, Aramoor is its conscience, endlessly annotating the margins.
