# Lurist Dress: The Abdomen, Legs, and Feet

Scripture says less about the lower body than about any other part of dress, and most of what governs it comes from commentary. The rules below the waist rest on Judge Het-Zaphar's instruction to bind, which the commentaries carried from the hair and the sleeve to the robe; on the ethic of Right Appetites; on the principle of serving and flattering; and on the tradition of what the Prophet wore. Climate does the rest, and it does more here than anywhere else. The account describes dress as it stands in the Aetheric Age, and notes earlier forms where they differ.

## The Sources

### Het-Zaphar and the Girdle

A loose robe at the brazier catches as readily as a loose sleeve, and the commentaries applied Het-Zaphar's ruling to the waist from the first. The robe is girded before the brazier is approached, and the girdle is the binding of the garment itself, worn beside the cords that hold back the sleeves. The vesting of a western cleric runs outward from the skin: loincloth, robe, girdle, tender's cloth, and last the sleeves, taken up as the cleric goes to the flame.

### Right Appetites

Right Appetites is the ethic of the Body, and excess of appetite is what weighs the Soul. The commentators read the girdle about the belly as appetite reined in, and that reading gives the visible girdle its meaning as a display of devotion.

### Serving and Flattering

The commentators' distinction between what serves the body and what flatters it governs the lower body as it governs the face and the torso. Breeches against cold serve. Padding, stiffening, and false shape flatter, and they are the flattering likeness worn over the body.

### What the Prophet Wore

In the canticle of the revelation the Prophet, when he was Enlightened, wore a loincloth, which was the everyday dress of his hot country and nothing more. He walked in rope sandals. Neither tradition states a rule. Like the covered head of the same canticle, they describe what was done and leave the rites to decide what follows, and in both cases an ordinary article of dress acquired its standing solely because the Prophet happened to be wearing it. The northern schools, which hold that the Prophet came to understand he had always been bared to the Light, find the loincloth a telling detail: at the moment of his Enlightenment he was very nearly bare.

A rope sandal is plaited from plant fibre, Soil's own growth, while leather comes from the knacker and the tanner, whose trades stand by the same gate as the fire-trade. The tradition gives rope a quiet precedence over leather in every rite. The devout prefer it, most people wear leather when they can afford to, and nobody is condemned for doing so.

## The Girdle

In vestments the girdle is a broad cloth belt, wound several times about the waist and stiff enough to hold the robe close. In everyday wear it is a simple and sturdy girdle of cloth or plaited cord.

Between the two lies a gradient, and the breadth of a girdle says a good deal about the piety of its wearer, or at least about the piety on display. The gradient is most legible in Orthodox realms. A layman who wears a girdle broad enough to recall a vestment over his street clothes is showing devotion, and because the girdle stands for appetite reined in, the broad girdle binds the belly more visibly than the narrow. The well-fed burgher whose broad girdle is doing a great deal of visible restraining is a standing figure of Orthodox preaching. The North keeps its girdles plain and does not read breadth as devotion. The Concordians fold the gradient into their civic codes, as they fold the length of sleeves.

### The Belly-Band

The band blessed for a pregnancy is the girdle extended to its oldest use. The blessing is archaic and rural. It is kept in pastoral backwaters where the Noble Soil holds far more sway than it does at court or in the inner cities, and it is performed in the parish's consecrated planting, because that is the ground where a rural parish holds its Soil observances. Orthodox clergy tolerate it with the unease they bring to elemental customs generally. A few rural Concordian parishes have it written into their charters as a permitted observance. Women in the cities regard it as something their grandmothers did.

## The Loincloth

The loincloth is a single length of cloth with a loop at one end and a pair of strings at the other. The loop is passed over the head to rest at the neck, the cloth falls down the front and passes between the legs to the back, and the strings are carried round the hips and tied. It was the everyday dress of the Prophet's country, and it has remained so in the hot and humid North and in the similarly tropical Merhal, where it is the ordinary undergarment of everyone and the whole of the working dress of labourers, boatmen, and fishermen in the heat of the day. Nothing about it is remarkable there except that the Prophet wore one.

Men and women wear the same cut. A fisherwoman of the Merhal at work wears the loincloth as a fisherman does, with at most a further string tied beneath the bust, which leaves little to the imagination and troubles nobody on the Merhal waterfront. In more modest realms women augment it with a long band of cloth wound firmly about the chest, and in the North, where the loincloth is worn beneath loose outer garments by everyone, the chest-band is the ordinary undergarment of respectable women.

The faith carried it south, and it remained the undergarment of the whole Communion until the Aetheric Age. When smallclothes of southern cut came into general use, the loincloth fell out of ordinary wear in the cold countries and passed into devotion. In the southern rites it is now worn over ordinary smallclothes on feast days, in penitence, and in mourning, unseen and unchecked, so that the Prophet's everyday garment has become a southerner's private act of piety. A northerner who learns of the practice finds it strange that anyone should consider his underclothes devout.

The rigorous Dozenians wear the loincloth in an archaic form of coarse bast or palm-fibre, the roughest the jungle offers, kept against the skin because the Body must be reined in. The southern rites that keep it for devotion keep it in softer cloth.

## Breeches, Hose, and Trousers

Breeches are the natural layer over the loincloth. In southern Orthodoxy they grew into hose, worn beneath tunic or robe, and into trousers wherever the setting calls for them: in the saddle, in the field, in heavy work, and through the winters of the mountain sees. In the hot North the leg remains bare below a loose lower garment, and a northern visitor in a southern city is recognisable at once by his legs.

Loose clothing is the norm in every rite until the Imperial Age. A loose robe flatters nobody's shape, and before the Imperial Age there was little in Circulean dress for the principle of flattery to catch. When Lavienese fashion turned militaristic, the court took its dress from the soldier: fitted, structured, and padded, with stiffened bodies and false shape. The codpiece came with it, from the soldiery whose dress the court copied, and it is the flattering garment at its least subtle. The North forbids all of it, the Libraferentians denounce it, and the codpiece is the one article of court dress that even Orthodox preachers mock from the pulpit without hesitation. The rest of the Circle Sea, poorer and stretched thin by war, dressed down while Lavion dressed up.

## The Feet

### Thresholds

The North leaves its sandals at the sanctuary door and approaches the brazier barefoot, as the warm countries of the Circle Sea have always done at sacred thresholds. On a feast day the door of a northern sanctuary is lined with rope sandals. Orthodoxy, grown in the cold south, remains shod on stone floors, and a northern pilgrim's first complaint about a southern cathedral is usually the boots.

### The Shrine Garden

The East has the strongest reason of all to go barefoot. The eastern shrine is a garden, and eastern gardens are where the dead are planted. One does not walk shod over one's dead, and eastern congregations remove their shoes at the shrine gate. A western visitor who walks an eastern garden in boots is understood, correctly, to be treading on people.

### The Vigil

The watch kept over the dead from death to pyre must be quiet and unbroken, and the watchers go unshod. In the North the kin walk barefoot from the vigil to the pyre as well. In the south, where the ground is cold, mourners make the same walk in cloth shoes.

### The Rope Sandal

The rope sandal is the ordinary footwear of northern clergy and of most northern laity, suited to the jungle country and worn as the Prophet wore it. Through most of the southern year it is impractical, and the Orthodox keep it for the occasions on which they choose to walk as the Prophet walked. Chief among these is the High Day of the Light at the estival solstice, when the triad of processions tracing the Prophet's path toward enlightenment is walked by southern clergy and devout laity in rope sandals. The Libraferentians observe that the tradition records what the Prophet wore and not when anyone else must wear it, and walk the processions in rope sandals regardless.

A traveller under the escort of the Custopontians wears rope sandals with the pilgrim's hood, and the pair is the recognisable dress of the protected road in the Concordian West. The mendicant Judges of the East wear no other footwear, and walk every circuit in it through desert, highland, and floodplain alike.

Wealthy devout households commission rope sandals of fine fibre and elaborate plaiting, recognisably the Prophet's sandal and plainly not a poor man's, and the East regards them as the western habit of making humility expensive. Worn-out rope sandals are burned on the parish brazier, like worn sanctuary caps and vestments.
