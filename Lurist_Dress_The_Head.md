# Lurist Dress: The Head

Lurist scripture says almost nothing about dress. The rules that govern what a Circulean wears on the head, how the hair is cut and bound, and what may be put on the face all descend from a single narrative canticle and a handful of rulings by the early Judges, none of which was concerned with clothing in the form it came to take. The commentaries built the rest. Because the rites divided after these sources were fixed, every rite holds the same texts and reads them differently, and the head is where those readings are most visible. The account describes dress as it stands in the Aetheric Age, and notes earlier forms where they differ.

## The Sources

### The Covered Head of the Prophet

In the canticle of the revelation, the Prophet covered his head when the Light came upon him. The Verse describes an act and states no rule, and the rites read it three ways.

Orthodoxy reads the act as reverence and imitates it. The covered head is the fitting posture before the Light, and the faithful assume it in any act of devotion, including morning recitation at home where no brazier burns.

The northern schools read the act as a human reaction to the sublime, which the Prophet overcame in the realisation that he was one with the Light. He had been bared to the Light all along, and the covering was a stage he passed through. The North therefore follows the Prophet's insight and not his example, a distinction that governs northern practice wherever a scriptural act can be read as a step on the way to understanding. The same insight governs northern hair: the one who is always bared to the Light should be bare.

The Eastern Rite reads the act through its panentheism. The Light is present in all things, and the Prophet was assuming the ordinary human posture in a world saturated with it. Covering is appropriate everywhere and at all times, and the East has never experienced any tension between piety and the headgear its climates require.

### Het-Zaphar and the Flame

Judge Het-Zaphar, who taught the tending of flame as spiritual practice, instructed the tenders of the brazier to bind and cover the hair, so that nothing of the tender would be given to the fire. The instruction was practical, addressed to people who stood over open flame with long hair. The commentaries extended it to everyone who approaches a brazier and then to everyone in any sanctuary where one burns. It is the root of the covered head in worship across the western rites and the root of every canon on the binding of hair.

### Hadad-Phez and the Open Face

Judge Hadad-Phez ruled in a hard case that the one who comes before the Circle comes with an open face, so that the Circle may see what it hears. The head may be covered; the face may not. From this descends the requirement, held in every rite, that a face be bare at oath, at trial, at the making of a contract, and before anyone exercising judgment.

The earliest commentators read the face to include the brow, and a brow is open only if nothing of the hair falls across it. The clean brow is therefore the oldest hair rule in the faith, fixed before the rites divided, and every rite has carried it forward in its own manner. The North overtook it by shearing the whole head, and reads it as forbidding anything that falls across the brow while admitting what marks it. Orthodoxy keeps it as the first article of its binding canon. The East reads the clean brow as a brow clean of hair, and satisfies it by shaving the hairline and forbidding any fringe.

### Tar-Essari and the Hidden Face

Judge Tar-Essari, in the generation of the tests of orthodoxy, ruled that the one who hides his face has something to hide. The ruling concerned suspects under examination. In the crusading centuries, when Shaped bodies and marked skins were being hunted across the Remean frontier, it became a general suspicion of the veiled face, and a gloss of the same period named the cheeks and the chin as the places where a hidden face hides. The gloss does not name the upper lip.

### The Commentators

Two further rules come from Judges who never ruled on dress at all. Judge Nallume unified the liturgical chant, and the Psalterian commentators observed that a covering which stops the ears prevents the listener from receiving the resonance the chant exists to produce. The one who sings carries the tone within him and needs no open ear to hear it; the one who listens must receive it from without. The fence therefore binds the congregation and not the celebrant. In sanctuary the worshippers bare their ears, and the clergy who lead the chant keep whatever covering their office prescribes. Judge Bar-Samniya first spoke of the harmony of the six, and anything cut to be worn in worship attracts his number.

The commentators on the solar observance ruled that nothing may come between the eye and the Light when the face is turned to the sun. The principle of interposition governs the brimless cap, the shaved brows of the northern rigorist, and everything worn over or upon the eye.

The commentaries distinguish paint that does something for the face from paint that improves it. Kohl against glare, clay or grease against sun and wind, and salve on cracked lips all serve, and are permitted in every rite. Whitening, rouge, and the reshaping of brows and lips make the face a flattering likeness of itself, which is the same lie as the flattering portrait, told on the living subject. Every rite accepts the distinction, and every rite draws the line in a different place.

## Sex and Custom

No source says anything about sex, and the obligations to cover, to bind, and to keep the brow clean bind men and women equally in every rite. Regional custom is layered on top of doctrine and is frequently gendered. The married woman's headscarf of the Anish and Varsan lands, the Southern Reach practice in which the marital covering is worn by whichever spouse married into the other's house, and the civic headgear of the Commonwealth, much worn down by long contact with Svenu, all belong to this layer. The Libraferentians say plainly that the gendered customs are custom and not law. The parishes observe them regardless.

## Clerical Headgear

Orthodox clergy wear a close cap beneath a headscarf. Concordian clergy wear an arrangement of cloth that frames the face and covers the hair, throat, and ears to the jaw. The mendicant Judges of the Eastern Rite wear the full veil of the desert roads, which conceals all of the face but the eyes.

The eastern Judge keeps the veil drawn on the road, where the Salt Road and the desert routes make it a necessity, and keeps it drawn while hearing a case, so that the one who is heard cannot read the one who hears. At judgment the veil is lowered, since Hadad-Phez's ruling that judgment be given openly binds the judge as surely as the judged. No layman may wrap or tie a face-veil in the manner of a Judge. The reservation lies in the fold and the knot and not in the cloth, and a layman may draw a headcloth across his face against sand without offence so long as he does not tie it as a Judge ties it. In the North, a veiled eastern Judge is precisely what Tar-Essari warned of, and eastern Judges travelling west are stopped and questioned for it.

## Lay Covering

### Orthodoxy

In the presence of the brazier the head is covered, which in practice means within any sanctuary, and the reverent reading of the Prophet's covering extends the obligation to devotion at home. Outside worship Orthodoxy requires nothing and regional custom governs.

The standard lay covering is a small cap cut in six gores. The gore count is read at a glance and serves as a mark of confession: a Dozenian cap is cut in twelve, and a cap cut in three is taken for provincial or old-fashioned. Hoods are drawn back and scarves tied behind the ear at the chant, and a worshipper with covered ears is understood to be ill or not listening. At oath, before a magistrate, and at the signing of a contract, any cloth worn across the face against weather is removed as part of the ceremony.

### The North

The North keeps two rules at once. Indoors, in the presence of the brazier, Het-Zaphar governs and the head is covered. At the solar observance the northern reading of the Prophet's insight governs and the head is bared, since the worshipper faces the sun and nothing may stand between them. Northern laity cover to enter the sanctuary and uncover to pray at the hour, and visitors from the south routinely get both wrong.

Northern caps have no brim. Nothing may shade the eyes at the observance, and a brim is a foreign and secular object, removed at the door and regarded in pious households as faintly insolent. The rule costs the North more than it would cost any other rite, since the northern shore is the hottest country the Communion holds, and the pious of the North go brimless under its sun. The face is never veiled for any reason, including weather and illness, and a covered face on a northern road invites questions.

### Harmonism

The Similitudeans, common among the jurists and engineers of Balboa and Ertra, treat the cap as a mnemonic and a discipline, and hold that any modest covering satisfies it. The Aureomedians have produced patterns for correctly proportioned caps that are followed chiefly in the academies. Balboa wears brimmed hats against sun and sea and removes them at the sanctuary door without ceremony, which is why the North regards Balboan piety as a matter of manners.

### The Concordian West

The sanctuary covering is required. Outside the sanctuary, headgear is governed by charter and guild, masters and journeymen, aldermen and burgesses each wearing their prescribed covering, with the sumptuary rules written into a town's charter beside its fee schedules. The codes form a living codex, amended continuously since the founding of the first guilds. The religious and civic rules are not formally distinguished, and the headgear of a Commonwealth town is a readable map of its constitution.

A traveller under the escort of the Custopontians wears the pilgrim's hood, which confers the protection of the Communion on the road; an assault on its wearer is an offence against the Church and not merely against the person. Hoods are issued at the start of a journey and surrendered at its end, and a forged hood is a grave crime. The Vespertinians draw the hood at the evening office, and their lay associates do the same at dusk, so that the hour of vespers is visible in the streets of Stranic towns.

### The Eastern Rite

The eastern shrine is a garden and not a building centred on a brazier, and panentheist teaching weakens the distinction between the sanctuary and everywhere else. Eastern laity cover the head for sun and weather in all places, and observe Het-Zaphar's ruling chiefly where a western-style brazier stands.

#### Opening the Face

Western custom has the lesser uncover the head to the greater: tenant to lord, ward to elder, layman to cleric. That is the upward-only reading of the Six Relations that the Eastern Rite rejects. In the East the head remains covered and the face is opened, and it is opened by both parties to one another, at a greeting of any formality, at a contract, and at the threshold of a host's house. The gesture joins Hadad-Phez's open face to the Kharunite insistence that the Relations run in both directions.

The mechanics follow the country. In the desert the headcloth carries a face-veil, and the face is opened by lowering the veil below the chin; both parties lower together, and whoever lowers second is understood to have been received. In the jungle highlands of the Vel Kiriyan trade cities, where the covering is a broad woven hat over a light head-cloth, the brim is tipped back from the brow so that the whole face is in the open, and the hat stays on. On the floodplains of the Mer, where the cloth is worn light, tied high, and often dampened, the free end that shades the face is drawn aside and tucked. Easterners of each country read the others' versions without difficulty.

Out of the three forms came a single gesture that needs no veil at all. The open palm is swept upward across the face, from the chin to the clean brow, clearing the face as a lowered veil or a tipped brim clears it, and ending with the hand open and empty. It is the standard greeting of the East, made by both parties, in cities and indoors where no face is covered and between people who have never worn a veil in their lives, and it carries the full weight of the act it stands for: whoever makes it has opened his face to the other. As with the veil, the one who answers second is received. A greeting in which only one party makes the gesture is a claim of superiority, and the East knows few graver discourtesies.

Westerners get the gesture wrong in both directions. A western merchant in Vel Kiriya bares his scalp to his factor in full sun, receives nothing in return because the factor has already opened his face, and is thought ridiculous. An eastern merchant in a western city opens his face to a magistrate, waits for the magistrate to do the same, and is thought insolent for not removing his hat. The swept palm itself is taken in the West for a man wiping his brow.

The lay gesture is the everyday form of the Judge's final act. The laity imitate the moment a Judge pronounces, and never the long hours in which he listens.

#### Headcloths of Contract and Plea

A contract of weight, or a sworn brotherhood, is sealed by the exchange of headcloths, and each party wears the other's for the remainder of the day. Merchant practice has produced the contract headcloth, a cloth made for the exchange and never worn before it, and the great houses of the eastern trade exchange rich fabrics in a display that is as much about the houses as about the agreement. The original exchange of the cloth actually worn survives as the archaic and vastly more pious form. It is kept by Judges, highland elders, and sworn brotherhoods, among whom a rich fabric would be an insult, and a merchant who offers his worn cloth in place of a contract cloth is making an extraordinary statement about the weight of what is being agreed.

A plea to a superior is made by laying one's headcloth at the superior's feet. Under Kharunite reciprocity this is a claim and not an abasement, and it carries the tacit expectation that the one pleaded to answers in kind by laying his own headcloth beside it. Once he has, each holds the other's, and the plea has become a bond. A lord who steps over a headcloth laid before him has refused the bond in front of everyone present, and has sinned against the Light as gravely as a liegeman who refuses service.

### Customs Common to All Rites

Watchers at a funerary vigil cover their heads, since a light burns throughout and Het-Zaphar's ruling applies to any tended flame. Worn-out sanctuary caps are burned on the parish brazier and are never cut down for other use. A child receives a first cap on the Day of Names following the age fixed by regional custom, and from that day is answerable in the sanctuary.

## Hair

### The North

Northern hair is short. The common forms are a bowl cut, a close crop, and the fully shorn head, and the most devout reading extends the shearing to the brows and the body. Cheek and chin are shaved.

The shorn head descends from the Church Militant, which cut hair close under helm and mail from the war against Reme onward, for grip, for heat, and against lice. A practical habit of the crusading orders became the mark of the knight-monasteries and then of northern piety generally, and the Prophet's insight gave it its doctrine. The shaved brows follow from the principle of interposition, which the rigorist carries from the cap to the body. The full shaving of the body is the same reading pressed to its end, practised by the most severe Dozenian houses and admired more than imitated.

The beardless face comes from the crusading gloss on Tar-Essari, which names the cheeks and the chin. Its silence on the upper lip is known to every northern soldier, and the moustache is the one growth a pious northern fighting man keeps with a clear conscience. The soldier culture of the North is accordingly heavy with moustaches, and western laity read a moustache as the mark of a soldier. A civilian who grows one is making a statement about himself whether he intends to or not.

Northern clergy keep a broad bare crown and turn it to the sun at the observance. The Dozenians crop the whole head beneath the twelve-gored cap, and their lay associates imitate them.

### Orthodoxy

Lay length is free. Clergy crop at ordination, short enough for the hair to go wholly beneath the cap, and the crown is shaved at the same time and kept so for life. The Orthodox tonsure is a mark nobody sees except at thresholds: at the ordination itself, at the solar observance when an Orthodox priest is in the North, and on the pyre.

Orthodox energy goes into the binding canon. The canon is Dark Ages custom codified by commentary: the ways in which the southern parishes bound their hair in the centuries after the Sack were gathered, glossed, and made law. It opens with the clean brow, so that no fringe or loose lock may fall across the forehead in sanctuary, and proceeds through Het-Zaphar's requirement that hair be bound where flame burns, specifying what counts as bound, which knots and pins satisfy the rule, and how much hair may hang free below the binding. A canon that specifies acceptable bindings in such detail has produced a lay culture of elaborate coiffure built on its letter, most ornate at the Lavienese court.

The standing dispute is whether a sufficiently elaborate binding is itself the covering, since the source instructs the tender both to bind and to cover. The fashionable answer is that it is, which lets a lady satisfy both halves of the rule with her hair alone and dispense with the cap, or reduce it to a ribbon, a pin, and a scrap of lace. The Libraferentians hold that it is not, every Hierophancy has agreed with them, and the court has not altered its habits.

Three rules meet at the sides of the head in sanctuary: the brow must be clean, the hair must be bound, and the ears of the congregation must be bare. Fashionable places answer all three with a single object, the headband or hairband that draws the hair back from the forehead, holds it behind the ears, and leaves brow and ears open together. The headband began as a Concordian affectation of the Dark Ages and was taken up so quickly across the wider Varsenne that it was worn throughout the basin by the time of Genovrast's settlement. At the Lavienese court and in the merchant houses of Balboa such bands are worked in gold thread, enamel, and pearl, and are handed down as heirlooms; in the parishes they are plain cloth or leather. A band worn over hair dressed high is the court's favoured proof that binding is covering, and the object the Libraferentians most dislike.

### The Kharunites

The East kept the Hadad-Phez brow and never had a Church Militant to crop the rest. The forehead and hairline are shaved, no fringe is permitted, and the hair is otherwise worn long and plaited, with the plaits governed by rule.

Nothing is cut on a journey except to keep the forehead clean. The rule descends from the circuit Judges and binds every traveller, so that a caravan hand's hair shows how long he has been out, and a man who goes straight to the barber on his return is announcing that he is home.

Nothing is cut in mourning. The mourner lets the plaits down, and unbound hair beneath the headcloth is the visible sign of bereavement across the whole East. The end of mourning is the rebinding of the plaits, which is done by a kinsman and never by the mourner.

Beards are full and cropped. A long beard reads as devotion, since it resembles the uncut growth of a Judge on circuit, and merchants grow long beards to appear pious and prosperous at once. The Judges find this tiresome and say so.

The Judges take no tonsure. A Judge does not cut his hair while on circuit, the length of it beneath the veil shows how long he has been walking, and when he returns to the shrine he departed from, the whole of it is cut and planted in that shrine's garden. The oldest eastern shrines stand on a great deal of their Judges' hair, and it is said with some pride that their oldest cypresses grew from it. The West keeps no rite for cut hair of any kind.

### The Concordians

The conciliar principle holds that the meaning of scripture emerges from the deliberation of the community, and the councils affirmed only two hair rules as binding everywhere: the clean brow of Hadad-Phez and the binding of hair in the presence of the brazier. Everything beyond those two is set locally, by diocesan synod where the synod has ruled and by town charter where it has not, and it varies from one Commonwealth town to the next. The North regards the result as disorder, Orthodoxy as provincialism, and the Concordians as the proper condition of a faith that trusts its communities.

The civic layer carries the real expectations, and it runs by occupation and rank. Apprentices are cropped for the term of their indenture and let their hair grow at release, so the length of a young person's hair in a Stranic town tells how long ago they became a journeyman. Masters wear what their guild prescribes, which in the older guilds is specific and in the newer ones merely respectable. Magistrates, aldermen, and councillors wear the council beard, kept full and at a set length, the mark of one who sits in deliberation. A woman who holds a magistracy or a guild-eldership wears her hair in the binding the office prescribes for its holders in place of the beard, and the Concordian West is the only rite in which that substitution is written down: the office's hair belongs to the office and not to the sex. The moustache belongs to the town watch, the one armed civic body a Commonwealth town keeps, so that a moustached Stranic man is presumed to be of the watch, formerly of the watch, or giving himself airs. The port trades follow their ships, and Balboan influence brings Orthodox coiffure into the merchant houses of the coast.

The Mendicants, under Restraint and Humility, never cut their own hair. It is cut by whoever offers, when anyone does, and a Mendicant's head shows how much kindness he has received of late. Concordian clergy crop close at profession beneath the cloth.

## The Face and Eyes

### Gaze

Western custom has the lesser lower his eyes before the greater, which is the upward-only reading of the Relations in the register of the eye. Eastern custom holds the gaze level and mutual for as long as the face is open. An eastern merchant who meets a western magistrate's eye and continues to meet it is correct by his own standards and insolent by the magistrate's. The two customs match the head rules exactly: the West bares the head and drops the eyes, and the East opens the face and holds them.

The eastern Judge's veil conceals the eyes while he hears a case, and eastern commentators say plainly that this is its purpose. It is the only case in the East in which a gaze is rightly withheld, and it ends at judgment.

### The Observance

The rule of interposition binds the whole western Communion at the dawn washing and sun-gazing of the High Day of the Light, and the North keeps it at every solar observance of the year. Kohl is washed off, spectacles are removed, and tinted glass is put away. Northern soldiers wear kohl on campaign without any scruple and wash it off for the observance. The elderly and short-sighted of the North pray to a sun they cannot clearly see, and regard this as fitting. Tinted glass, which a Balboan traveller might wear against glare, is not merely removed at a northern sanctuary but is faintly offensive to wear anywhere near one.

### The Ash-Mark

The ash-mark renewed at the Fast of Ash and Patience is liturgical and neither serves nor flatters. In the North it is placed on the brow and is the only mark permitted to rest there. The northern reading of the clean brow forbids anything that falls across it and admits this one thing that marks it, so that for the closing days of the year the brow carries the single mark of the faith that the North will place on a face.

### Paint by Rite

The North permits paint that serves, admits the ash-mark, and forbids all else, including kohl worn for its looks. Antidiscordian preaching treats face paint as the flattering likeness worn in the flesh, and a painted man or woman in a northern town is presumed to be foreign, theatrical, or worse.

Orthodoxy permits a good deal, and the Lavienese court permits more. Paint follows naturally from a culture of elaborate coiffure, and the Orthodox line is drawn at the sanctuary door, where paint is washed off or toned down for worship. The Libraferentians hold that court paint crosses the line in every setting. The court does not agree.

The Concordians leave the matter to the community, and the civic layer sets expectations by office. Magistrates, councillors, and guild-elders sit unpainted as a mark of honest dealing, and paint belongs to the stage, to certain feasts, and to the port trades.

The East reads the clean brow as clean of hair and places no restriction on what is drawn upon it. Kohl and henna are ornament and not flattery in eastern eyes, and are worn freely on the brow, around the eyes, and on the hands. Whitening and rouge are permitted in the household and at festivals and are removed before the open-face greeting of a contract, since a contract is made between faces as they are.

No rite permits paint in mourning.

## Ornament

The gradient of the given form places piercing as disreputable in the North and unremarkable in the ports of the Merhal, and the face is where that gradient bites hardest, since the face cannot be hidden. The North forbids facial ornament of every kind. Orthodoxy permits earrings for both sexes by custom and frowns on piercing elsewhere on the face. The Concordians leave it to the community. The East permits ornament freely within its principle of consent, a ring freely chosen by an adult for his own face being a small matter, and nose rings and brow rings are common in the floodplain towns of the Mer.

### The Sailor's Ring

One piercing is tolerated by every rite and quietly honoured by most. A seaman wears a gold ring in the ear that pays, by long custom, for his pyre if he dies ashore in a foreign port among strangers. Common hands are not carried home, and the ring is how a common hand makes certain he will be burnt and not buried. A port that takes the ring and still refuses the burning has given the recognised insult twice over, and every harbour-master on the Circle Sea knows it. Sailors' rings are blessed at Shore's Blessing in Rainwash along with the sluices and the weirs, the one rite of the year at which the harbour and the pyre meet.

## The Masking Nights

Under Tar-Essari's reading a mask is the extreme of concealment, and it is for that reason that the southern towns suspend the rule once a year. On the seven nights of the Solstice Celebrations that follow the High Day of the Light, the towns of the south go masked through their night markets and fairs, and the ordinary rules of recognition and deference are set aside until the last night is over. The custom is younger than the feast. It arose in the Age of Corsairs, when the ostentatious display of fortunes made at sea met an older strand of southern festivity given to the reversal of the ordinary, and the two together produced a week in which the rich could display themselves without being known and everyone else could behave without being recognised. Balboa carried the masking nights furthest, to a license decried in every other see. The inversion is exact. On the morning of the High Day the whole West turns its bare and washed face to the sun, and for the week of nights that follows it hides that face. The North, which keeps the High Day most severely, regards the masking nights as the least defensible custom in the western year.
