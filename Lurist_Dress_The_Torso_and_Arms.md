# Lurist Dress: The Torso and Arms

The rules governing what a Circulean wears on the body and the arms descend from the same small body of sources as the rules of the head, and chiefly from the same ruling. Judge Het-Zaphar's instruction to bind reaches the sleeve as surely as it reaches the hair, and most of what distinguishes a Lurist vestment from any other robe follows from it. The remainder comes from Hadad-Phez and Tar-Essari, from the commentators' principle of serving and flattering, from the given form, and from the rites' opposed readings of the Six Relations. The account describes dress as it stands in the Aetheric Age, and notes earlier forms where they differ.

## The Sources

### Het-Zaphar and the Bound Sleeve

Het-Zaphar ruled that the tenders of the brazier bind and cover the hair, so that nothing of the tender would be given to the fire. A loose sleeve over open flame is a greater hazard than loose hair, and the commentaries applied the ruling to the arm from the first. The original Lurist vestment has a whole loose sleeve that is bound back when its wearer approaches the brazier. The closed and banded sleeves of later vestments are innovations upon that form.

The ruling says to bind. It says nothing of cloth. Het-Zaphar tended the brazier in the jungle country of the faith's first homeland, and he tended it in linen. The wool rule that governs western vestments is a later fence, raised in the colder southern sees during the Second Hierophancy, where wool was already the ordinary cloth of every household. The southern councils observed that wool smoulders and puts itself out while linen catches and runs, codified what their own clergy already wore, and read the rule back into the Judge. The Similitudeans hold that the rule concerns fire and not sheep, and that a linen proofed against burning satisfies it, and this is not a southern convenience but the original position, which the North has held throughout. The Orthodox dioceses of the hot northern shore vest in proofed linen. The Patriarchate of the North has never worn wool at the brazier, and a northern cleric answers any southern complaint about his linen by observing that the Judge who wrote the rule wore linen too.

One piece of wool is accepted in every rite. The tender's cloth, carried over the forearm for the handling of hot vessels and tools, is the piece that actually touches the heat, and it is woollen everywhere. In the North it is the only wool a cleric wears.

### Hadad-Phez and the Open Hand

The ruling that the one who comes before the Circle comes with an open face extends to the hand. At oath, at trial, and at the making of a contract, the hand is bared, and gloves are removed as part of the ceremony. The eastern greeting of the swept palm ends with the hand open and empty, and a gloved hand cannot complete it honestly.

### Tar-Essari and the Hidden Arm

Wide sleeves conceal, and the ruling that the one who hides his face has something to hide was read in the crusading centuries to reach whatever a sleeve might carry. It was also in the crusading centuries that the forearm became the place where marks of Shaping were sought, and suspects bared it for inspection. Both readings survive in the North.

### Serving and Flattering

The commentators' distinction between paint that serves the face and paint that flatters it transfers to cloth without adjustment. A garment serves when it keeps out cold, protects in work or war, or covers what custom requires. It flatters when it gives the body a shape it does not have, whether through padded shoulders, stiffened bodies, false hips, false bosoms, or false calves. That is the flattering likeness again, worn over the body where paint is worn on the face.

The given form draws the harder line. A garment that shapes the body only while it is worn is reversible and remains a matter of flattery. A garment that reshapes the body permanently moves toward the grave end of the gradient, and the decisive case is the child. The lacing of an adult's torso is vanity. The lacing of a growing child's torso reshapes it for life, and every rite that holds the given form treats it as an alteration of the body whatever the fashion of the day.

### The Light in Cloth

The aniconic restriction concerns the depiction of the Light and not figures in general, so heraldic beasts may be worked on any garment. The Light appears on cloth only through the creed's geometry, the dot, the triangle, the hexagon, and the circle, which read in order as One, Three, Six, and All. They are the signs the crusading fleets carried on their banners in place of any saint, and they are the whole vocabulary of Lurist vestment ornament.

### The Relations on the Arm

The Eastern Rite holds that the Six Relations run in both directions, and the western rites read them as duties owed upward. Where the head shows this difference in the uncovering of the head and the opening of the face, the arm shows it in what is worn at the wrist and on the hand.

## Sleeves and Class

The fire-trade works with bare forearms, because that is how the work at the pyre is done safely, and the bare forearm is part of its stigma. Respectable western dress answers it with long, loose sleeves in which nobody could tend a fire, announcing that the wearer has never done so for pay. Sleeve length grows with rank. Hanging sleeves reach the knee at the Lavienese court, and in the Commonwealth the permitted length is capped by rank in the town charter.

The laity bind their sleeves to handle flame at the High Day of the Light, and Kharunite quarters do the same at New Fire, where the taper passes from hand to hand and every hand that takes it has a bound sleeve. The more magnificent the sleeve, the more its binding is noticed, and a great lady who binds a court sleeve to light a taper is understood to have made an act of humility in public.

A cleric's loose sleeve, bound for the flame and loosed after it, marks one who tends the fire without pay, and occupies the honourable middle between the court sleeve that has never been bound and the fire-trade's bare arm.

## The Northern Forearm

The North wears close sleeves without sleeve pockets, and a wide-sleeved foreigner in a northern town is watched. When called before a magistrate, a northern witness pushes the sleeve to the elbow and bares the forearm without being asked, as suspects once bared it for inspection in the crusading centuries. Few who do it know why. Southern magistrates hearing northern witnesses find the gesture faintly alarming.

## Garments That Flatter

The North forbids flattering garments outright and cuts its clothing plain and close. Orthodoxy leaves flattery to custom. Through most of its history that meant little, since loose robes and tunics flatter nobody's shape, but when Lavienese fashion turned militaristic in the Imperial Age the court built a fitted, structured, and padded fashion upon that license; the canon draws its hard line only at permanent reshaping and at the lacing of children, and the Libraferentians object to everything short of that line as well. The Concordians cap padding and stiffening by rank in the same charters that cap sleeve length. The East reads the matter through consent. An adult who shapes their own body with a garment has done a small thing, and a child laced into shape has suffered compulsion of a body that cannot consent, which is the root of the Remean sin. The East is accordingly the most lenient of the rites on adult fashion and the strictest of all on children.

### Armour

The crusades were fought with bronze. The war-priests went against Reme and into the Wolves' Teeth in bronze scale, and rarely plate, over divided trousers and a short tunic, with bronze helms and the creed's geometry painted on their shields and borne on their banners, and their armour was never shaped to the body. The Remean rank and file fought in laminate corselets of wood, hide, and packed linen bonded under pliant, and Remean officers wore bronze cuirasses cast and chased into an idealised musculature. To the war-priests that was the flattering likeness in metal and a Shaper's vanity at once: a breastplate claiming that the body beneath it had been perfected, worn by the officers of the empire that perfected bodies. The plain Lurist scale was its direct and strict repudiation.

The contrast outlasted the Empire. Northern armour has been plain in every age since, a shaped cuirass is read in the North as Remean in the plainest sense, and a southern nobleman who wears one to a northern ceremony has made an accusation against himself.

## Vestments

### The Common Western Form

Western clergy vest in three layers: a plain undershift, a white robe, and an outer mantle or cloak that is shed or thrown back before the brazier is approached. The robe is white because white is the Church's own colour and because white cloth is the first to show soot. Its cloth follows the wool rule where the wool rule is kept, which is to say in the southern sees; the Orthodox dioceses of the northern shore vest in proofed linen, and the North in plain linen and cotton. Orthodox allegorists read the three layers as Body, Mind, and Soul, and have written at length on which is which. The Libraferentians call this hasty allegory and observe that the layers were settled by tailors.

Rank is shown by the creed. The signs are worked on a band, and a cleric may wear the creed only as far down as his rank allows: a deacon's band bears the dot alone, a priest's the dot and the triangle, and a bishop's runs to the hexagon. The circle, All, is reserved to the Hierophant, and a band bearing all four signs identifies its wearer anywhere in the Communion. Anti-Hierophants of the North have worn the circle as a claim, and nothing in a northern schism is more quickly understood by the laity than a circle worn where Aramoor says none should be.

The tender's cloth lies over the left forearm, folded in the manner and dyed in the colour reserved to the wearer's order. It is put on last, immediately before the sleeves are taken up and the cleric goes to the brazier, so that the order of vesting runs outward and ends at the flame.

Worn-out vestments are burned on the parish brazier and are never cut down for other use.

### Orthodoxy

Orthodox vestments are rich, as befits the heirs of a Theocracy whose altars were gold. The signs are worked in gold thread, mantles are lined, and a cathedral chapter in full vestments is meant to be seen from the back of the nave. The creed band is sewn down the front of the robe.

The original loose sleeve has grown grand. It grew grand at Aramoor through the Dark Ages, and the canon of sleeve-binding, like the canon of the hair, is Dark Ages custom codified by commentary. Vestment sleeves are cut full and long, and a canon of sleeve-binding governs how they are taken up for the brazier, as the binding canon governs the hair: cords crossed over the back and looped over the shoulders, the sleeve gathered and tied at fixed points, with the knots and the colour of the cord proper to each order. The binding of sleeves before the brazier is a slow and deliberate part of the liturgy, performed by a whole chapter in unison, and a prelate whose sleeves are bound by attendants shows his rank as much by the intricacy of the binding as by the sleeve itself.

### The North

The northern vestment is the oldest cut in the Communion and was made for the jungle. It is a long, loose robe of white linen or cotton, ankle-length, light enough for the heat and quick to dry after rain. Over it goes an open cloak, shed before the brazier. The creed's signs are worked in plain thread on a stole that is wrapped across the breast and back, and never sewn down the front as in the southern sees.

The North has made one change upon the ancient form. Its clerical sleeve is gathered permanently into a band above the wrist and never needs binding. The banded sleeve first appeared in the late Age of Enlightenment, and the North adopted it for its clergy during the Dark Ages, for its severity, for its likeness to the working clothes of a soldier, and in deliberate contrast to the ever more elaborate sleeves of Aramoor. Northern preachers present it as the sleeve of a man always ready for the flame. Southern critics present it as the sleeve of a man who has forgotten the difference between a sanctuary and a barracks.

Beneath the vestment, the devout wear the loincloth in its archaic form, woven of coarse bast or palm-fibre and kept against the skin, because the Body must be reined in. The Dozenians never take it off. Other northern clergy wear it through the fasts and at the discretion of their confessors. The southern rites keep the loincloth in softer cloth and regard the bast form as the kind of observance that gives rigour a bad name.

### The Concordian West

Concordian vestments are gowns, and they resemble the gowns of civic office because the Concordians draw no formal line between the two. Synodal office is shown by the cut of the sleeve, on the model of academic degree: its shape, length, and lining are fixed for each office by the synod, and a Concordian procession can be read sleeve by sleeve, as a Commonwealth guild procession can. The sleeves are bound for the brazier after the Orthodox manner, in a plainer form, with fewer cords and simpler knots.

The Concordian clerical headdress, which frames the face and covers the hair, throat, and ears, is continuous with the vestment at the neck, so that head covering and gown read as a single cloth. From the front a Concordian cleric shows only the face and the hands.

### The Eastern Rite

The Judges vest for the road and not for the brazier. Het-Zaphar's ruling binds the East chiefly where a western brazier stands, and the wool rule does not reach the mendicant Judges at all. They walk the desert routes, the highland cities, and the floodplains of the Mer in linen and cotton.

The Judge's robe and veil are dyed a deep colour when new, and they fade with the road, paling over years of sun and dust to grey-blue or dun. The paleness of a Judge's robe shows the length of his service, as his uncut hair shows the length of his present circuit. Worn and faded vestments are more venerated in the East than grand new ones, in keeping with the archaic exchange of worn headcloths being more pious than the exchange of rich ones. Orthodox and Concordian visitors are puzzled by this. Their instinct is that the Church honours the Light by vesting well, and to them an eastern shrine whose oldest Judge presides in something close to a rag looks like poverty or neglect.

A faded robe is never handed on. Each Judge must walk his own circuit and make his own experience of the road, and to wear a robe faded by any road but one's own is to project a false piety. A newly made Judge vests in deep colour and is expected to look new.

The Judge's sign is the road-mark carved on eastern stelae, worn at the breast, with the creed's geometry beneath it.

The Judge's wrist is bare. Every other adult in the East carries threads, but a Judge stands outside the bonds he hears, and ties none and accepts none while he holds his office. It is the only honourable bare wrist in the East.

### Where the Rites Meet

In the paired sees of the Merhal, a Virellite bishop in white, gold signs, and bound sleeves processes beside a Kharunite Judge in faded linen and veil, with a bare wrist and a road-mark at his breast. The joint processions are choreographed so that neither walks behind the other, and the laity watch them the way a city watches a treaty being kept.

## The Fire-Trade

The pyrewrights, ash-sweepers, and bonepickers keep the bare arm, with a rough leather sleeve or a long glove for the work at the pyre. The leather is cut deliberately wider than the work requires, flaring from the elbow, so that nobody can mistake it for the fitted leather a smith wears at his forge. The smiths' guilds insisted on the difference in most cities, since a smith also handles fire for pay and will not be confused with those who tend the dead. The flare has since become the fire-trade's own recognised shape, known in every western city.

The fire-trade tends the pyre under the same ruling that governs the clergy at the brazier, and the leather sleeve and the tender's cloth descend from a single instruction. The resemblance is resented on both sides. The Church reserves the fold and the colour of the tender's cloth, and a fire-trade master who wears a woollen cloth folded in the clerical manner has committed an offence that his own guild will punish before the diocese can.

## Gloves

Gloves come off for oath, trial, and contract, and they are never worn for the eastern greeting. They are required in one place. A fragile gem is damaged by the moisture of a bare hand, and everyone who handles the relic gems wears gloves. The Lucentians' gloved hands are the most visible mark of their office in the galleries, and a Lucentian is never seen ungloved in the presence of a reliquary.

## Threads and Rings

### The Eastern Wrist-Thread

The custom is older than the faith. The binding of threads about the wrist was practised in the eastern countries before Lurism reached them, was folded into accepted folk custom as the faith took hold, and was canonized by the Eastern Rite as the proper keeping of the Relations.

When a relation of weight is formed, whether a pledge, a contract, a wardship, an apprenticeship, or a marriage, each party ties a thread on the other's wrist. The thread is worn until the bond is discharged, and then both parties cut the threads together. Because the tying is mutual, a bond shows on both wrists, and a creditor carries a thread for every debtor as each debtor carries one for him. A merchant whose wrist is heavy with threads is a man of many bonds and is trusted accordingly. A bare wrist on a grown adult suggests someone who has never been bound to anyone, which is not a compliment.

The threads complete what the headcloths begin. The exchange of headcloths seals the moment of an agreement, and the thread records its duration. A plea that became a bond when the superior laid his own headcloth beside the pleader's is followed by threads before the day is out. To cut one's own thread without the other party is repudiation, done publicly and almost always beyond repair. A thread that breaks by accident is retied at once, by the other party if they can be found.

A bond with the dead is not discharged, since the Kharunite dead remain in relation with the living. The thread is not cut at a death. It is worn until it wears through of its own accord, and an old eastern widow carries a frayed thread on her wrist that she will neither replace nor remove.

### The Western Ring

The West reads the Relations upward and uses rings, which are given by the superior and worn by the inferior: the lord's ring on the vassal, the master's ring on the apprentice, the guardian's ring on the ward. The episcopal ring inverts the pattern as the one ring a superior wears so that the inferior may kiss it. The Orthodox wedding gives a single ring, from husband to wife.

### The Concordian Ring

The Concordians have made the western ring into a record of a whole life's relations. Concordian teaching treats Father, Mother, and Child as three mutually constitutive relations and not as a hierarchy, and it holds that a person is drawn toward harmony by the web of relations they stand in. The ring is how that web is worn.

Every Concordian wears at least one ring, the ring of their parents. It is given on the Day of Names and matches the rings the parents exchanged at their wedding, so that a Concordian household wears a set. It is the first ring a Concordian receives and the last removed, and a Concordian hand without it is unthinkable. A foundling or an orphan receives the ring from the parish or from a guardian, which stands in the parents' place, and the parish ring is recognisable by its plainness and by its mark.

Rings are added through life as relations are formed. A marriage adds the exchanged wedding ring. An apprenticeship adds a ring at indenture, fealty adds a ring at the swearing, admission to a guild or a synodal office adds the ring of that body, and every other covenanted relation by which a Concordian builds and maintains their place among the Six Relations adds its own. The Concordian ring is exchanged wherever the relation is covenanted, since Concordian teaching reads no relation as running in one direction only. The master wears a ring for each apprentice he has indentured, the lord a ring for each vassal who has sworn to him, the guild-elder a ring for the body that elected her. A Commonwealth notable's hands are accordingly heavy with rings, and an old master whose fingers have run out wears the overflow strung on a cord at the neck.

A ring is not removed when the relation it records has run its course. The apprentice keeps the master's ring after release, and the master keeps the apprentice's, because a relation that has formed a person remains part of them. The rings therefore make a biography, readable by anyone who knows the marks, and where the eastern thread is a ledger of bonds still open, the Concordian ring is the record of every bond a person has ever held. Only repudiation removes a ring. A broken fealty or a dissolved indenture is ended by returning the ring in public, and a pale band of skin on a Concordian finger is a story everyone in town will already know.

The cord of overflow rings is the origin of the Commonwealth's chains of office. A notable's rings strung at the neck were in time forged together link to link, and every Commonwealther reads a chain of office as what it is: rings of relation made into one cohesive whole. A town's mayoral chain is forged from the rings given to the office by each of the town's guilds and wards. The continuity is the point, and no Commonwealther misses it. An old craftmaster wears the plain rings of the apprentices he has trained strung together at his neck. The High Prince wears a chain richly worked in gold and enamel in which each ring stands for one polity of the Commonwealth. The two are the same object at different scales, and the humblest workshop and the throne of the Commonwealth wear their relations in the same way.

Orthodox observers read the exchanged rings as a small Concordian heresy against the upward order of the Relations. Eastern observers regard them as the one western custom that has understood the Relations, and fault it only for keeping the ring once the bond is discharged.

The North permits rings, since a ring is not a piercing, and restricts them to rings of office and of marriage.

## Mourning Dress

The southern rites mourn in undyed wool, the wool of the pyre-side, undyed because mourning admits no paint and dye is paint for cloth. The North mourns in ash-grey linen, taking the colour of the pyre itself onto the body for the whole of the mourning. The East has no mourning colour. Its funerals are long and loud, the plaits are let down, the thread to the dead stays on the wrist, and those signs suffice.

The sharpest sign of mourning is on the arm. The sleeve is where western dress shows rank and class, and western mourning takes the sleeve away. The rites divide over how far the rule is kept, much as they divide over the gathering of the bones.

The North keeps it entire. Northern mourning linens are sleeveless and loose, cut as an open robe or as a tabard, which the warm climate makes easy to wear through the whole of a mourning. The kin gather at the pyre with tongs, which is tending work, and they do it bare-armed like the fire-trade itself, humbling the whole family to the labour for the length of the rite.

Orthodoxy permits mourning cloth of a more traditional cut, and does so above all in the mountain sees, where a sleeveless robe cannot be worn in the cold. It keeps the spirit of the rule by giving the mourning robe unattached sleeves, tied to one another across the back and never sewn to the robe itself, so that the mourner wears sleeves without the robe having any. At the pyre in Lavion, where the family lifts the first token and leaves the rest to the trade, the right sleeve is untied and set aside for form.

The Commonwealth does not keep the rule. A man is paid to gather, and Concordian mourners' sleeves stay long, loose, and sewn, so that they stand at the pyre in the dress of people who do not tend fires.
