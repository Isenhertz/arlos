## Origins and History
Aethermatons did not arrive as a single invention but as the slow accretion of different technologies. First came **valve code** -- the discovery that an air channel's state between pressurized or vented provided thresholds that were enough to count, to step, to decide. After the 1450s, harbor beacons clicked through fog with simple on-off sequences; tally clocks advanced by puffs timed to a specific rise in pressure; loom shuttles learned to wait for yes-no cues. Lecturers wrapped the practice in neat diagrams, but the workshops already understood: a brass throat cut thus can act like an **AND**, and cut thus for **NOT**, and cut differently yet again for **OR**. Truth became something you could feel against your palm as a push of air.

Then dangerous work and Lavion's wars left many a mangled body in their wake, and surgeons and artificers, desperate to put men back on ladders and women back at benches, braided together pliants that shortened and relaxed under the right pressure. Fitted to braces for knees and hands and shoulders, they turned the compressed air that had powered tools into human grasp and step. At the same time, [[Fulminance|fulminant stacks]] continued to shrink without losing in efficiency, growing small enough to wear under a coat or bolt to a bench. With such a pocket heart to prime a compressor and a net of hoses to carry the pressure to the braids, a limb no longer needed a line from the wall to move.

Sensors followed, first as instruments for people who wanted to know the world and only later as switches for people who wanted to control it. [[Fractalism|Fractalist]] labs -- ever searching for deeper, finer layers of truth in reality -- learned to create membranes thin enough to quiver under a whisper of pressure, then varnished them to react with heat or strain and sat them over capillaries to measure the breeze and the temperature of morning dew. What began as a way to weigh the world and map the flow of elements turned, in the hands of makers, into **whiskers** and **lenses** and **baffles** and **plates**, each tuned to a certain kind of sensory information such that it could tell a cube of ice from an open flame or when a foot was at an edge.

Piece by piece, the parts learned to talk to each other. Valve code expanded to become **fluidic logic** -- little brass mazes where intersecting jets made decisions and bleed delays made time. That logic began to grow by leaps and bounds, particularly once the push of pressure was augmented by the pull of a pumped vacuum, enabling a circuit of self-sealing logic that was particularly good at distinct ON/OFF states where positive pressure itself provided gradients and thresholds.

Prosthetics became **mechano-pneumatic linkages** -- pliant tendons and pistons and cuffed hoses moderated by shuttle valves. Membranes moved from instruments to sensor housings, guiding without a thinking mind behind it. All the while, foremen and quartermasters demanded predictability: not just movement, but movement to order; not just force, but force just so. So a grammar took shape, stripped down to verbs and numbers and useful adjectives, and orders learned to travel in **tactile slates** -- embossed metal plates read by spring-loaded air feelers that opened or shut the tiny pilot lines feeding the gatework beneath. A slate slid into a reader slot; the interpreter hissed and clacked; a **logic lattice** of cams and pawls advanced limbs with intent that came from detents and trapped puffs, not from conscious thought.

Put the heart, the muscles, the senses, the interpreter, and the lattice together, and you had a worker that could lift to a count, follow a groove down an aisle, avoid heat and sudden contact, and halt when the plate was ripped free. It did not tire. It did not learn beyond what its plates and springs permitted. It had no opinion about dark, cold, or overtime. Inventors called them **aethermatons** because the name fit how they looked -- a little sacred, a lot mechanical, humming and hissing softly as if some captured breath lived within. Workshop owners called them "assets" and watched their margins widen. Workers called them "brassbacks" and watched their lifelihoods dry up.

None of it would have spread so fast if the people holding the reins had cared more for the wrights laboring in their yards than the rungs of their career. But charters and companies saw in aethermatons an answer to every line item that made them wince: wages, liability, strike risk, the cost of light and fresh air. A construct took a slate at dawn and never asked for bread. It did not join a mutual; it did not send a delegate to shout at a steward; it did not write its name on a strike bill. If it crushed a hand because wear had filed a STOP nub flat, the blame belonged to "operator error" -- and that operator could be replaced by another who would not complain. Where guilds balked, contracts went to shops that did not; where ordinances required safety headers and checksum ridges on plates, private presses learned to fake the face and skip the ridge. When a city inspector arrived, foremen hid the manipulated slates that omitted the safety header while everyone nodded at the compliance instructions and said the right words to get the necessary seal and writ.

It would be comforting to pretend that all this torque served only cruelty. It did not. The same components did carry hope: A prosthetic elbow might let a mangled mason hold his child again; a stove shutter that sensed heat from a forgotten stovetop might save a tenement from inflagration; an automated dredger could clear a channel even as the storm surge rolled in. But the accounts cannot count hope -- only throughput. So the machines multiplied where people were already the cheapest, and the people who needed bread learned to deal with a new reality: That the jobs the guilds used to issue to working crews might now be taken away by a single aethermaton that didn't complain and didn't sing.

In the streets, the technology bled into policing because once power has a tool, it wants to use it for all its problems. The same reader throats that drove hoists could accept slates with an expanded logic that included IDENTIFY, PRIORITY, and CONTAINMENT bands. A band encoded stride cadence, shoulder breadth, common coat textures; another set how long a chase could run before a kill vent forced a rest; a third defined grips and holds. A properly seated plate turned a brass-backed enforcer into a relentless pursuer -- not sentient, not vengeful, simply obedient to whatever a clerk embossed after hearing a witness guess at height on a rainy night. Rip the plate and the machine sagged as its logic lattice vented to neutral. Chain the plate in place and it would hunt until a spring failed or the slate changed. It is no wonder that the Companies Royal or the Northern Seaboard Port Authority turned to such enforcers; the latter's feared "Thieftakers" were a particularly egregious example of mechanized enforcement not of law, but of company directives.

Society raced to keep up, but could never catch the runaway developments. Still, courts learned to hold slates in evidence; jurors ran fingertips over worn brass; magistrates weighed ridges and stamps; expert witnesses argued hotly about whether a brassback's hesitation at a crucial moment was for a GO misread as PAUSE, or a failure of proper pressure in the actuators. Unions demanded **deadman plates** that required a minder's squeeze on a hose connected to the pneumatics main to keep a construct active; bosses smelled wilful sabotage when constructs went idle in the minder's lunch break. In the margins of all that noise, the machines kept hissing and moving because nothing short of elemental upheaval could have derailed the hunger for just that one livre more.

The century before the 1550s taught Arlos to turn structure into logic and air pressure into muscle analogues, to miniaturize engines into beating hearts and make membranes thin and sensitive enough to let constructs see and touch. The century after learned what it meant to live beside these constructs, things that never thought and never slept, that did not bleed but made bleeding easier to ignore.

## An Engineer's Primer
An aethermaton is a tool taught to breathe. Every shopman worth his oil knows the principle: air under command already does a lot of the work in our shops, and what is work under command but a servant? So we begin with the old **valve logic** -- the shop's name for both the grammar of signals and the analog fluidic logic they enable. In brass and gum we cast our **pin manifold**, a gallery of tiny passages where flowing air meets decision valves: A **keeper valve** remembers a state until told otherwise; a **moment valve** gives a single pulse and goes quiet; a **bleed valve** gives us time, holding charge and letting it sigh away at a known rate. Link enough of these in the manifold and you have a vocabulary of cause and consequence, but the reeds of these valves do not jump like knife switches. They lean, they creep, they know between-states, so we build **hysteresis** -- higher than *this* pressure to say yes, and anything below that to say no -- to make the logic arrive at a decision. This is the "mind" of the machine: thresholds, deadbands, and careful pressure calibration.

Orders come on **instruction slates** -- embossed plates, rods, or keys according to local habit -- and they enter by the **reader slot**. Inside, spring pins find the dots or cavities and open pilot jets that feed the manifold. We call them "breaths" for ease, screw the implications. The **manifold scribe** is the interpreter stage as a whole, a packet of pinwork like a bed of nails that maps the dot bands to valve lines. Out of it comes a clean current of decision into the **logic lattice**, where cams and pawls turn those breaths into stepwise motion. The shop name for what emerges is the **catechism** -- the slate's words, now made into counted acts: LIFT to FOUR, WALK to BEAD, AVOID HEAT, HALT on STRIPE. You can hear the catechism enacted if you listen close -- the hiss, the click, the step, the vent, a whole range of sounds and sighs like a liturgy of motion.

That motion lives in the limbs. We lace **pressure braids** -- braided pliants that shorten under either positive or negative pressure and relax when vented -- into **sinew cordage** along arms and legs. A forearm might carry three sheaves for pull, twist, set, while the thigh will carry heavier pressure cordage in parallel. Positive braids activate quickly and ramp to a high force ceiling, but can overshoot if the governor fails and come with the cost of keeping the pressure up for sustained force; negative braids are quieter, pull only to the limit of available differential -- thus kinder to jars, porcelain, and limbs alike -- but that limit puts a hard cap on their force, and their pneumatic lines must be reinforced against the threat of collapse. One blows seals on failure, the other sucks grit into the valves; learn the benefits and behaviours of either so you know when to use which, and when to pair them.

Regardless of their type, these braids are fed by **muscle valves** independent of the manifold, so the logic never lifts the load directly; it merely opens the right valve at the right time. Remember that braids do not like to be driven hard, so we ramp them and cap their force with diaphragm vents, and vent with grace so a jar remains whole, not in shards. This is what makes a brassback useful in a kitchen or a ward, not just in a yard.

The pulse that feeds everything is the **fulminant engine**, a compact version of the ones that drive vehicles; on constructs meant to function independently of a hose connecting it the pneumatic mains, you'll also find a **compressor** that makes sure the manifold has the pressure it needs to work. Very few independent brassbacks are meant to be active for longer than a workday, and their catechisms will have instructions to fall back to the shop, activating when the total system pressure dips below a certain threshold so they don't fall idle in the field.

Sensing is done by **feelers and baffles**. **Touch whiskers** and **pressure plates** report contact and level of force; **bead readers** in the feet read the raised lines we put on the floor and know from the shape of the beads whether to FOLLOW or STOP. **Heat baffles** sag when the world is hot and lift when it is safe to grip. To see color we fit **oculars** -- simple lenses seated over filter plates that let a catechism decide RED or BLUE without argument. Oculars are not there to dream; they are there to sort sacks marked in dye and to tell a door from a wall. We usually pair them with a low, wide **warm-sight** -- a special array of heat baffle that paints silhouettes so a brassback can find the living among smoke and steam. All these senses rely their findings by feeding back into the pneumatic manifold: a whisker lifts a pin, a baffle sags a flap, an ocular filter warms a pigment cell on a pocket of air and trips a reed. We might breathe without thinking much of it, but to the construct, to breathe is to think. You'll sometimes find a construct following a complex catechism seem to hesitate with a little huff of air -- that's the manifold waiting for the thresholds to reach consensus on the next activation.

Instructions on the slate come in what we call "rails" of priority, and usually there are two of them. The first is the work: all the instructions a construct needs to perform its task. The second is for safety: override lines that may stop the work at once. What you call that second rail depends on the yard -- **safety header**, **guard line**, **mercy band** -- but its purpose is the same everywhere. Though we call it the second rail, instruction usually carry it at the top in practice, and the reader slot on a lawful construct refuses any slate that comes without. Pull the slate entirely and you trip the **rip-stop** -- **rip-vent**, **deadman shutter**, or whatever your shop decides -- that makes the manifest scribe dump the pilot jets so the body sags to a safe posture. Even if a keeper valve holds for a heartbeat longer, the bleed valve will win and let the grip loosen. Be careful here: If a minder allows a damaged header to be scribed into catechism, the law says the resulting accident is his, no matter if the damage came from wear or sabotage. Always check your headers twice.

A typical startup sequence runs so: In the morning, check the seals and filters, then start the engine. You take the day's slate to the reader slot and insert it firmly -- wait for the click that tells you the rip-stop is engaged, that's when you know the slate is seated correctly. Next the manifold scribe engages with a rattle of pinprick clicks, and the pilot breaths hiss as the manifold is primed; the logic lattice arms. From then on, it's valve work: A **moment valve** clocks the count and tips the **step ring** one station forward. A **keeper valve** sets the shoulder, a second sets the elbow, a third the wrist; each is forced to wait upon the last by a little **bleed wait** that ensures the body moves in sequence, not in shudder. A **bead reader** finds the aisle line and whispers left or right to the hips by throttling one side's pressure braids more than the other. A **heat baffle** in the warm-sight fires as the construct threatens to step into a worker, and the **backoff** branch steals the breath from the muscle valves, venting the limbs to force rest and avoid a collision. Pull the slate and the catechism ends in a hiss.

You will service more than you will invent. Learn the **pin manifold** by ear, the hisses and claps that tell you if a valve runs clean or sticky. Fit **flicker mesh** over anything that runs hot from pressure or too cold from evaporation; all sorts of sprites love to gnaw on these edges. Keep a **mist seal** on the intake so fog does not foul the reeds. Watch the **wink flags** -- tiny tell-tales that bob when a branch fires -- and you will read the entire thing as easily as a page. In summer, bleed holds run long in the heat while in winter, the seals tighten; be ready to read the weather like a flightmaster when you perform the day's calibrations. If a **keeper valve** forgets, check its spring. If an ocular errs, wipe the filter plate with vinegar and test against a standard banner.

On the floor you will hear every slang for the **instruction slates** and you should answer to all of them -- plates, writs, keys, rods. The faces differ for every manufacturer, the principle is the same. What the reader slot wants is a clear catechism and a valid authorisation ridge if your magistrates mandate one. Never let a catechism engage without knowing its guard line is intact; never trust a hired plate that arrives from unknown hands, conspicuously polished on the corners; never run a pressure braid dry. If a brassback fails, check the pressure levels first, then ask whether the manifold scribe failed or the slate did. Most "machine errors" are the work of men who wanted a job done faster than the manifold can safely arrive at its decisions.
