## Dark Ages: Lu-Pto surfboards and the long shadow of the Wind Cage

After [[An-Mirda]] was sacked and the [[Church of the Light#^4d3257|Dark Ages]] settled over the Circulean, nobody had the surplus to think about, much less build grand sky-engines. Instead the usual bearers of progress made the first attempts, those bored, clever people trying to die in novel ways.

Along the basalt escarpments and chott rims of [[Lu-Pto]], where dry air come up the cliffs like invisible tide, local tinkerers and oasis youths started skirting these thermals with simple planks and fabric, lying prone and letting the rising gusts carry them a few dozen heart-stopping spans. These first "surfboard" gliders led to a tradition that married pure physics and a death wish to local traditions of venerating the sprites of air and fire that form in the thermals over the silt deserts, basin rims, and cliff faces. A decent enough wing section, some gut instinct about lift, and an invocation of the elements could in fact get you airborne.

The [[How the Wind was Caged|myth of the First-and-Only trying to cage Morwynt in stone and blood]] was already old by this point, and endlessly retold. Anything that looked like binding Air in a vessel rather than riding her in place was soon denounced as "an echo of the follies of the First-and-Only". Lighter-than-air experiments did occur here and there, as smoke balloons, heated bladders, or crude gas cells inflated with swamp gas or other volatile alchemicals. The results tended to be short-lived and occasionally exploded over a village square. Clergy and village elders called it sacrilege and stomped it flat, citing the cautionary tale of the Wind Cage and the Wild Hunt that followed.

The serious business of the age remained maritime. The Age of Corsairs would later explode across the Circle Sea in the 10th to 13th centuries, but during the core Dark Ages, civilisation was busy not dying and re-stitching coastal trade. No one sane was going to attempt putting their grain surplus into a sky experiment when they could invest in a bigger castle.

## 1300s AR: Feast-day balloons and the taming of the taboo

By the 13th century AR, the Golden Age of Aetherics is underway. Cities adopt [[The Breath of the City|compressoriums]], [[Arlosian Engines|choir engines]] evolve from crude acoustic blowers into serious machines, and pneumatics start creeping into civic life.

In that context, the old taboo gets defanged by rebranding flying as a harmless spectacle.

Enter the man-propelled feast-day balloon. Small guilds and confraternities in [[Kingdom of Lavion|Lavion]], [[Principality of Balboa|Balboa]] and other major ports already run air organs and festival machines, so hoisting a wicker basket under a linen envelope filled with warm air or light gas is the next logical stunt. The key social trick is framing the thing as playful rather than as an attempt to "command" the wind. The balloon is tethered over the square, goes up a few dozen poles, and comes straight back down. No one is steering Morwynt; they are, ostensibly, honouring her humour and marvelling at the world's harmony.

Technologically, these 1300s balloons are still crude. Fabric is waxed or oil-treated canvas; gas is either hot air or a mildly buoyant gas boiled off from spirit distillation. Safety is equally primitive: redundant tether lines, ballast buckets, a few half-understood valves. But the social effect is huge. After a generation of feast days where children and minor dignitaries ascend without the world ending, a whole generation grows up with the idea that human bodies in the sky are not automatically a blasphemy.

But those feast-day rigs are also a laboratory. They let artisans watch how envelopes deform under gusts, how ballast and wind interact, and how little control you actually have without some sort of directed thrust. They also convince people that if you want air travel to be anything but a parish fair trick, you will need something more disciplined than a bunch of sweating apprentices at the winch.

## 1320–1360 AR: Proto-dirigibles and the birth of dynamic lift

Right on schedule, aetheric engineering throws more fuel on the fire. In the late 13th and early 14th centuries, Academy savants close their thermoacoustic pipes into rings, insert regenerators and heat exchangers, and produce proper traveling-wave choir engines: compact, reliable acoustic heat engines that can feed steady power into pistons, fans, and screws.

Someone, inevitably, bolts one to a gas bag.

The first proto-dirigibles are essentially elongated feast-day balloons with a gondola that carries a small choir engine driving a propeller. They are baggy, draggy, and structurally terrified of their own size. Envelopes are patchwork; frames are rope, cane, and light timber tripods. They fly low and slow, mostly along river valleys and coasts, so a crash means a dunking rather than a crater.

The breakthroughs in this period are not glamorous but vital: pilots and designers notice that when you move an elongated hull forward, it stops wallowing and starts riding on its own airflow. Fins and rudders suddenly have authority; a slightly cambered envelope or hull yields measurable dynamic lift, taking some of the load off the gas. This is not yet formalised, but it is good engineering instinct, backed by experience on the water with keels and hulls. Similarly, drawing on centuries of sail lore, riggers treat the envelope and its fins like a vertical rig, such that trimming lines, reefable stabiliser surfaces, and catenary suspensions appear. From the musical tradition of organ-building comes experience with reliable valve arrays, which is exactly what you need to throttle choir engines and manage ballonet pressure safely.

Though these craft are mostly experimental and prestige items -- observation platforms in sieges, naval scouts for wealthy ports, or mobile pulpits for particularly theatrical bishops -- they prove that you can steer in three dimensions. Still, they are fragile, fuel-hungry, and terrifying in bad weather.

## 1360–1390 AR: From toys to tools -- practical semi-rigids

By the mid-14th century, a lot of things snap into place at once.

First, self-harmonising resonance design is formalised. Choir engines stop being temperamental hand-tuned brutes and become predictable, modular units. Closed-cycle regenerators come into their own as smoothers and energy stores, and engines in general shrink enough to fit launches and riverboats.

Second, materials improve. Alchemically treated fabrics resist rot and gas leak; early aluminum and steel from arc-furnaces, though still rare, supply ribs, tension members, and critical fittings for airship frames. [[Virell]]'s foundries and [[City of Balboa|Balboa]]'s arsenale begin producing standardised components that can survive long service and sea air without falling to pieces.

The semi-rigid era is born. Instead of floppy bags, you get hulls where a light keel-girder and partial frame carry the loads, with the envelope tensioned around them. Internal ballonets managed by organ-style valves allow on-the-fly trim without dumping precious lifting gas every time. Rope and cane give way to latticed bronze and rare steel.

Operationally, things move from circus tricks to scheduled service. The first stable routes are short: riverine couriers between Virell and its up-river dependencies, coastal runs along Ertra, tactical scouts for fleets. Then the obvious happens: the Circle Sea already runs on predictable Gyres; if you fly above the same Rim routes, you can piggy-back on the same weather and logistics. Airship routes start to mirror sea lanes, treating harbors as both quays and splash-down ponds.

By the end of this period you can plausibly book passage on such a craft if you are rich, important, or very lucky. They remain expensive, maintenance-heavy, and not entirely trusted, but they are no longer novelties.

## 1410–1450 AR: The Aeronave Age and the first heavier-than-air temptations

^2ab1b6

Late 14th to early 15th century is when engines have shrunk, public mains are commonplace, and pontoon airship liners now link major ports.

Architecturally, the early gas bags have completed their transition into "ship of the sky". Rigid-frame or semi-rigid hulls now routinely carry multiple gas cells in a structured envelope, and below hangs not a flimsy basket but a proper keeled and enclosed gondola with pontoons, letting them dock comfortably at jetties on water rather than precipitously at a pylon raised into the sky. Balboa and Virell extend their harbor logic vertically, raising mooring towers and sky beacons over quays, integrating the landing, loading, and launching of these "aeronaves" into the same carefully choreographed traffic systems that already govern their basins and canals.

Wing-assisted aerostat pontooners appear as the grand liners of the age. Their gas lift carries perhaps two-thirds of their weight; the rest comes from deep, stubby wings and control surfaces that generate dynamic lift once the ship is moving, as well as gentle negative modulation of Attraction. This allows them to run with smaller gas volumes and lower risk of catastrophic loss, while still being able to alight on the sea like a very dignified waterfowl. Their choir engines and regenerators sit deep in the hulls, humming away to drive props and dynamos for onboard power.

Alongside these come specialised types like the big aeronave freighters. These are essentially catamaran hulls slung under the same gas cells as with any other pontooner, but with a large roll-on space between the keels that is optimised for hauling bulk freight. They shine over relatively short aerial hops where overland routes are truly dreadful, in places like [[An-Suur]]'s tangled coast or the approaches to [[Vel Kiriya]], where rivers, deltas, and jungle make overland transport an exercise in masochism.

Another specialized aeronave comes on the heels of the first experiments with shifting the gas cells from the envelope into foil-shaped lifting surfaces, leading to pressurised stressed-skin spars with integrated propellers that can tilt up or forward. These small, high-performance tiltwing couriers can lift cleanly in cramped waters and courtyards, achieving near-vertical take-off and courteous, step-free alightings where larger craft skip and slam, perfect for running high-priority parcels and passengers between capitals, hopping from pond to tower to pond. As this type of aeronave matures, the pressurised spars remain for poise and descent control, but in cruise most lift is earned aerodynamically, the gas within the wings serving as quiet insurance rather than the mainstay -- a first herald of the shape of things to come.

Because at the same time, early experiments on true heavier-than-air craft are taking place at the [[Grand Academy]] and a few wealthy lunatics' private yards. True to the Circulean tradition of looking at things from a decidedly maritime perspective, these experiments often take the shape of tailless lifting bodies formed like flying boats. These first true "aerodynes" are technically capable of flight but barely useful: underpowered, temperamental, and heavily reliant on aetheric modulation of Attraction and Cohesion to be remotely safe. Contemporary descriptions explicitly call them "experimental curiosities", and that is generous.

On the cultural side, air travel is prestigious but no longer exotic. The large pontoon liners are the marvels of the age, booked by nobles, merchants, clergy, and pilgrim groups. Ordinary people glimpse them overhead or see them docked in the bay, the way 19th-century peasants might see a railway train and dream of taking a trip on one of them. Timetables exist, mishap inquests are reported, and engineers argue in journals about optimal lift-to-drag ratios like particularly boring gladiators.

## 1480s AR: Lifting-body boats, citadel rotostats, and the glider comes back with a knife

By the later 15th century, the Imperial Age is tightening its grip. Lavion has professionalised its soldiery and begun to think in terms of theatre-scale logistics that include air, not just sea and river. Other realms -- the [[Commonwealth of Principalities|Commonwealth]], Vel Kiriya, [[Varngard]] -- are following suit.

On the technical side, aetheric engineering matures enough that Attraction can be modulated safely at small scales, letting structures "cheat" their apparent weight without completely thumbing their nose at ordinary physics. At the same time structural materials improve, leading to better pliants, more aluminum, and lattice designs for making girders that are both light and strong. Lastly, the experiences of the preceding decades have led to a wealth of accumulated aerodynamic data that finally coalesces into a more formal understanding of lift and control.

The result are heavier-than-air aerodynes in practical use that need no gas cells at all. Built as catamaran-shaped tripod with a lifting-body hull that blends into wings terminating in floats, they take off from water with props for thrust, and a geodesic tractite structure designed to spread loads and resist flexion. True to the engineering paradigms of their day, they still steal a little from the weak elemental relations -- not flagrantly, just a marginal bias here, some subtle cheats there -- and many keep an internal gas cell for emergencies in case of catastrophic loss of lift. To the man on the street, they are ships that happen to fly. To theologians, they are a headache that can just about be classified as "lawful exploitation of lawful properties" rather than "trying to shackle Morwynt again".

Parallel to this, the "citadel" rotostat appears. These are immense hybrid craft, descendants of the pontooners engineered for persistent loiter rather than smooth cruise: rigid hulls of cathedral size, carrying multiple gas cells and rotors, with choir engines sufficient to keep them moving and maneuverable even in rough air. They function as aerial fortresses, command posts, and siege platforms, able to remain above a theatre for days, project arcannon fire, and deliver troops or supplies where roads do not exist. In keeping with the Circle Sea ethos, they still think in terms of basins and gyres, and often park above key river confluences, straits, and caravan nodes, acting as visible embodiments of imperial reach.

But you cannot have flying citadels without people to fight from and against them. The need for specialised aerial infantry re-ignites interest in personal gliders. The Lu-Pto "surfboard" glider tradition is rediscovered and aggressively refined: lighter materials, better airfoils, and a more consistent hexcraft approach rather than a trust of fickle sprites turn them from daredevil toys into insertion tools. Troops train to drop from carriers, ride thermals or tow-lines, and land within a few dozen paces of a target. In rugged or urban terrain where an aeronave cannot safely touch down, glider troops become the knife that the big sky-ship delivers.

Oasis and desert tribes, who never abandoned their own glider practices, suddenly find their ancient sports and hunting tools being studied by Academy tacticians and copied in Lavienese and Velkirian workshops. It is all very flattering, right up until the moment imperial troops arrive with improved versions and markedly fewer scruples.
