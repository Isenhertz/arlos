# On the Materials of Arlos

## The Shape of the Problem

Arlosian material science is governed by four facts, none of them a matter of ingenuity, and every substance the Circulean world ever worked is an answer to some arrangement of them.

The first is the scarcity of iron. Workable ore occurs in quantity in only a handful of places known to Circulean commerce: the black volcanic sands of Ertra's coasts and the shores of Lu-An and Lu-Pto where they land across the Eastern Gyre, and the bog iron of Varngard's marsh country, drawn from the soil and renewed by the slow chemistry of its waters. Everything else is incident and exception, enough to make a man rich but never enough to found an industry upon. Iron and steel could be made from antiquity onward and could simply never be made cheaply. They were never, at any point in the record, metals to waste.

The second is the arithmetic of the woodlot. The furnaces of Arlos burn what the forests give, chiefly charcoal and the resinous saps and spirits of the timber trade, and these renew only at a tree's pace. No burning stone or earth-oil has ever been found. The size of a realm's metal industry is therefore bounded not by its ore but by its acreage of managed woodland, and the price of every worked material in the basin has always risen and fallen with the price of charcoal. Smelting and shipbuilding are competitors for one resource, and always were.

The third is that nothing may come to the flame already wedded. A fuel that carries within itself everything it needs to burn is a standing concentration of Fire's character, and such a body attracts a notice that no craftsman wants. The rule is old, universally observed, and rarely stated outright, since it is simply what everyone does. Its consequences are enormous and largely invisible to those living inside them. There is no prepared explosive anywhere in the record and no word for the idea. There is no gas main in any city on Arlos and never will be, since a leaking main is a prepared mixture the size of a district. And there is no engine anywhere that flashes a wedded charge inside a closed chamber, because such a machine is a lure with a crankshaft. What remains licit is heat applied from without to a substance that meets its air only at the burner, one instant's worth at a time, and the whole lineage of Arlosian engine-craft descends from that permission.

The fourth is that nothing is harder than bronze. This sounds like a crippling limit and is not, because a cutting tool need only be harder than what it cuts, and a world without cheap steel is also a world with almost nothing steel-hard in it to be cut. Bronze, brass, timber, horn, hide, and the pliants make up the whole workpiece inventory of Circulean industry, and a properly composed and properly hardened bronze cuts all of them. What the limit costs is time, and the shops of every age are organised around paying it. Where an edge harder than bronze is genuinely required, the answer is not a better metal but a mineral: a stone set in a bronze body, or loose grit carried on a bronze lap.

To these four a fifth may be added, which is not a constraint but a resource. The Aether is workable, and from the thirteenth century onward heat, reduction, and consolidation can be had without any fire at all. This does not lift the woodlot's arithmetic so much as move it, transferring the cost of high-temperature work from the forest to the guild roll and from the fuel-yard to the licensing office. It is a different scarcity with different politics, and it reshapes the material record more thoroughly than any single substance ever did.

## The Vocabulary of Pliants

The word covers everything the modern trades classify as a pliant, and the classification itself is late, formalised only in the Aetheric Age and applied backward across two millennia of craft that never used it. It is set out here at the start because without it the earlier ages read as a list of unrelated recipes.

A pliant is any body that can be brought to a workable state and then made to hold a form. The theory that explains this need not be correct to be productive, and the account the trades settled on runs by way of the Bonds. A pliant is a body of long grain, its Cohesion running lengthwise while its Attraction to neighbouring bodies stays weak. Working a pliant means loosening the grain by heat, solvent, or beating, placing it, and then striking it so that the grain knits and will not loosen again.

Two axes follow, and the guild vocabulary carries both. Soft and hard describe the struck state: soft pliants keep their give, as gums, cured latex, waxes, and gummed cloth do, while hard pliants strike to rigidity, as resins, moulded shellac, laminate board, and pressed horn do. Quick and dead describe whether the striking can be undone. A quick pliant softens again to heat and can be reworked, scrapped, and recast. A dead pliant is struck once and never returns, and burning is the only way out of it. Dead pliants are stronger, more tolerant of heat, and wasteful of stock, and the whole industrial arc of the later ages is the story of learning to strike things dead on purpose and at scale.

A third axis arrives only with the engines, and belongs to a civilisation whose machines work by holding a deliberate tone. A pliant has a voice, and a stock is graded for it as carefully as for hardness. Materials that damp are wanted at every joint that must stay silent, and materials that carry a tone cleanly are wanted at the few places where a machine is meant to speak. A journeyman's examination in any pliant trade consists largely of being handed unlabelled samples and asked to place them on all three axes at once.

## Prehistory

### The State of the Art

Metal enters the record as a curiosity of the riverbed. Native copper is hammered cold into ornament and small tools, gold worked for its own sake, and the occasional fallen iron is treated as a prodigy and never as a material. Everything that does real work is stone, bone, horn, hide, sinew, timber, and gum.

The adhesive crafts are already old and already good. Hide and sinew boiled to glue, fish skins and swim bladders to a finer grade, tars cooked from bark and gums bled from standing trees for hafting and caulking, beeswax and tallow for dressing. None of it survives damp, and none of it needs to, because none of it is asked to.

Two peoples arrive independently at the insight that governs the next twenty centuries, and arrive at it from opposite directions with nothing in common but the idea.

In the eastern lands of the Circle Sea it is the flatbow. A wide limb working in bending puts the quarrel between the tension face and the compression face across a broad glue line instead of concentrating it at a point, and the bowyer who backs such a limb with sinew and bellies it with horn has built the first true engineered composite: one material carrying tension, another carrying compression, and an adhesive managing the shear between them. The trade's proverb, that the glue line is the weapon, is older than any polity that later repeats it, and every subsequent Circulean insight about lamination is a restatement of it.

In the west the Ukili'ata reach the same principle without any glue at all. Their hulls are lashed at every joint because a lashed joint gives where a bolted one tears out, and their hydrofoil struts are built up of laminated timber tuned to flex within limits and return. They come to it through cordage, wet timber, and the behaviour of a multihull in a seaway, and they express it as a matter of tune. Neither tradition knows the other exists, and when the two vocabularies finally meet in a Circulean shipyard some three thousand years later, neither trade can quite believe the other got there first.

Horn is meanwhile worked as a quick hard pliant by anyone who has horn: split, softened in hot water, flattened between heated stones into panes and cups and combs. Nobody calls it a pliant, and the tooling is already the tooling of a moulding shop.

### What Changed

Copper is alloyed with tin, and the crucible enters the world. The consequences are so large and so gradual that no age of the record contains them.

The whale fisheries of the Circle Sea open along every coast that has a beach and a boat, and with them the material that will matter most to the ordinary machinery of the next two thousand years. The plates of the baleen whale are read at once as horn of the sea, and the horners take them: sea-horn is worked with the same heat and the same presses, and it does something no other available material does. It stores work and returns it, through many thousands of cycles, without splitting like wood or slackening like sinew or taking a set like bronze. In a world that will never have steel to spare, this is where springs come from, and it remains where springs come from until the closing century of the record.

## The Rivian Epoch

### The State of the Art

Bronze is the substance of civilisation, and the copper and tin trades are older than reliable memory. The alloy rewards exactly the virtues the world was forced to cultivate: it melts at a temperature a charcoal furnace reaches without strain, casts cleanly and repeatably, resists salt air far better than iron, and forgives everything, since a flawed casting goes back to the crucible and is poured again, wasting fuel but never metal.

What a later age would find surprising is how far the alloy is already differentiated. A Rivian toolmaker of any standing keeps several distinct compositions and regards confusing them as an apprentice's error: low tin for anything struck or bent, the working alloys in the middle, and at the top a very high tin content for tools that only ever cut, hard and glassy and certain to shatter under a hammer blow but capable of a fine edge. Because bronze cannot be quenched, it is hardened by cold hammering, and an edge is peened back to hardness as part of sharpening. The tool is not merely abraded back to sharp; it is slightly reforged, every time. Bronze also work-hardens further in use, so a broken-in tool is harder than a new one, and a master's tools are better than a journeyman's for reasons beyond sentiment.

Beside the bronze grows the tradition that actually makes precision possible. A civilisation that cannot make its tools harder than its work makes its abrasives harder instead, and abrasives are minerals, which is to say they are free. Emery, corundum, garnet, quartz sand, pumice, and volcanic grit are plentiful, and the Rim of Fire and the Ertran volcanic country supply the best of it. Grinding, lapping, honing, and scraping are not the finishing stage of Rivian manufacture but the primary means by which hard and accurate things are made. Soft metal charges with grit and carries it, so the very softness that disqualifies bronze as a cutting tool qualifies it as a lap, and three plates worked against one another with sand give a true flat, from which follow the true straightedge, the true square, the true bearing, and the true seat.

The glue economy matures in parallel and is not primitive. Hide, sinew, and fish glues; casein curd set with lime; blood albumen; pitch cut with charcoal or brick dust for seams; tar for hafting. One absence shapes all of it: there is no bitumen anywhere in the world, so every waterproofing problem in Arlos is a resin problem, and resin problems consequently receive an attention out of all proportion to their apparent importance.

The answer comes from the hot country, and it comes as oil and gum rather than as anything distilled. The Merhal's floodplain and the tropical coasts of An-Suur between them supply the palm and nut oils on which the whole epoch's finishing trades run: pressed oils for lamps and dressing and soap, castor for a non-drying oil that lubricates without gumming, flax for a drying oil that skins over into a film and is therefore the first varnish anybody has. Along the Anish coast the tree gums are so abundant that hulls are caulked with what would elsewhere be a luxury, and the resin trade of that shore is old enough that no city on it remembers beginning one. Elsewhere the lac insect yields a resin gathered for sealing and finishing, fossil varnite comes off the Gulf shore in trade and is worked as ornament, and fresh copal moves along the same routes. In one coastal culture and nowhere else, the sap of a lacquer sumac is worked into a coating that resists water, salt, and every solvent then known, by an apprenticeship so slow, so secretive, and so poisonous to the skin that the craft never leaves its home workshops. Its nut-oil is discarded as waste for the next fifteen centuries.

What the epoch does not have, and never misses, is the conifer. The great pine forests lie on the southern continent, on the Stranu peninsula and through the Varsenne basin, far from every polity that matters in these centuries and worked by nobody for anything beyond local timber. The whole naval-stores economy that later ages treat as the foundation of Circulean industry is, in the Rivian Epoch, a stand of trees in a cold country with nobody in it.

Iron is known, rare, and prestigious. Varngard's marshes are already being cut, and the ore crosses the Southern Gulf to be finished by hands that will still be finishing it sixteen hundred years later.

One further material enters the record in this epoch and refuses, for the next two thousand years, to be explained. Gryph-bone is stiffer, stronger, and very much lighter than any bone has any business being, and the discrepancy is not marginal: it is the plain reason the greater gryphs fly at all, since nothing built to the ordinary rules of bone could carry those frames into the air, let alone carry them gracefully. Worked, it holds an edge poorly and holds a shape magnificently, and it goes into instrument frames, the plates and arbours of fine mechanisms, bow risers, and the fittings of anything whose owner cares more about weight than about cost. It cannot be increased by any craft. It comes only from the dead, in quantities the hunt and the accident allow and no more, and so its working is licensed from very early on and given to particular horners who account for their scraps and answer for their waste. The horners thereby acquire, in the same epoch, the three materials that will define their trade to its end: horn, sea-horn, and the bone nobody can account for.

### What Changed

The lapidary trades and the metal trades discover one another. Corundum and harder stones, bedded and cemented into bronze holders, give turning points, engraving points, drill tips, and dies for drawing wire, and the man who sets them is a gem-cutter and not a smith. This is the permanent Arlosian answer to the hardness limit, and it puts the jewel trades adjacent to the machine trades in a way that has no parallel elsewhere. Every precision mechanism of every later age descends from it.

Oil-pressing passes from a household task to an industry, on the beam and wedge presses of the floodplain temple estates, and the same presses turn out to serve a laminating shop as readily as an oil mill. The drying oils are recognised as a class in this epoch and not merely used, which means that varnish becomes a thing a craftsman selects instead of a thing he inherits, and the distinction between an oil that skins and an oil that stays wet is the first genuinely chemical distinction anybody in the basin draws.

And the whale stocks of the Circle Sea begin to thin, quietly and without anyone connecting the observation across two coasts. The basin is enclosed and its whales are slow to breed, and the fishery has perhaps a thousand years left in it.

## The Remean Era

### The State of the Art

Reme raises the material arts to a summit no successor has reached, and does so in three fields at once.

In bronze the empire casts at a precision and scale the succeeding centuries could only marvel at, and thinks in the metal as naturally as later ages would think in brass. Yet even Reme, commanding the iron sands of two coasts, stratifies its metals with visible care. The rank and file wear laminate, not bronze; the cast muscle cuirass belongs to officers and is display as much as protection; and iron goes only where nothing else will serve, or where the show of it will make the most difference.

In brass the empire holds a secret it carries into its grave. Common brass, copper cemented with zinc earths, is made everywhere and is uneven, unreliable, and coarse. The retort distillation of zinc, driving the metal over as vapour and condensing it pure, is a Remean process and a Remean monopoly, and purified zinc yields a brass of even and dependable grade that no rival can match.

Before either of those, however, comes the empire's least celebrated and most consequential material act, which is the opening of the southern forests. Reme's expansion along the southern coasts of the Western Gyre puts the conifer stands of Stranu and the Varsenne within reach of a chartered supply system for the first time in history, and the empire does to them what it did to the Merhal's grain: it schedules them. Tapping, tar-burning, and distillation become a plantation industry under charter, and pitch, tar, rosin, and turpentine enter Circulean commerce in quantities that no gum-bleeding coast could ever have matched. Everything downstream follows from this. The caulking of a fleet, the varnish on a laminate, the sizing in a glue, the fuel in a lamp, and eventually the sprit in an engine and the soot in a struck gum all trace back to a stand of southern pine that nobody had thought worth crossing a sea for until an empire needed its navy caulked on schedule. The woodlot's arithmetic, which governs every later age of the record, is written in this era and by these plantations.

In the pliants proper the imperial contribution is not a new substance but water resistance, standardisation, and the press. The hinge is struck glue: a gelatin glue treated with alum ceases to soften in damp, and an ancient material becomes, by the addition of a cheap salt already familiar to the dressing trades, an outdoor material. Combined with rosin sizing and a drying-oil finish, it makes glued assemblies that survive at sea. The kinship between the mordant salt of the alum trades and the light metal that a much later furnace wins from the same earths is real, and the naming of that metal preserves it.

From struck glue comes the plate. Linen laid up in many plies, each brushed with glue thinned to a varnish, pressed hot between bronze platens under a screw press, and faced with silk or fine ramie, gives a hard pliant board of real toughness that can be formed to the body while warm. Interleaving thin scales of hardened horn or boiled leather in the outer plies gives a stiff face over a compliant back, which is the same principle the armourers of the Imperial Age rediscover and express as doctrine. Reme arrives at it by feel and never states it. The armour it produces is light, sealed against river damp and coastal salt alike, producible in quantity, and repairable in the field by any competent artisan with the right resin instead of a bronzesmith with a forge, which is a logistical advantage worth more to the empire than any property of the material itself.

The same craft goes into hulls, where layered wood, hide, and bonded fibre are built up over a mould into a single stiffened shell that carries load continuously instead of through many thousands of worked joints. Laminated masts and yards are built up from staves and hooped, spanning lengths no single tree in the basin supplies, and oil-and-rosin impregnation driven into the timber's own grain slows rot and worm. The imperial fleets scale past the limits of grown timber on this basis, and the empire's naval power sits where the pine and the alum are.

Two smaller trades deserve mention because they persist. Reme exports clear resins pressed and worked to imitate gemstones, an ornamental line that keeps the copal and varnite trades alive across the whole periphery. And the empire's chartered conveyance system, which determines what it needs and where and pays or compels the relevant parties to deliver it on schedule, is what makes any of the material arts reproducible at imperial scale. The formulae matter less than the guarantee that the alum, the linen, the rosin, and the hide arrive in the right quantities at the right yard in the right month.

### What Changed

Everything, at once, and the losses are specific enough to be worth stating precisely.

The Fall takes the retort distillation of zinc, and true brass with it. It takes the chartered supply that underwrote every laminated hull. It takes whatever assistance the Shaping curriculum had lent to the state yards, which appears to have been employed not on the ordinary run of the craft but on the deep work: driving impregnation through a finished frame, and striking a thick lay-up evenly through its whole section instead of curing it from the outside inward. And it takes the empire's name, which means that everything subsequently recovered must be credited to somebody else.

What survives is the honest craft. Presses survive, and horn-working, and pitch, and ordinary glue, and the whole abrasive and lapidary tradition, which was never imperial in the first place. The regression that follows is therefore not a regression in chemistry. It is a regression in scale and in section: thick pieces, deep impregnation, and reliable large mouldings become unobtainable, while small work continues almost unaffected. Every later recovery in the pliant arts is a recovery of process, and this is why the aetheric trades, when they eventually arrive, are built into the industry from their first day. They are filling a hole whose shape everyone can see.

## The Age of Enlightenment

### The State of the Art

The first century after the Fall is defined by what no longer works at the size it used to.

The alchemical glues survive in fragments and in provincial workshops indifferent to their provenance, and what is reconstructed from them is coarser, less durable, and unfit for a hull. It remains entirely serviceable for shields, chests, cases, and small work, and small work is accordingly what the pliant trades of this era consist of. Hull construction reverts to plank across the whole basin and takes back with it the length limits plank imposes.

Brass is made everywhere and made badly. Cemented from zinc earths, it varies from batch to batch and from workshop to workshop, and no bore cut in it can be trusted to hold pressure. Bronze carries the load it always carried, and the tool alloys and the abrasive trades, having never depended on any imperial institution, come through the transition without a scratch.

Alum, meanwhile, is a common commodity of the dressing trades, and the empirical reconstitution of struck glue proceeds slowly in a dozen places at once, credited in each of them to a local saint, a legendary artisan, or a providential accident. To a much later archaeologist the convergence of the surviving methods is conspicuous. At the time it is invisible, and the proscription of the empire's name ensures it stays that way.

### What Changed

The advance of the era belongs to the foundry, and its consequences take three centuries to arrive.

In the second century AR the retort distillation of zinc is recovered from the same scattered provincial workshops that preserved so much else without knowing what they had. Purified zinc yields true brass, an alloy of even and dependable grade, and true brass is the material foundation of everything distinctive in later Circulean engineering. It can be cast true, bored fine, drawn into seamless tube, and trusted to hold pressure through years of service in damp sea air. Bronze is too dear in tin and too coarse in the boring for such work at scale, and iron rusts from within and cannot be spared besides. The tube-drawers' and instrument-borers' guilds that grow up around the alloy become, without anyone yet understanding why it matters, the custodians of their civilisation's central technology.

Nothing in this era makes use of it. The tube waits five hundred years for something worth putting inside it.

## The Dark Ages

### The State of the Art

The material arts stand at their lowest point since the Rivian Epoch, and stand there without collapsing, because the substances the age depends on are the forgiving ones. Bronze returns to the crucible when a casting fails. Brass is recoverable from scrap. Glue is cooked from what a butcher throws away. Pitch is boiled from bark. None of it requires an institution, and institutions are exactly what the age lacks.

Iron and steel hold their narrow and honoured ground, made more elevated by scarcity. Varngard's bog ore crosses the Southern Gulf as it always has and comes into the hands of the svartalfr of the high fells, whose long lives have made them smiths without rival, and leaves them as a steel with no equal anywhere in Arlos. It is a blade-metal and an instrument-metal, and Circulean merchants credit the blades to Svenu while only those who deal in the ore know how much of that fame was dug from a Varngardi marsh.

The basin's own whale fishery fails in this period, quietly and past the point of recovery, and sea-horn becomes an import. What had been a coastal harvest becomes an ocean trade beyond the Gate, in Svenish and Ukili'ata water, and the horners' guilds of the Circulean rim reorganise around the buying of it. Since sea-horn is the spring material of every torsion weapon, latch, catch, and mechanism in the basin, this quietly converts a domestic craft supply into a foreign policy, several centuries before anyone has cause to notice.

Lead is worked and universally despised, useful for weights and pipes and sheathing and carrying no standing whatever. Quicksilver is an older craft material with a long train of folk hazard behind it, used for gilding and amalgam by men who know what it costs them.

### What Changed

Two developments, neither of them recognised at the time.

The first is the sea tax. Brass sheathing enters use as a rich owner's fitting, and where it is laid over an iron-fastened hull it eats the fastenings beneath with an appetite no weather explains. Svenish yards, alone in carrying both metals in the same hull in quantity, meet the problem first and arrive at rules of thumb generations before anyone has a theory: bed the sheathing, keep the metals apart, watch the garboard. The rules are correct, they are followed everywhere within a century, and nobody knows why they work for another seven hundred years. When the explanation finally arrives it brings the tamed Charge with it.

The second is the retort. Charcoal burning is the one industry that never stopped, and burning it in closed vessels instead of open pits, to reclaim the fuel from the smoke, delivers as byproducts a sour liquor and a heavy tar that had previously gone up the flue. Nobody has any use for either. They are collected, sold cheap for preserving timber and dressing rope, and set aside. This is the beginning of the chemical spine of the whole later industry, and it arrives as refuse from a trade that runs flat out in every age regardless of who is in charge.

## The Age of Corsairs

### The State of the Art at the Opening

True brass is mature, the drawn tube is a routine product, and the organ-builders of the wealthier cities are turning pliant-sealed brass pressure vessels for their instruments, using struck glue, leather, and gut for the sealing and doing it well enough that a chest holds its wind for a season. The material world is otherwise the world of the Dark Ages with better commerce laid over it.

Latex is known. Two sources reach the basin's markets, a scrubby milkbush of the hot northern margins harvested by crushing the whole plant, and a coastal sea-gum tree whose latex sets hard and stays quick. Both are curiosities. Raw latex is sticky in heat, brittle in cold, and rotten within a season, and no craft has any use for a material that cannot be trusted through a year.

### What Changed: the Striking of the Gum

The gating discovery of the age is the sulfur cure, and it converts latex from a novelty into the material on which everything mechanical afterward depends. Heated with sulfur, the gum acquires a working temperature range, elastic recovery, and years of life. The trade word for the process is striking the gum, and the two-sense collision with the zinc trades is not accidental, since zinc's white calx turns out to be what makes the cure even instead of patchy, and the same guilds handle both.

The full recipe is four things and only the first is obvious. Sulfur and heat anyone can work out. Zinc calx and lime make the cure even. Pine-black, the fine soot of a starved turpentine flame and otherwise a worthless residue, multiplies the strength and abrasion life of the struck gum several times over. And the accelerant, a trace addition made from charcoal and sulfur roasted together to a foul liquor and then worked with the ammoniacal fractions of the char retort, turns a two-day bake into a two-hour one and decides whether a seal lasts a voyage or a decade.

The accelerant is the perfect guild secret. It is invisible in the finished article, impossible to recover from a cut sample, and it determines whether a competitor can hold pressure. Guilds keep it for generations, settle it in dowries, and lose it twice.

What it unlocks is the whole of pneumatics and hydraulics. Cup seals and packings, gaskets, flexible hose built as gum-and-cloth plies, diaphragms, and valve seats: before the cure, hydraulic work means greased leather and low pressures, and afterward, pressure becomes a design variable. The ceiling is metallurgical rather than chemical, since cylinders, accumulators, and pressure vessels are brass and bronze, and that caps working pressure and pushes the whole tradition toward large bores, low pressures, and generous seal areas. It pushes the working fluids the same way, toward castor oil and water-soap emulsions, neither of which attacks the gum and both of which are to hand.

Three hard pliants come out of the same shops. Hard gum, struck with a heavy sulfur charge until it turns black and brittle and machinable, cuts on a lathe, resists acid, and insulates, and goes into valve bodies, pipe fittings, instrument cases, and chemical ware. Struck fibre, made by soaking rag paper in a strong zinc-salt liquor, laminating the softened sheets, washing the salt back out over weeks and pressing dry, is tough, light, machinable, and unbreakable in the ways horn is breakable, and goes into gears, washers, pump vanes, and trunk shells. And moulded shellac, filled with wood flour or slate dust and pressed hot in bronze dies, produces the first genuinely mass-moulded goods in the world and establishes the shop practice that every later moulding trade inherits.

### What Changed: the Contained Air

The breath-arm arrives from noble duelling culture and not from military need, commissioned from organ-builders who already know how to make a vessel hold its wind, and founds the whole later air-gun tradition. Cheap and unreliable military airguns follow as a poor man's alternative to arcannon and crossbow.

Behind the fashion, the serious development is municipal. Pumps, bellows, and small machines driven off charged vessels give way to piped supply, and by the early thirteenth century a mature Concordian city runs on graded pneumatic mains, high pressure for forge and foundry, medium for workshops and public buildings, low for hearth-bellows and small tools and tube-mail, fed from crude open-tube charging stations whose waste heat warms whole districts, with portable brass bottles rechargeable at public points.

Every one of those systems has sea-horn in its valving. A valve spring is worked in bursts and rests between them, which is exactly the duty sea-horn is best at, and the guild that controls sea-horn import in a given city therefore controls the pace at which that city can plumb itself. This is a far subtler form of leverage than any monopoly on a fuel or an ore, and the horners of the Corsair cities exercise it with a discretion that has kept them largely out of the chronicles.

The distinction the trades arrive at here, without ever needing to explain it, is that sea-horn is for springs that rest and steel is for springs that wait. Sea-horn held bent for a season takes a set, and takes it faster warm and faster still damp, so anything that must sit under sustained load for years and still read true on the day it is called on has to be steel. In a pneumatic civilisation that category is small, unglamorous, and safety-critical: the relief valve on a high-pressure main, the setting spring in a gauge, the governor on a charging station. Guild ordinance and municipal inspection specify steel by name for these and for almost nothing else, so that a city's entire iron ration goes into a few pounds of components whose failure empties a district.

The whale trade meanwhile splits along a line drawn by fire. Rendering blubber means an open furnace burning for days aboard a timber ship soaked in oil, which is licit, since nothing about it is a prepared mixture, and is nonetheless a concentration of Fire's character in a place with nowhere to run. Svenish crews render aboard, keep their own courtesies over the try-pots, and take long voyages. Circulean owners cannot get such a hull underwritten, so they render ashore at fixed stations, which means their whaling requires coasts, concessions, and treaties. The head oil that comes off the toothed whales, clear in the barrel and setting to a soft white wax, burns brighter and steadier than anything else known and does not gum in a bearing, and it becomes at once the standard against which every other lamp is measured and the lubricant of every mechanism too small for grease.

## The Aetheric Age

### The State of the Art

Alchemy comes into the age already a science, matured over centuries of bench work into a discipline with its own reagents, its own procedures, and its own accumulated law about what substances do to one another. What it acquires in this age is a floor beneath it. Academy science organises aetheric engineering under the triad of Structure, Resonance, and Materiality, and alchemy discovers in aetheric theory the explanation of why its own rules hold, in the way any science of substances is eventually underwritten by an account of the field those substances sit in. The two remain entirely distinct trades with distinct guilds, distinct examinations, and a good deal of mutual condescension. An alchemist who takes up modulation is using a second science to sharpen his own, and an aetheric engineer who commissions a preparation is doing the same in the other direction. Neither has absorbed the other, and the persistent outsider habit of treating the two as one profession is a source of irritation to both.

What the pliants gain from the union is a theory where they had a family of recipes. The classification set out at the head of this account belongs to this era, and so does the practice of grading a pliant for stiffness the way a string is graded for tension, and the trade language of compliance that follows from it.

The engine drives everything. The compression-expansion work cycle is demonstrated around 1225 through a pliant-membrane engine, a boiler-less prime mover that slots directly into the existing mains, and some fifteen years later the Law of Harmonic Conversion sets the limit that governs all engine-craft afterward: heat may always be turned into organised resonant or pneumatic motion and never wholly, its yield bounded by the ratio of temperatures at the hot and cold ends. A limit honestly stated is an incentive, and the incentive is for hotter throats, colder ends, and no waste in the geometry between them.

Answering it is a materials problem before it is a mechanical one. Membranes and seals of graded compliance let bores be coupled, staged, and packed together without the leakage and rattle that made the old open tubes so profligate. Stacked engines mature through the first half of the fourteenth century, self-harmonising resonance design is formalised by mid-century, and closed-vessel variants displace the open choirs in most heavy service before the century is out.

This is where the third axis of pliant grading earns its keep. A machine that works by holding a tone cannot be built of materials that argue with it. Steel rings, and a resonance-engineer will avoid it for acoustic reasons well past the point where scarcity alone would explain the choice. Choir engines are brass, pliant, and horn substantially because those materials keep their mouths shut. And varnite, the fossil resin of the Gulf shore, worked for centuries as mere ornament, is found to hold resonant properties of direct worth to choir and resonance work, and passes within a generation from jewelry to strategic material.

### What Changed: Heat Without Fire

The aetheric kiln is the age's largest single contribution to the material record, and it is worth being exact about what it is. It is a kiln worked by direct application of aetheric modulation instead of by burning anything, and its cost is licensed practitioner time and inspection instead of cords of charcoal. It is not a gate on aetheric practice, and nothing about aetheric modulation depends on it. It is simply the most conspicuous product of an understanding of applied aether that by this age permeates every trade in the basin, and which is why the centuries carry the name they do.

Where a kiln goes to modulation, the constraint on that industry moves off the forest and onto the guild roll. Glass, lime, and brick are enormous consumers of fuel, and every one of them that makes the transfer eases the woodlot's arithmetic and tightens somebody's grip on construction itself. The pliant works make the same move for a different reason: presses heated by circulating hot oil, or by resonant means in the better shops, give an even flameless heat with no vessel of hot water anywhere near the varnish, and the world has excellent reasons for keeping raised vapour out of any building it cares about.

Modulation earns its keep in the pliant shops in three further ways, all of them process control and none of them a new capability. Resin can be degassed before lay-up. Impregnation can be driven deeper into a thick section than soaking alone will carry it. And a thick lay-up can be brought to strike through its full depth at once, which is the difference between a laminate that is sound and one that is sound near the surface and hollow in the middle. This is precisely the hole the Fall left, and it is filled at last by a wholly different means than the one that made it.

It should be said plainly that none of this is a gate. What modulation buys is throughput and consistency, and time and hands buy the same thing more slowly. A shop with a licensed practitioner strikes a thick section in hours; a shop without one strikes the same section in a long soak cure with more presses and more labour, and the panel that comes out is the same panel. The pliant trades are therefore open to any populous state that wants them, which is a fact of considerable consequence in the following century.

### What Changed: the New Metals and the Old Tools

The aetheric furnace yields alumen in the closing decades of the fifteenth century, the first genuinely new metal in nearly two millennia, won not by combustion but by a sustained working driven through a molten bath that strips from certain common earths a metal no fire had ever loosened from them. It is light beyond any prior experience of metal. Its first great consequence is military, since a compressed-air bottle light enough to carry several completes the breath-arm's long passage from duelling curiosity to infantry weapon. Its second is aeronaval, where every ounce spared is less load on the envelope and fewer calibrations of Attraction to hold. Its third is political: the furnaces are costly, few, and demanding of the rarest calibration expertise, and Lavion's early lead in building them hardens into an effective stranglehold that every rival power's planning bends around for the next century.

Alloyed with copper, alumen gives a bronze harder and far more wear-resistant than the tin alloys, indifferent to salt, and workable by the shops that already exist. It does not reach steel. It narrows the gap enough that a great deal of work which had been steel-only quietly stops being so, and it is the first improvement in general tooling since the Rivian tool alloys were first graded.

The most consequential material discovery of the age is not a new substance at all but an explanation of an old one, and it comes early, among the first fruits of aetheric engineering as the discipline finds its feet.

Gryph-bone had been an anomaly on the horners' benches for two thousand years, licensed and accounted and never understood. What the young discipline establishes is that the anomaly is not in the bone's composition but in its arrangement: the lattice is itself a modulation, held in the living animal and persisting in the dead one, working the Attraction between Soil and Air across the whole body of the material and lightening it against its own substance. A gryph does not fly because it is strong for its weight. It flies because its skeleton is a device, grown rather than built, and the greater gryphs are as large as they are precisely because that device scales in a way that feathers and hollow bone alone never could.

Two things follow at once. The first is that a natural modulation can be sustained indefinitely without a practitioner attending it, which reorganises a good deal of aetheric theory and is argued about for a century. The second is practical, and it is the making of the age. If a lattice can hold the working, then a lattice can be laid down deliberately, and the material that results is tractite: a wrought substance whose whole body carries a standing modulation of the Soil-Air Attraction, and which therefore weighs against itself and, in sufficient grade, lifts. It is difficult, slow, and expensive to produce, the working is spoiled by careless handling and lost outright by over-heating, and the guilds that make it hold their calibrations as closely as any accelerant-house ever held its liquor.

The consequences belong properly to the account of the aeronaves and the gliders, both of which are tractite before they are anything else. What belongs here is the shape of the thing: the first deliberately engineered aetheric material in the record is a copy of a bird's bone that is not a bird's, taken from an animal whose flight nobody had thought to find suspicious, and it was sitting on a licensed horner's bench the entire time.

The abrasive and lapidary traditions reach precision in this age. Jewelled pivots make possible the pressure clocks and valve-code beacons of the 1450s and the fluidic logic that follows them, and precision timekeeping and small mechanism work in the Circulean world are, and always were, a jeweller's business. Bonded abrasive wheels arrive as a pliant product, shellac and rosin at first and harder binders later, which makes the pliant works a supplier to the tool trades and knits the two industries together permanently.

### What Changed: the Char Trunk at Chemical Scale

The refuse of the charcoal retorts becomes a chemical industry. The sour liquor gives wood vinegar and wood spirit and, on further work, acetone. The tar gives creosote and the phenol-bearing fractions. Wood spirit passed as vapour over hot copper gauze gives a sharp preservative reagent, and that reagent condensed with the phenol fraction, or better with the nut-oil of the lacquer sumac that fifteen centuries of lacquerers had thrown away, gives the first genuinely dead resin: a dark, hard, heat-proof thermoset that moulds under pressure and takes filler happily.

Laid up with paper or cloth it gives board, and board is the workhorse hard pliant of everything that follows. Gears that run quiet, insulators, structural panel, and machine fittings all come out of it. Cut with the sumac oil it gives friction linings that outlast brass. The material that carries the industrial age therefore comes, by a route nobody would have predicted, out of the ash pits and a discarded byproduct of a luxury craft.

The small engine closes the age. Pliants of ever finer grade, resonant elements cut from varnite and its refined kin, and modulation applied through the whole body of a miniature machine yield sealed hearts no larger than a strongbox, then a loaf, then a fist. Alumen arrives in the same years to lighten bottle and frame alike, and the two developments complete one another. Every such engine runs on the head oil, because nothing else stays sweet in a small bearing in a salt-damp climate, and the whale trade thereby acquires an industrial customer at the exact moment its lighting market is about to be destroyed.

## The Imperial Age

### The State of the Art

The material world of the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries is a settled type. Structure is bronze, true brass, and laminate. Motion is contained air. Sealing, damping, insulating, and tensioning are pliant. Cutting is bronze, alumen bronze, and grit. Springs are sea-horn where they rest and steel where they wait. Precision is a jeweller's craft. Lift is gas, modulated Attraction, and tractite. And the whole of it is metered by two supplies that no state controls outright: the woodlot, and a marsh across the Southern Gulf.

Aethermatons enter docks, yards, and policing in the early decades, pneumatic constructs with fluidic logic for a brain, a choir engine for a heart, and braided pliants for tendons, and the name everyone actually uses for them is brassbacks. Their effect on the material trades is felt first in labour, since a brassback hauls and lifts and holds where a gang once did, and the handling that had always set the practical pace of a yard or a works ceases to do so.

The chartered company becomes the characteristic institution. Lavion's Companies Royal hold crown monopoly over trade with distant coasts, the Commonwealth's United Chartered Companies answer them, and the paper war between them stays cold in the Circulean and turns hot in the Rim of Fire, where the plantations, the whaling stations, and the harder abrasives all are.

### What Changed: the Industrial Pliants

Cellulose is attacked three ways, and the record of the three is a useful demonstration of how the world's rules select its chemistry.

Nitrated, it gives a quick hard pliant that plasticises beautifully with camphor and behaves impeccably on the bench. It is also a body that carries within itself everything it needs to burn, and shops that made it learned within a season what that means. The stock is abandoned, the works that made it are remembered in the trade with a certain grim affection, and the route is closed for good.

Acetylated with the acid of the char trunk, cellulose gives the same virtues without the vice. It can be spun, cast as film, drawn into fibre, and doped onto fabric, and it becomes the standard quick pliant of the age for everything from instrument cases to airframe covering.

Dissolved through the charcoal-and-sulfur liquor and spun back out into acid, it gives a strong regenerated fibre, and this is the one that matters most. Cord made from it is what lets a pressure hose or a pneumatic wheel carry load without the fabric plies chafing themselves apart. The Corsair-era cure made hose possible; the fibre cord makes it survivable at industrial duty, and every hydraulic and pneumatic system fielded in the following war depends on it.

Reinforcement completes the picture. Basalt melted and drawn fine gives a mineral thread that is stiff, incombustible, and made from the most worthless rock in the world, of which the basalt deserts have a great deal. Glass gives a paler and finer version. Either, laid in the dark dead resin, gives a hard pliant with the stiffness of a light metal and none of its corrosion, and the composite trades and the ceramic trades thereby become the same trades, with consequences for who owns what.

These materials also close a circle back to the tool question. A fibre-laden pliant destroys a bronze edge in seconds and cannot be machined by ordinary means at all. It must be moulded to final form and finished with grit, which gives the Arlosian habit of net-shape moulding a hard technical reason underneath the economic one, and hands yet more of the finishing trades to the abrasive guilds.

### What Changed: the Vat

The fuel question is reopened by a bench curiosity that becomes an industry. A fixed plantation oil worked with wood spirit and a lye of lime and ash separates into two layers. The upper is a thin, clean, near-odourless oil that pours cold where a fixed oil goes to grease, burns without gumming a burner, and has no meaningful vapour space at any temperature a hold will see. It is licit by the plainest reading of the rule, since it will not even light without a wick, and it beats sprit outright on cold-weather handling and burner life. The formal registers call it palm sprit and no dock has ever said so; within a generation of its arrival the word on every wharf in the basin is palmit.

The lower layer is a heavy sweet syrup, and the works that draw it call it the settled syrup for the plain reason that it settles. It keeps a hard pliant from going brittle in frost, serves as a humectant and a soap stock, and goes into the water-soap hydraulic fluids to keep them from freezing. A works that makes fuel makes pliant softener as an unavoidable consequence, which knots the fuel economy and the materials economy into a single industry with one set of guilds and one set of political interests.

Two consequences follow immediately. The first is that palmit destroys the lighting market for the head oil, since a crop that yields every year undercuts a voyage that yields sometimes, and the whale trade contracts hard onto lubricant and sea-horn. The whales are spared, insofar as they are, by a vat in a chemical works, and nobody involved intended it.

The second is that a civilisation whose engines have burned southern pine since the day they were invented, and whose furnaces have burned it since Reme first chartered the plantations, now finds that its best fuel grows in other people's countries. Nut and palm crops want heat and rain, which means the northern jungle margins, the An-Suur littoral, and the Rim of Fire. The wheel has turned a full circle nobody in the trade notices: the pressed oils of the Merhal and An-Suur, which lit and dressed and lubricated the whole Rivian world before an empire ever went south for a conifer, return after three thousand years to fuel its engines. Charcoal and sprit were a domestic constraint that everyone shared. Palmit is an imperial one, and it puts a real economic weapon into hands that had not previously held one. The woodlot's arithmetic is loosened and not broken: palmit renews at a palm's pace instead of a pine's and yields far more per acre, and it is still a crop, and there is still no depot anywhere that does not have a plantation behind it. Range remains a logistics problem in perpetuity. It simply becomes a colonial one.

### What Changed: the War

The Great War consumes materials at a rate no previous conflict approached, and its demands fall almost entirely on the pliant trades. Hose and gasket by the mile for every pneumatic and hydraulic system fielded. Cord and struck gum for wheels and tracks. Self-sealing gum linings for tanks. Gum facepieces and corrugated hose for gas defence. Dead-resin board and hard gum throughout. Dope and resin-bonded ply for airframes. And armour.

The armour scheme of the age is the Remean plate rediscovered with better ingredients and finally stated as a principle. A hard face of brass or, late and rarely, piatrium breaks and blunts the striking body; a thick backing of cord and fibre laid in dead resin takes what remains through delamination across many plies and does not spall. The face does the shattering and the back does the catching, and the glue line is still the weapon. Where weight rules absolutely, the backing is filled with tractite beads to a foam, giving a panel that is stiff, quiet, and very nearly weightless, and this lay-up is the most jealously kept secret in the aeronaval yards.

Hard pliant armour is what makes the rigged soldier possible, worn as the pneumatic-tendon exoskeleton the ranks call dumb brass, and what puts a fighting brassback beside him. It also decides a good deal about the war's shape, because pliant craft is not gated behind the aetheric kilns that bottleneck alumen, and a power with a larger population can therefore field hard pliant armour at genuine parity by substituting hands and time for licensed practitioners. Vel Kiriya does exactly that, and the eastern front is fought on the consequences.

The guild secrets do not survive the requisitioning. What ends four hundred years of accelerant lore is not legislation but a war administration that needs the stuff by the thousand tons a year, takes the recipe, publishes the standard, and settles compensation afterward. The horners lose their trade in the same decade for a different reason, as hard pliant takes the stays and the springs together, and a guild that had quietly gated the plumbing of cities for six centuries loses in one generation the material on which that leverage rested. What survives is the oldest and smallest part of the craft. The gryph-bone bench is still licensed, still accounted, and still theirs, because the licence was never about the difficulty of the work and is not made obsolete by any pliant, and a horners' hall of the following century is a room of combs and buttons with one locked cabinet at the back of it.

### What Changed: Piatrium

The smelters of the black sands had always known their ore carried a second burden. A fraction of the sand resisted every furnace, fouled the bloom, and thickened the slag, and the treatises of the Aetheric Age named it an uniron and characterised it as a metal-bearing earth no art then known could reduce. There the matter rested for decades as a scholarly fact of no consequence.

Consequence arrived by way of the Rim of Fire, where tropical waters had taught the United Chartered Companies at ruinous cost to prize salt-resistance above nearly every other virtue, and Company money began funnelling into the light earth whose laboratory grains shrugged off brine as only brass would. The outbreak of formal hostilities in 1561 first paused that inquiry and then transformed it. Under the war administration of Parliament, specialists drawn from every relevant guild were convened at Barvella, a municipal principality whose long history of armory work made it the natural seat for anything demanding furnace discipline, pressure-craft, and discretion in equal measure.

The metallurgical line succeeded in 1571. The Barvella process, sibling to the alumen furnace and of a distinct lineage, drives a sustained aetheric working through a bath of molten salts in which the stubborn earth is seated, stripping it of its burden directly and yielding the metal as a porous sponge instead of a melt. The sponge is consolidated by heat and the hammer, in sealed vessels and under jealous exclusion of open air, since the hot metal ruins itself on contact with the atmosphere. The discipline is wholly new in its particulars and, in its reliance on the hammer over the crucible, oddly ancestral in feel. The metal is pale, light, uncannily strong, and indifferent to salt, rust, and rot alike, and the guildsmen who first worked it named it piatrium after the Noble Element of Stone, for a substance whose obstinacy in the furnace and constancy once won seemed to speak for themselves.

In spring terms piatrium is metallic sea-horn without the vices. It flexes far, stores a great deal of work for its weight, and neither creeps nor cares about damp, salt, or heat, and it is the first material in the record that does the sea-horn duty and the steel duty at once. After 1571 it walks into every application where weight and calibration both matter: flight fittings, small engine valving, precision instruments, exosuit anchors. It leaves the machine shop entirely alone, since it takes no cutting edge, so files, dies, hardened races, and every tool for working steel remain steel permanently. It does nothing at all for the soft iron that Tension work requires. And it is ruinously expensive, so that the practical situation for a century afterward is three-tiered: sea-horn for the bulk of springs, steel for the few that must not drift, and piatrium for whatever the crown or the Consortium has decided is worth the price this year.

Word of the breakthrough reached Santra in the months before the Desolation and burned with the city. What survived was Barvella itself, the charter and the guilds and the process. In the fevered patriotism of the war's final years piatrium went to the field by the improvised channels of individual armories supplying the lances they knew, experimental exosuits and bottle sets and rotostrat frames, no two batches to a common pattern, in numbers too small to weigh on any battle. The war ended before the metal could matter to it, and its qualities made it matter all the more in the peace that followed. The Project passed into the Barvella Consortium, a standing joint venture owned by its member guilds in common, a legal novelty in its own right since the process belongs to a body that exists only because its members can no longer untangle their contributions to it.

Alongside it, and much less remarked, the plating baths of the aetheric trades came of age in the same decades and displaced the quicksilver gilding that had served since antiquity, laying true brass, silver, or gold onto baser work in films of any fineness by a sustained working through a bath. Nobody at the time could say what such a bath actually was. The generation that followed the war found out, and the answer reopened every question in this account.

## The Standing Bargain

For all that changed across sixteen centuries, one fact did not.

Arlos never had a hard, cheap, universal material, and never found one. Every age of the record is a variation on the same response, which is to stop asking for a stronger substance and start arranging weak ones so that their weaknesses point in different directions. Bronze is cut by grit because grit is harder than bronze and free. Springs are horn because horn returns what it is given and iron cannot be spared. Hulls are glue because glue distributes what a fastening would concentrate. Armour is a face and a back because no single thing does both jobs. The pliants are not a departure from that instinct but its consummation, and the industrial composites of the Imperial Age are the flatbow's answer restated in a chemical vocabulary the bowyer would not have recognised and a principle he would have recognised at once.

There is one exception, and it is instructive that it had to be stolen from an animal. Tractite is the only material in the whole record that does something no arrangement of ordinary substances can be made to do, and the world did not reason its way to it. It found it lying on a horner's bench, where it had been lying, licensed and accounted and quietly impossible, since the Rivian Epoch.

The two scarcities that opened the account were never lifted. Iron stayed dear and stayed foreign, and the fulminant century that followed the war discovered, at the exact moment it needed soft iron for cores by the ton, that its bloodless power was founded on the one metal the Circle Sea has never had. The woodlot's arithmetic was loosened by palmit, moved by the aetheric kilns, and never once broken.

And through all of it the same sentence has been said in every workshop of every age, by bowyers and shipwrights and armourers and pliant-men who would have had nothing else to say to one another: the glue line is the weapon.
