# On the Ships of Arlos

## The Shape of the Problem

Arlosian shipbuilding is governed by four facts, none of them a matter of skill, and every hull ever floated in the Circle Sea basin is an answer to some arrangement of them.

The first is the scarcity of iron. A ship is, on any other world, an object held together and held straight by metal: spikes and bolts through the planking, knees and brackets at every corner, strapping laid diagonally under the deck to stop the ends from drooping, chain at the bow and a great iron anchor at the end of it. Arlos has iron for edges, springs, and instruments, and nothing to spare for any of this. What follows from that single absence runs through every age of the record.

The second is the arithmetic of the woodlot. The same managed forests that feed the furnaces feed the shipyards, and a deep-keeled ocean hull consumes several times the timber of a barge of equal burden. Fleet-building and metal-founding are competitors for one resource, and the price of a ship has always risen and fallen with the price of charcoal for reasons no shipwright had to be taught.

The third is the Circle Sea itself. It is enclosed, shoal along most of its rim, and near enough tideless that its harbours neither dry out nor scour themselves. Its two Gyres turn with a patience that can be timed on a calendar, and the Rush between them, where the currents meet north of the Ertran cape, has governed passage between east and west for as long as anyone has kept records of passage at all. A sea like this rewards a wholly different vessel from the one an ocean rewards, and rewards it so thoroughly that for the better part of two thousand years no Circulean had any reason to build the other kind.

The fourth is that the sea is inhabited. A captain's real capital, on the Circulean rim as in the fjords, has always been the set of standing courtesies she holds with the elemental company of the water she works, and those courtesies end where her water ends. Beyond the Gate of Continents a ship carries no introductions. This is the oldest and most durable reason the Open Ocean stayed closed, and it survived every improvement in hulls that the later ages produced.

## Prehistory

### The State of the Art

Two boatbuilding traditions arise in the basin, independently, from opposite starting points, and their descendants have never fully merged.

In the east the ancestor is the raft. A raft is a platform of bundled or pegged sections, and when sides are built up onto such a platform the section divisions become partitions running across the hull. This is the origin of the transverse bulkhead, the single most consequential structural idea the Circulean world ever produced, and it arrives by accident: a hull built this way is compartmented before anyone has thought of compartmenting it, survives a holing that would sink a hollow hull, and takes the ground on a bar without breaking its back. Because such a hull has no spine to grip the water, the boards that give it a bite are hung over the side and lifted when the bottom rises to meet them, and the leeboard enters the record at the same moment as the flat bottom it serves.

In the far west, on the jungle coast of An-Suur, the ancestor is the dugout. The short, steep, cataract-broken creeks of that country favour a small hollowed hull with an upswept forefoot for running a surf-line and nosing over a bar, and when plank strakes are sewn up onto such a hull to raise its freeboard, the hollowed base becomes, without anyone intending it, a keel plank. The Anish tradition therefore possesses a spine from its beginnings, and this is the reason the Anish and no one else were making long open-water runs across the Gyres in ages when every other people hugged its own shore.

Into this the Ukili'ata arrive from the Open Ocean in the west, on hydrofoil skims and catamarans, having abandoned their home in the Rim of Fire. They bring a third design philosophy entire, and one wholly foreign to the basin: nothing on their vessels is rigid and nothing is heavy. Their hulls are lashed at every joint, because the cross-beams of a multihull take loads that would tear a bolted joint out of the timber and a lashed joint simply gives; their outrigger stays always to windward, so that a change of course is made by reversing the vessel end for end instead of turning its bow through the wind; and their foils are carried on struts of laminated timber tuned to flex within limits and return, which is the oldest working demonstration in Arlos of a principle the alchemical trades would not formalise for another two thousand years.

### What Changed

Everything in this age is a beginning, but three of the beginnings determine the rest of the record. The bulkhead makes cheap bulk carriage possible in shoal water. The leeboard makes a spineless hull steerable, and thereby forecloses any Circulean interest in the keel for two millennia. And the Ukili'ata make landfall, guarding their techniques from the first day and trading freely in everything else, so that the basin acquires a permanent neighbour whose seamanship it can observe, admire, and never acquire.

## The Rivian Epoch

### The State of the Art

The gyre barge takes the form it has held ever since: flat-bottomed, bulkheaded, unrigged or nearly so, steered by sweep and held to its course by leeboards, and carried southwest on the Eastern Gyre's turn at a pace that can be reckoned in advance to within a day or two. It is not a primitive vessel. It is the correct answer to a rotary current, and because it is correct it stops all further inquiry: a hull that needs no rig needs no rigging crew, no ballast, no windward ability, and no seamanship beyond pilotage. Cargo moves on the belt, the belt runs on a schedule, and the schedule is written into the calendar.

On the rivers the same bulkheaded logic divides into two incompatible crafts. The Hal, running in a single incised channel from the western escarpment to the sea, is a road: it carries a deep, narrow trunk barge that drifts down loaded and is warped or hauled back up along a towpath, and the whole system terminates at the foot of the escarpment steps, where cargo passes to the caravan roads. The Mer, braided and silting and forever abandoning its own channels, cannot be roaded at all. Its craft are very wide and very shallow, spoon-bowed to ride up over a bar, poled and swept where they cannot be sailed, and divided into many compartments because grounding is an event of the working day and not an accident. The vessel is worth less than the man aboard it: knowledge of the Mer's reaches is inherited property, held by particular families on particular stretches, and passed with the introductions to the water's own company that make the passage safe. Whoever holds the pilots holds the river, and the city-temples that consolidate the floodplain in this epoch do so with one hand on the irrigation law and the other on the pilot houses.

Along the Anish coast a third pattern matures. No river there is worth conquering and no city can absorb its neighbour, so growth runs outward along the shore by daughter-founding, and the sea between the bluff-cities is the only road that connects them. Anish shipbuilding accordingly perfects the sewn plank hull on its dugout spine, caulked with the resins of a coast that has them in absurd abundance, and Anish seafaring perfects everything that surrounds a hull: the harbour mark, the survey of a shifting bar, the sailing direction written down and kept, and, most consequentially, the underwritten voyage. Temple-treasuries take deposits, witness contracts, and stand surety for cargoes, and a risky crossing becomes a routine business the moment somebody else carries its risk. This is the Anish contribution the later Circulean world most thoroughly absorbed and least often credits.

In the southwestern fjords the boat-clans of Svenu first appear, raiding and trading along the Open Ocean rim and delving past the Suurian Gates into the Gyres. Their vessel at this stage is not the ship the later world will know them by. It is a long, low, double-ended pulling boat, its overlapping strakes sewn or lashed to one another and to light internal ribs, without keel, without mast, and without sail, driven wholly by oars and steered with a side paddle. What it possesses already is the quality that will define every Svenish hull after it: an overlapped shell built to flex with a sea instead of resisting it, light enough to be hauled out by its own crew and repaired anywhere with cordage and a knife. What it lacks is range. A rowed boat carries its own limit in the food and water its rowers consume, and Svenish reach in this epoch is a reach along coasts and across the Gulf, made in short legs from beach to beach, with the open water crossed only where it cannot be gone around.

### What Changed

The Ukili'ata homestead fleets begin trading regularly at Anish ports, and Rim-wise seamanship becomes a badge of standing along that coast. The rig is the one part of the Ukili'ata package that can be stolen by looking at it, and it is: the fore-and-aft sail plans of the Western Gyre descend from Ukili'ata patterns copied at Anish quays in these centuries, giving the west a rig lineage distinct from the battened lug that comes down the rivers out of the east.

And Svenu acquires, in this epoch, the constraint that will govern its naval power for the whole of the record. The bog iron of the marsh country across the Southern Gulf renews at the pace of marsh chemistry and never faster, and the smiths of the southern Lupines who finish it work at the pace of their own hands. A supply of that shape can arm and fasten one excellent ship. It can never arm a fleet, and no later development of the Svenish hull ever changes this. Every subsequent age finds Svenu superior at sea and incapable of numbers, and the reason is metallurgical and not cultural, whatever the clans themselves have come to believe about it.

## The Remean Era

### The State of the Art

Reme raises Circulean shipbuilding to a summit no successor has reached, and does so through two advantages that were never available to anyone else.

The first is material. The empire's mastery of gums, resins, and alchemical glues, best attested in the laminate armour issued to its rank and file, was applied equally to hulls: layered wood, hide, and bonded fibre built up over a mould into a single stiffened shell, sealed against salt and river damp by the same craft. A laminated shell carries load continuously instead of through many thousands of worked joints, which means it does not droop at the ends as a plank hull does, and it is light and stiff besides. Remean warship hulls could accordingly be built longer than any plank hull of that or any later age, and it is this, more than the empire's bronze, that outside observers found impossible to reproduce.

The second is the crew. Big oared ships are no Remean monopoly: the Anish yards build them as a matter of course, rating each hull by the rowers who sit in one file of oars, and their largest, the tens, put several paid rowers to each oar so that only the one at the end of the loom needs to know the stroke. Reme's difference lies elsewhere. Every Remean oar is held by a citizen who is also a legionary, so a warship carries no passengers and lands its whole crew as infantry. A Shaped body pulls two or three times what an ordinary rower can, so a Remean oar carries fewer hands than any other of its size. And the cadence that moves a legion as one body moves a crew the same way, so a Remean warship needs no drum and no piper. Drill can bring any crew to a steady stroke; what it cannot buy is the recovery from a disturbance, a wave or a fouled oar or a sudden order to back water, which runs down an ordinary crew and takes several strokes to settle. A Remean crew settles at once. It backs off a strike before it can be grappled, draws in one side's oars at the last instant to shear an enemy's, and turns in its own length, and its tactics are built around the helpless seconds an Anish ten spends recovering from a blow it has dealt or taken.

It is worth being precise about what the empire actually kept at sea, because the great ships have crowded the rest of the fleet out of memory. A hull's crew is a unit of the legion and a fleet underway is the legion on the march, and the number of hands to an oar rises with the size of the hull. The coastal and riverine patrol craft row one citizen to an oar on twenty-four to thirty-six oars, two or three spiracionês to a boat, and do the tedious work of a standing navy year in and year out: they keep the Circulean rim in order and meet the odd Svenish raiding clan. The belanion of sixty rows two to an oar on thirty oars. From the biabelanion upward the standard is three to an oar, the biabelanion's hundred and twenty on forty oars in a single level and the toton's three hundred and sixty on a hundred and twenty oars in two levels of thirty files, the line ship of the empire. All of these lie within the length a plank yard could build, and the empire's laminate makes them light and stiff without being required to make them possible. The estertoton of seven hundred and twenty is a toton at double length, sixty files a side, the one hull only laminate allows, and like most summits the estertotonês were few, expensive, and concentrated in the fleets of the mother city. Civilian craft, crewed by Harmonized citizens stronger than any ordinary rower but well short of the legionary, row one or two to an oar.

The Anish count never measured any of this. Rated by the rowers in a file, the patrol craft is a one, the toton a six, and the estertoton a six as well, since the rating takes no notice of length; the empire's line of battle rates no higher than the middling classes of the Anish yards, and beats their tens. Every Remean oar is also two or three times too large for the bench that works it, a signature that marks every Remean hull, civil or military, and grows with the hull's rank.

Around all of this the empire lays down the ordinary furniture of a thalassocracy. Bronze is cast at a scale and precision the succeeding centuries could only marvel at, and goes into every fitting a ship requires. Ports, dams, lighthouses, and moles are built throughout the periphery, since an empire that only extracted grain and iron would have generated rebellion faster than compliance. The tribute barges of the Merhal are standardised and multiplied, and the gyre barge passes from a regional habit into the basin's universal freight vessel. And a staging harbour raised on the tip of Ertra, where the two coasts of the Gyres come nearest, is sited precisely because whoever holds the Rush holds the passage between east and west.

The empire's artillery goes to sea in the same hulls. The dinamium is an open trough banded with stages and charged by a gang pounding a trigger block in turn, and its trough must lie straight, which only a hull that does not droop at the ends can promise. Small dinamia on swivels throw grapnels, and a spiracion hauls the grappled hull alongside by hand. The relemium lies along the centreline of a toton or an estertoton and is aimed by turning the whole hull, its strikers drawn one hand from each oar while the other two keep it pulling; it is pounded up on the last approach, fires once at close range before the grapnels fly, and batters coastal walls from the water in siege. The furvex, largest of the troughs, rides the spines of the greatest hulls and of estertotonês built to carry it, and ashore lies bedded in moles and harbour forts, where no fleet anchors within reach of a harbour mouth it covers.

### What Changed

The laminated shell, the standardised barge, and the harbourworks are the era's three lasting bequests, and only one of them survived the Fall intact.

Far outside the empire's scope, the Svenish hull takes its second step. Sewn and lashed seams give way to iron rivets clenched over roves, which hold a shell far more securely than cordage and permit thinner strakes and a longer boat. The step is only possible because Svenu sits at the end of the one iron supply chain in the world that renews itself, and it commits the tradition permanently to that supply: from this era onward a Svenish hull is an iron object as much as a timber one, and the size of the Svenish fleet is set in a marsh across the Gulf. The boat remains rowed, keel-less, and mastless, and its range remains the range of oars. Reme meets these clans with the same patrols it uses for pirates and thinks no more of them than that.

At the era's close, the Lurist crusade against Reme sails from Anish harbours in an Anish-built and largely Anish-crewed fleet, drawn from the shipwright legacy those cities had spent centuries building. Its hulls are bronze and timber, its rowers unshaped, and its banners carry the creed's geometry. It never arrives. The catastrophe at Remea scrambles the Western Gyre badly enough to carry the whole fleet far south of its landfall, and the empire's naval art ends without ever being defeated at sea.

## The Age of Enlightenment

### The State of the Art

The first century after the Fall is defined by what has stopped working.

The Remean warship, remembered under the Lurist name of polyreme, cannot be manned. The hulls can be measured, and are; the bronze fittings can be copied, and are; the oar plans are reproduced in yards from Balboa to the Anish coast. The oars defeat them. Each is sized for three Shaped hands, which is to say for six to nine ordinary rowers, on a loom that seats three, and the hull is too narrow to take the longer loom an ordinary crew would need, so that a toton re-oared with lighter sweeps can be rowed across a calm harbour and never fought. The surviving records then contradict one another. Whoever reads the benches counts the crew correctly and cannot credit what the records say the ships did; whoever reads loose oars and texts assumes ordinary rowers and inflates the crews past belief; and within two generations the descriptions of Remean fleets are read as boastful exaggeration by people who cannot conceive of the missing half of the machine. The proscription of the empire's arts, and the erasure of its name, close the question for good.

The Anish tens outlive the empire, rowed as they always were by paid crews several to an oar, and fade for a plainer reason: the Theocracy has no rival left at sea worth building them against.

What does not stop working is the galley. The single-banked vessel, one man to an oar, needs nothing the empire took with it: any yard can build one, any port can crew one, and ordinary drill will bring a crew to a serviceable standard inside a season. The monoreme therefore survives the Fall entirely intact, and is the ship on which the whole of the following millennium's naval power in the Circle Sea is actually built. Where the empire's fleets are remembered as a marvel, the empire's fleets are inherited as a rowing bench.

The bireme survives too, and survives as something else. Two banks are precisely at the edge of what an unshaped crew can be brought to do: it takes years of drill to work an upper and lower oar together at speed, the skill decays within months of a fleet being laid up, and it can therefore only be held by a state wealthy and stable enough to keep a professional rowing establishment in continuous practice. This makes the bireme, within a century of the Fall, the recognised measure of naval seriousness in the Circle Sea, and the Theocracy of Eternal Dominion adopts it as such without hesitation. Patriarchal squadrons, processional vessels, and the flagships of the crusading fleets are biremes, gilded and painted and carrying the creed's geometry on the sail, and the number a see can keep in commission is read across the whole basin as a plain statement of what that see is.

The awkwardness in this is never acknowledged and never quite absent. The bireme is the toton's pattern rebuilt in plank: its two levels of thirty files, the outrigger box that carries the upper bank, and half the vocabulary of the rowing bench are Remean, rowed one or two to an oar with lighter sweeps at a fraction of the power the pattern was drawn for, and the Theocracy that has suppressed the empire's name cannot say so. The pedigree is quietly transferred instead: the form is credited to the Anish yards that built the First Crusade's fleet, whose shipwrights had in fact been building it under imperial charter for eight centuries, and the crusading fleet becomes the origin story of a ship it had merely inherited. Educated men in every age since have noticed. Almost none have written it down.

The laminate craft, meanwhile, fares somewhat better than the polyreme and still badly. The formulae for the alchemical glues survive in fragments and in provincial workshops indifferent to their provenance, and what is reconstructed is coarser, less durable, and unsuited to a hull, though it remains serviceable for shields, chests, and small work. Hull construction across the Circle Sea reverts to plank, and reverts with it to the length limits plank imposes.

For several years after the Fall the Gyres themselves are unreliable. The short-hop coasting trade that has always been the real economic backbone of the Anish and Circulean rim barely notices, since a city trading three coves along has little cause to venture into open current at all. What suffers, badly, is exactly the long open-water traffic the Anish were proudest of, and for the better part of a decade the far-reaching ventures simply stop answering. When the Gyres settle back into their old character, the barge trade resumes as though nothing had happened, which is the first demonstration of a property that will define the next thousand years: the Circulean freight system is very nearly indestructible, because it depends on almost no skill and almost no equipment.

### What Changed

The Circulean advance of the era belongs to the foundry and not the yard, and its consequences for shipping arrive centuries late. In the second century AR the retort-distillation of zinc is recovered from scattered provincial workshops, and purified zinc yields true brass, an alloy of even and dependable grade. Everything distinctive in later Circulean engineering rests on it, and two of those things are naval: an alloy that can be cast true, bored fine, drawn into seamless tube, and trusted to hold pressure for years in damp sea air, and a sheet metal that will one day be laid over a hull's underbody to keep the worm out of it.

The advance that matters more happens in Svenu, unremarked at the time by anyone outside it, and it is the arrival of the keel and the mast together.

The two are one development and not two, because neither is worth having alone. A mast set in a keel-less shell drives the hull sideways and works the seams apart; a keel without a sail is dead weight on a rowed boat. Laid down together, with a heavy timber running the length of the bottom and a keelson bedded over it to spread the mast's thrust into the whole shell, they convert the old pulling boat into a different kind of vessel entirely. It carries a single square course on a short mast, with a spar to hold the luff out on a close reach, and it holds a course under sail without the crew touching an oar. The oars remain, for calms and for the last mile into a beach, and the boat still draws little enough to be run up onto shingle, which is the combination nothing else in the world offers.

What the keel and the sail buy is range, and range is what the age has suddenly made valuable. Reme's patrols are gone from the Gyres and the Suurian Gate, and the whole Circulean rim is open to whoever can reach it. A rowed boat cannot reach it. A boat that sails can, because a sailing crew eats and drinks at a fraction of a rowing crew's rate and can therefore stay at sea for weeks instead of days. With the sail comes the navigation it requires, and the Svenish answer is the only one that works in a cold and overcast southern sea: run out to the latitude of the destination and then hold it, east or west, needing no clock and no chart. The empire's absence made the Svenish ship. The clans themselves have never told it that way.

## The Dark Ages

### The State of the Art

Circulean shipwrighting stands at its lowest point since the Rivian Epoch, and Circulean freight stands almost exactly where it stood under the empire, which is the paradox of the age.

The barge economy carries on. It needs no master shipwright, no rigging, no ballast, and no navigator, and warlordism and fragmentation have no purchase on a system whose knowledge lives in a calendar. Grain and stone and timber move on the Gyres as they always did. What has gone is everything above that baseline: the long-range hull, the harbourworks maintained to imperial standard, the surveyed and marked approaches, and the institutional confidence that underwrote a long voyage. Anish maritime law persists, because Anish cities persist, but the ventures it once underwrote have shrunk back toward the coast.

The bireme goes with the rest, and goes for the reason it was always vulnerable. A two-banked ship is not a thing a polity owns but a thing a polity practises, and the collapse of the Hierophancy into braided obediences and rival claimants leaves no see wealthy enough or settled enough to keep a rowing establishment at sea year on year. Warlords man monoremes, which is all a warlord's revenues will stretch to, and the coasts of the age are patrolled and raided by single-banked galleys that any fishing town could have built. Biremes do not disappear so much as thin, decade by decade, into the possession of the few surviving sees that can still afford the pretence, where they are maintained at ruinous cost and rowed chiefly on feast days. The form's slow passage from instrument of war to instrument of display begins here, and is never reversed.

Into that gap sail the Svenish, with a ship only a few generations old. With Circulean shipwrighting diminished from Remean heights and not yet recovered, Svenish dragonboats command a rough-water seamanship no Circulean hull of the age can match, and Svenish raiders range deep into the Western Gyre and beyond it into the Open Ocean proper, striking coasts no Circulean captain would have approached. The advantage is not one of size or number, and never becomes one. A clan fields a ship, not a fleet; the ore will not stretch further. What the ship possesses is the combination nobody else has: a hull that crosses an ocean and then beaches on a shingle strand, a navigation that needs no instrument, and a crew whose introductions to the elemental company of open water travel with the people who hold them and do not stop at a home coast. This last is the technique the Circulean powers were structurally forbidden from adopting, since taking it up would have been apostasy, and it is the reason admiration never produced imitation.

### What Changed

Two developments, one visible and one not.

The visible one is settlement. Svenish raiding clans begin wintering over on the Keystone Isles instead of braving the return crossing twice in a season, and the practice hardens by slow degrees into settlement, marriage, and kinship with the islands' own fisherfolk. Wintering over converts a two-crossing year into a one-crossing year and thereby doubles a clan's effective reach without altering the ship in any particular, which makes it the most valuable naval development of the age and the least technical.

The invisible one is the sea tax. Brass sheathing enters use as a rich owner's fitting, and where it is laid over an iron-fastened hull it turns out to eat the fastenings beneath it with an appetite no weather explains. Svenish yards, alone in possessing both metals in the same hull in quantity, meet the problem first, and arrive at rules of thumb generations before anyone has a theory: bed the sheathing, keep the metals apart, watch the garboard. The rules are correct and nobody knows why.

## The Age of Corsairs

### The State of the Art at the Opening

At the tenth century's opening the basin possesses a bulk tradition that cannot go to sea and, beyond the Gate, two seagoing traditions that cannot carry bulk. That sentence is the whole strategic situation.

The gyre barge is unbeatable inside the Circle Sea and lethal outside it. Its flat bottom rolls viciously and pounds in a swell; it carries nothing to right it once past the angle at which its beam gives way; its leeboards strain and carry away in real water and cannot be held down on a beam sea; and without a serious rig it cannot claw off a lee shore, which is the situation that kills ships. Its masters navigate by the current and not by the sky, and that skill is worth nothing where the current ends.

The Svenish clinker hull goes anywhere and caps out at some thirty-odd metres, beyond which the technique cannot be pushed. The Ukili'ata multihull goes anywhere faster than anything afloat and carries a community instead of a cargo. Neither can be scaled to freight, and this is why the early expeditions beyond the Suurian Gate falter: Circulean masters take Circulean hulls out through the Gate and are killed by the first real swell, and those who survive the outbound leg discover that the way out was never the difficulty. The way home was.

### What Changed: the Bloom

The unlock is a route and not a hull. Svenic pilots chart the western currents and sell what they have charted, and the Stranic ports that hire them thrive. The Commonwealth's western harbours issue letters of passage; merchant-princes fund voyages and find their feet on the floors of Parliament. This is also the age in which Svenish captains make the first reliable contact with the Rim of Fire, and Svenish charts and lore give the Circulean powers both the incentive and the means to follow where Svenu has already gone.

The hull answer, when it comes, is a graft and a deliberate one. A keel and frames on the Svenish and Anish pattern are married to the transverse bulkheads that have come down the eastern rivers since prehistory, producing a vessel that is both keeled and compartmented, a combination no other conceivable history would have arrived at so early. It forces frame-first construction on yards that have built shell-first since anyone can remember, and it forces the reorganisation of the guilds that follows from that, and the arguments are bitter and last for decades.

### What Changed: the Boom

Four developments compound across the eleventh and twelfth centuries.

Brass sheathing generalises. Warm western water eats an unsheathed hull inside a season, and a sheathed hull stays out for years. This is the single technology that turns exploration into exploitation, and it is available to Arlos centuries earlier than it could have been to any iron-founding world, held back only by the cost of the metal until the crossing itself makes the cost worth paying.

Ballast ceases to be dead weight. Stone ballast costs hold volume twice over, which is ruinous for a trade that lives on cargo, and the bulkheads offer the answer once pliant-sealed doors and a serviceable pump exist to make a compartment properly tight. Water pumped into a dedicated compartment can be pumped out again, and the practice is a raider's technology before it is a merchant's, because it is tactical: ballast down for the crossing, pump out for speed and shoal draft on the raid. Yards resist it for a generation as a novel way to drown oneself.

The arcannon enters service in the twelfth century and transforms ship and wall alike. Its importance at sea lies less in the shot than in the supply: a vessel with a charging bank and a stock of bottles is self-sufficient in ammunition, carries no magazine, and cannot destroy itself. A powder fleet's cruise is measured in rounds; an arcannon cruiser's limit is food and water. Nothing else in the whole sequence does more for an economy built on commerce raiding.

The arcannon also ends the war galley, and ends it quickly. Oared vessels had held their place inside the Circle Sea long after they lost it beyond the Gate, because a near-tideless sea with long calms rewards a ship that can move without wind, and corsair squadrons of low, fast, single-banked raiders worked the Gyre routes throughout the tenth and eleventh centuries. What kills them is not speed but the bank itself: an oared hull must keep its sides open and its rowers where a shot will find them, and it cannot carry weight high without losing the shallow draft that is its whole reason for existing. Against a sailing hull that can mount arcannon low behind a closed side, the galley has no answer, and by the middle of the twelfth century it has none anywhere in the basin. What survives is the bireme, in the sees and principalities wealthy enough to row one on a feast day, now wholly a vessel of procession and no longer pretending otherwise.

And the pneumatic trades climb from novelty to civic backbone across the same centuries, which matters afloat as much as ashore. Pliant-bound casks free the cooperage from iron hoops. Pumps make bilge management a solved problem instead of the daily labour that killed wooden ships. Pliant bushings at rudder pintles and leeboard pivots end metal-on-metal wear and, incidentally, break the couple that had been eating the fastenings.

### What Changed: the Bust

Three causes converge in the thirteenth century, and they reinforce one another.

The first is the convoy. The arcannon armed the merchantman as thoroughly as it armed the raider, and once a prize can shoot back and sails in company, the raider requires a decisive margin of speed. There is none to be had.

The second is the length cap, and it is the most Arlosian of the three. Without cheap iron there is no strapping to lay under a deck, and without strapping a hull cannot be made longer. A yard that wants more ship builds fuller instead, and a fuller hull is a slower hull. The corsair economy ends when the fastest vessel anyone can build stops being meaningfully faster than the fattest.

The third is the timber bill. A deep-keel hull wants large compass timber and a great deal more of it than a barge, and the managed woodlands are already feeding the furnaces. Prices climb, Varngard and Svenish timber become strategic goods in the same century, and the marginal venture stops paying.

The formal close in the 1250s is political: King Lurvric centralises the maritime power of Lavion, leveraging Balboan expertise, and what had been a trade of free captains becomes a crown navy. The door was already swinging shut.

## The Aetheric Age

### The State of the Art

The mature deep-keel merchantman is now the standard blue-water vessel of the Circle Sea: frame-first, bulkheaded, brass-sheathed, arcannon-armed, and capped in length at a figure the yards have stopped arguing about. Inside the Gate the barge trade continues exactly as before, undisturbed by three centuries of blue-water innovation, because the Gyres still turn and the belt still runs on schedule.

What changes the ship in this age changes it from the inside.

### What Changed: the Engine Goes Afloat

The compression-expansion work cycle is demonstrated around 1225, the Law of Harmonic Conversion set down some fifteen years later, and self-harmonising resonance design formalised by the middle of the fourteenth century, at which point thermoacoustic choir engines have become the heartbeat of industry ashore. Through the late fourteenth and early fifteenth centuries the type shrinks to fit a hull. Motor launches come first, harbour craft whose sealed engines and bottled sprit make them tireless where oars and sail are not. The paddlewheeler follows and becomes the riverine and maritime standard of the basin, the wheel's steady beat mated to the engine's steady chord on every great river and along every coasting route.

The launch is, whether or not its builders thought of it that way, the heir of the oared vessel. It does the work the galley did and lost the ability to do: it moves in a calm, holds a course into a confined harbour, tows what will not move itself, and goes where wind cannot be relied upon. Within two generations of its appearance no port of any consequence works without them, and the last practical argument for keeping a bank of oars anywhere in the Circle Sea disappears. The processional biremes remain, rowed once or twice a year by men hired for the occasion, and are by this age understood by everyone including their owners as a costume worn by a state.

Two further consequences deserve separate notice. The first is that the sea is the ideal cold sink, which means a marine choir engine runs at a better ratio than the same machine on land, and the cold outlet it produces is fresh water condensed from salt, so that the oldest limit on the length of a voyage quietly disappears for any vessel with an engine in it. The second is that the engine's range is bounded by the woodlot, since sprit renews at a tree's pace and no burning stone has ever been found. There is no cheap depot network and never will be. Engine range remains a logistics problem in perpetuity, and this alone keeps sail at sea for every long crossing.

On the Mer the paddlewheel does what armed patrol boats never could. A wheel needs no fixed channel and no reliable draft, only enough water in general, which is precisely what a braided and avulsion-prone river can offer and a keeled sailing vessel never reliably could. The old asymmetry between the disciplined Hal as trunk route and the wild Mer as a specialist's corridor inverts within a generation.

### What Changed: the Tuned Hull

The alchemical pliants mature in the same century, and their arrival at the shipyard is the age's quiet revolution.

Iron does two jobs in a hull elsewhere. It holds joints rigid, and it carries tension across them. A pliant cannot do the first and is superb at the second, and Circulean shipwrighting accordingly stops pursuing rigidity altogether and pursues tune instead. Braided pliant strapping run diagonally beneath the deck and set up to a measured tension does what iron riding does on another world, by an opposite principle: the hull is not prevented from working but permitted to work within limits and return. A yard-master surveys a hull not for tightness but for tune, and slack strapping is read exactly as a slack string is read.

The rest follows down the list. Braided pliant cable serves where chain and wire would serve elsewhere, and unlike fibre it does not chafe through. Pliant-bedded seams hold under working where oakum works loose. Pliant-gasketed bulkhead doors turn compartmentation from a good idea into genuine watertight subdivision, and pliant-gasketed ports let arcannon sit low in a hull without flooding it.

The line running through this is long and largely unacknowledged: an idea first demonstrated on Ukili'ata foil struts before the Rivian Epoch, industrialised by Reme as laminate, half-lost at the Fall, recovered by the pneumatic trades, and arriving at last as the material that lets a civilisation with almost no iron put a fleet on the ocean.

### What Changed: Overhead, and Elsewhere

Pontoon airships link the major ports from the late fourteenth century, the logic of the air following that of the sea, and the aerial corridor they open across the northern jungle turns a barrier into a fare. In the closing decades of the fifteenth century the aetheric furnace, which had won alumen only in costly ounces for a century and more, at last yields it at usable cost, and a metal light enough to matter to a spar, a fitting, and a bottle, the first genuinely new metal in nearly two millennia, begins its slow entry into the rigging loft.

The two ocean peoples adapt in character. The Ukili'ata take braided pliant lashing without hesitation, since a manufactured cordage of tunable stiffness is precisely what sennit had always approximated, and it upgrades their foils in the same stroke; they take alumen for the same reason, being a tradition that wins on weight; they take sheathing bedded on pliant over the underbody only, since fastenings are the one thing a compliant hull cannot tolerate; and they take engines as auxiliaries and refuse them as prime movers, because sprit comes from a woodlot ashore and a homestead fleet's entire point is that it needs no shore. They buy charts of Circulean water freely and have never in twenty centuries drawn one of anything west of the Gate.

Svenu, by the fyrstinna's reckoning, refuses choir engines, alumen, and academy prestige alike, and takes only what improves the ship it already has: cordage, canvas, pliant bindings, brass fastenings for work that carries no load. From this century onward the Svenish edge is skill, bond, and route knowledge, and no longer hardware, and the gap begins its long narrowing. Where a Svenish house wants a Rim trader large enough to carry a cargo worth the crossing, it must build frame-first and carvel, which is to say it must build a Circulean hull and keep a Circulean yard to do it. The outer coast builds hybrids; the inner Gulf holds to clinker; and the argument between them is not about aesthetics.

## The Imperial Age

### The State of the Art

The Circulean fleet of the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries is a settled type. Deep-keeled, bulkheaded, brass-sheathed, pliant-strapped and pliant-sealed, arcannon-armed from ports that no longer flood, ballasted with water it can pump away, provisioned with water it condenses for itself, and driven by sail on the long crossings with a choir engine for harbour work, calms, and rivers. Alongside it the paddlewheeler carries the coasting and riverine trade of the whole basin, and above both the pontoon liners give way to faster flying boats built on reduced gas bags.

The barge trade continues.

### What Changed

The small engine arrives with the closing decades of the fifteenth century, sealed hearts no larger than a strongbox, then a loaf, then a fist, and with it the aethermatons that enter docks and yards in the early Imperial Age. Their effect on shipbuilding is felt first in labour and cost: a brassback hauls, lifts, and holds where a gang once did, and the timber-handling that had always set the practical pace of a yard ceases to do so. Hulls are not built larger, since the length cap has nothing to do with labour, but they are built faster and cheaper, and a crown that wants a navy in five years can have one.

The chartered company becomes the characteristic naval institution of the age. Lavion's Companies Royal hold crown monopoly over trade with distant coasts in exchange for a share of the take, keeping private fleets and private soldiery of their own, and the Commonwealth's United Chartered Companies answer them; the paper war between the two powers stays cold in the Circulean and turns hot in the Rim of Fire. Svenish houses, whose captains opened that theatre five centuries earlier and whose factor-families still hold footholds throughout it, find themselves contested in waters they discovered, by fleets they cannot match in number and vessels they can still outsail.

The age's closing development belongs to the war that ends it. Piatrium, won by the salt-bath aetheric reduction proved at Barvella in 1571, reaches the field too late and in quantities too small to weigh on any battle, but it places into Circulean hands, for the first time, a metal beyond bronze and brass: strong as fine steel at a fraction of the weight and proof against the sea. Alloyed with copper into piatrite bronze, it is the first material in the whole of the record that answers the constraint the shipwrights have worked around since prehistory, and whether it will finally break the length cap is a question the yards of the following century inherit.

## The Standing Divide

For all that changed across sixteen centuries, one fact did not.

The Circle Sea produced a bulk tradition of unmatched cheapness that could not go to sea, and the Open Ocean produced two seagoing traditions of unmatched skill that could not carry bulk. Every naval advance of the record is an attempt to make one hull that is both, and every such attempt has been expensive, capped in size, and only partly successful. The Circulean powers bought, grafted, and scaled, and ended with the tonnage. The Svenish sold the map and kept the crew, on the correct judgment that a chart is copyable and a captain with forty years of bonded water behind her is not. The Ukili'ata withheld everything, held their knowledge in chant and never in ink, stayed too small to be worth a war, and kept their margin to the present day.

And through all of it the Rim of Fire, opened to Circulean shipping by Svenish captains in the tenth century and carved into concessions by chartered companies five centuries later, remained a country that one people in the basin's own harbours had always known how to find, and had never once mentioned.
