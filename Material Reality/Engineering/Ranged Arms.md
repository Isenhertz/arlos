**Slings** remain the old republic of the poor and the pastoral. The stone sling never leaves the field; it is cheap, quiet, and needs only practice and good stones to bring down game and men alike. In settled lands the **dart sling** (staff sling and leather cup variants) rises early where stones alone will not suffice; their mass and flight are tuned by rule of thumb long before anyone writes down ratios. In the early [[Eternal Dominion|crusader]] armies that pushed south in the fight against [[Reme]], sling crews long anchored the skirmish lines because their logistics were trivial. Even in later ages, the sling resurfaces time and again, in hunting, uprisings, and delivering alchemical payloads further than the human arm can throw them.

**Javelins** follow the same arc. Peasant levies carry throwing spears because they can be made in number, usually from ash shafts and bronze heads. In the riverine fights common around the [[Circle Sea]], short boat-javelins dominate boarding actions well into the later ages, since a shaft that can be thrown cleanly can also be thrust in a crush. Reme's elite light infantry kept weighted, fletched versions for accuracy; later city militias universally favor bundles of simpler darts thrown from the battlements, and the air-to-ground combat of the early aeronaval age similarly relies predominantly on such simple kinetic projectiles, leading the way for flechette munitions.

**Bows** arrive as early as they can, and never leave. Besides the simple forms found in all societies, **laminated flatbows** thrive around the Circle Sea because the alchemical knowledge of composites is well understood, and the resulting weapon fits equally well in humid ports and dry uplands. A laminated flatbow with careful tiller and sinew-backed belly scales from dockside guard to hunter's need. **Recurves** appear wherever horse peoples ride -- the [[Zanori]] of [[Lu-An]] perfect the reflexed horn-wood bow early, and the scattered human hunter-gatherers of the great plains learn from them until whole squadrons can shoot at speed from saddle before wheeling away, their doctrine shaped by open steppe and escarpment edge. In fortress war, the **greatbow** remains a reliable harasser long into the aetheric age, lofting heavy shafts over curtains.

The **belly-bow** however belongs fully to Reme and its legions. In their hands it was a marvel -- the frame fitted to the body, the string spanned between torsion arms with such force as only a [[Shaping|Shaped]] legionary could hope to perform. Its bolts hit like a hammer, straight and brutal. When [[The Light|Lurism]] proscribes the somatic arts after Reme collapses, the weapon instantly becomes a relic. Without the Shaped bodies necessary to arm them, most frames are retired to displays, and the craft that made them is folded into the broader caution that anything relying on outlawed shaping is suspect. Later chroniclers remember its massed effect on morale more than its ballistics. 

From the same Remean stratum we hear of the **lancea lapidea or stone-lance** -- a siege-park and shipboard instrument glimpsed only through hostile crusader pens as a trough that sings a stone along its length, launched in a trajectory straighter than any arrow's. The voices that describe it are as fragmentary and pious as they are baffled, and the art is considered lost with the empire's demise. Later centuries will read those scraps and try to square them with their more advanced understanding of aetherics as, cautiously couched in modern terms, a compounded Motility modulation directed along a tuned channel so that a stoneweight slug gathered speed in abundance. The consensus remains that this lost Remean weapon, though terrific, was a narrow, costly working whose maintenance died with its practitioners.

**Petraries** are the kings of weight before aetheric siege engines take their throne. Mangonels and trebuchets appear early in Circulean stonecraft, their frames a harmony lesson in timber and rope. They endure because cities can build them from what their arsenals already hoard. In the 12th century, as arcannons make their first thunder, petraries do not vanish; they shift to counter-battery, smoke-pot and carcass work, and to throwing alchemical jars that burst in the enemy ranks from behind the walls. The lack of cheap steel only slows torsion engines a little; bronze capstans and clever winches do the rest.

**Crossbows** proliferate in two families. Tension bows carry laminated laths over simple stocks and are the standard garrison arms for most of the Dark Ages. Torsion frames exist where workshops can tend them and guilds can keep their skeins in tune; their advantage is compact power in tight streets and on tower tops. In the early Age of Corsairs, **pistol-sized torsion crossbows** bloom -- one-hand quarrelers for the closing rush when boarding or brawling, in alleys and in rigging. They will persist as officer's sidearms for their great punch in close quarters, even after airguns begin to crowd the armories. 

**Grenades** arrive by the 10th century, but they are not powder pots. Because any prepared fire-mixture invariably draws the unwanted attention of fire sprites, none is ever given consideration as a potential explosive (and in fact such mixtures are a complete unknown outside of alchemical treatises on fire sprite bait). Instead grenades are **alchemical jars** and clay apples driven by hand or sling or cup-launcher: **quicklime, smoke, pitch-fire, and spirit mix** that burns hot yet is bottled to keep fickle sprites asleep until shatter. Alchemy in the service of warfare is an art of binders and quenches; the best city arsenals learn sealants and slow-burn gels that stick without calling for the Pyre’s attention. Street fighters and marines rely on them because the blast is modest but the effect is decisive in a doorway or on a deck. 

From the 12th century forward, the arrival of the **arcannon** changes the landscape of warfare forever. At first heavy and fussy, by the 13th it becomes the standard ship armament and wall-piece alike, throwing balls in the 6- to 32-[[Measurements|pound]] class on aetheric principles that do not feed sprites the way powder would. Its heart is disciplined resonance married to coils and chambers tuned to Fulminance and Tension so that the impulse is clean and repeatable. The crews learn rites as much as drills, because sprite safety is as real as recoil. On the Circle Sea the arcannon's crack and crackle replaces the petard's slap and boom; at [[City of Balboa|Balboa proper]] the Armament Court mounts them in tiers and lines the Sea Gate with their muzzles.

**[[Air-Arms]]** become the people's answer in the Age of Corsairs. The first true soldiery versions are single-shot, hand-pumped, leather-sealed, inaccurate, but cheap. They do not smoke, they do not spark, and they drink the same breath that begins to power the cities, so logistics becomes a matter of posts and tanks rather than powder kegs. By the Golden Age of the 15th century, magazines arrive -- 6, 12, even 24 lead balls in gravity-fed tubes -- and sealed reservoirs take a fast charge from a portable hand-crank tank as readily as from a public air post. Hunters love them because they can stay cocked for long periods of waiting and fire without fuss; armies love them because training marksmanship is easy, the tool is tractable and quiet, and its logistics well-understood. After all, in a civilization where pneumatics form the civic backbone, the airgun is simply a natural extension of existing tools. In large bore, the pneumatic punt gun finds use as boarding repellent for ships of the sea and the air alike.

Alongside, the **aetherbalest** -- the ballister -- gives dedicated marksmen the reach and penetration that the airgun cannot. Its barrels are built like miniature arcannon throats, tuned for tidy impulses, but the cost of such tuned chambers, their seals, and the careful calibrations necessary to keep its harmonics operating makes it ill-fit to become standard issue. In the 14th century it finds its place with siege sappers, forward skirmishers, sharps at sea and -- later -- in the air, and enthusiasts who can afford the upkeep. Its power is real; its temperament is that of any aetheric instrument -- fussy if starved of maintenance, sublime when kept in harmony. 

By the late 15th century the **Discharge lance** appears -- a close-range arc thrower powered by Fulminance and Volatility that spits lightning across boarding hooks or alley corners. It is dangerous in all the ways that Fulminance is dangerous: discharges that will wander along wet lines; coils that breed unruly sprites if insulation is poor; human bodies and wet rigging that look the same to an impatient arc seeking the ground. As a shock instrument in boarding and breach it is terrifying; as a line weapon it is too fickle to trust except in trained hands and very short distances. The Hierophancy's old cautions about Fire and Water in volatile embrace haunt its manuals; yard regulations wrap it in more safety rite than any other hand arm.

Across all these centuries the material economy shapes the kit. Steel is rare and reserved for springs, locks, and the bones of high craft; bronze, brass, and alchemica do the bulk work. Where Fire is admitted it is admitted under seal; where resonance is asked to aid, it is asked respectfully, in weak relations that will suffer guidance. This is why the Circulean army looks as it does by the 14th century: arcannons on carriages, airguns in ranks, aetherbalests in chosen hands, grenades for the breach, bows and crossbows still in service where cost and steady behavior beat novelty. It is also why the old Remean wonders either become cautionary tales or vanish entirely -- not for lack of genius, but because the culture that rose after Reme proscribed somatic Shaping, and its arms withered for want of the bodies that once made them both feared and viable.
