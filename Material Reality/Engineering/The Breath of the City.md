### A Description of the Pneumostatic Works underneath Balboa, as an Example of Similar Such Undertakings around the Circle Sea

At the foot of the Grand Arsenale, where the tide-channels meet the river mouth of the fast-running Ertra, stands the Grand Compressorium of Balboa -- a long arcade of sea-facing towers, each crowned with wind vanes, wave-paddles, and the throats of choir engines that hum like anchored voices. These slow-turning engines draw both air and surf, their coupled thermoacoustic hearts converting heat to breath and breath to motion. They are the lungs of the city, for from them Balboa's breath -- stored air -- is gathered, harmonized, and held against the calms of night or the doldrums of summer.

By day, when the sea breeze is steady and the paddles churn, the shafts drive immense pistons that throb like the city's own pulse. The choir engines, their resonant tubes glowing with restrained heat, lend voice to this labor to smooth and multiply the compressors' yield. As the air is pressed within, it grows hot with its own struggle, and this living warmth is not wasted: through pipes of copper and fired clay, it is carried to the deep vaults known as sprite beds -- long galleries of stone and crushed coral that glow like embers when charged. Workers say the sound they make is like distant surf, a low hum and whisper as if the sea itself sighed through them.

The cooled air is guided downward into sealed caverns beneath the city, where it rests under pressure until called for again. Whenever the wind slackens, the city itself exhales. Valves open, and the confined air rushes upward through the sprite beds once more -- but now the flow is reversed. The beds, still warm with the day's gathered heat, yield their charge back into the passing air, sending it on in a lively current toward the expansion engines that drive the lifts and cranes of the city. Thus Balboa breathes even when the sea lies still.

When explaining the rhythm of the Compressorium, the engineers speak of harmonizing three flows: one of air, one of warmth, and one of tone. The last belongs to the choir engines, whose low songs maintain phase between the two others. To keep this triad in balance, the guild maintains a third storehouse, the hot-water halls. These are vast cisterns, lined with sealant and wrapped in felted kelp. When the sprite beds grow too hot, water is run through narrow pipes among the stones. The water drinks the excess heat and carries it to the public baths, the dye-vats, and the palace kitchens. In winter, that same stored warmth seeps gently through conduits beneath the cobbles, giving every quarter of the city a share and ensuring that the streets stay miraculously free of sleet or ice.

It is said that no two nights sound alike in the Compressorium. The towers groan as they ease their burden, the sprite beds murmur and release their warmth, and the air sighs through the ducts in deep rhythm. The choir engines hum their sustained notes, their brass throats glowing faintly in the dark. Emberlings laze on the glow, curling on the hot stone and purring like kettles; Snapsingers cling to the outer pipes, their needling song warning the keepers to distract them with heated ceramic shards that they may crackle and snap instead -- small courtesies that keep the sprites from larger mischief.

By dawn the caverns lie spent, the stones cooled, the waters tepid. Then, as the morning wind fills the vanes and the paddles stir again, the choir throats catch their first note, the pistons rise, and Balboa draws her first great breath of the day. The hidden heart of the city begins to beat anew -- a harmony of air, heat, and sound that lets her live without the smoke of coal or the reek of oil.
