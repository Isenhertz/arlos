# On the Taming of the Charge of Air and Ice

## The Charge in the World

Of the two Charges that make up their Parallelism, Tension -- the old pull between Stone and Fire -- was always the better-behaved sibling. It sits quietly in lodestone and forge-lore, and the smiths have kept its courtesies for as long as anyone has worked metal. Fulminance, the Charge held between Air and Ice, was known just as long and trusted far less. It is the crackle in amber rubbed on fur, the bite of a brass latch on a frost-dry morning, the pale fire that walks a mast-top in heavy weather, and, at its full and terrible expression, the lightning itself. Folk observation had the attribution right centuries before any academy confirmed it: the spark bites hardest in cold, dry air, and thunder and hail ride the same storms. Whatever the Charge of Air and Ice was, the world made no secret of which vertices it hung between.

What the world did not have was a cheap way to hold it. Every other force the Circulean crafts had learned to husband -- heat through the choir engine, pressure through the bottle and the main, lift through the modulated Bond of Soil and Air -- had yielded to patient approach, and Fulminance yielded too, after its fashion, long before anyone could say why. It yielded in two forms that shared little beyond the name of the Charge. The first was chemical: small, weak, and forgiving, and kept for a century in the academies' cabinets. The second was aetheric: strong, exacting, and dangerous, and kept in the furnace works and the coilwrights' shops by the few who could hold it. Between the trivial prickle and the catastrophic stroke there was room for both, and for a long time nobody could say what held either one.

## Why the Sky Was Left Alone

Inquiry was retarded less by difficulty than by the smell of the subject. Lightning is the stride of the Noble Element of Air, and the Flash-Strider's history with those who presumed upon her is the oldest cautionary matter in the historical record: named and held captive once by the First-and-Only, freed as Chainbreaker, and unforgetting since. Behind that memory stands the nearer one -- a Collegium that reached for a Noble Element and pulled down its own civilisation in the reaching. Between the two, no tradition of storm-harvest ever formed anywhere around the Circle Sea, and no respectable scholar proposed one. To raise a snare for lightning was understood, without needing to be argued, as an attempt to force the hand of a Noble who is not called Flash-Strider for nothing.

The consequence, unremarked at the time, was that when Fulminance was tamed it was tamed from below: not caught out of the sky but raised, in small quantities from metal and brine in the cabinet and in great ones by a sustained working in the sealed vessels of the furnace works, by men whose stated business was glass, metals, and the arcannon founders' coils.

## The Cabinet and the Coil

The chemical form came first, and came small. By the 1420s the cabinets of the Grand Academy of the Broken Isles held vessels in which two dissimilar metals, joined through brine or a soaked cloth, gave up a faint and steady charge for as long as the metals lasted. The crude cell lit nothing and moved nothing. The Materiality college studied it for what pliant sealants and organic catalysts did to its endurance, and the cells passed between cabinets as specimens, much as lodestones and amber had done before them.

The cell became a trade article when the cabinet-makers learned that cells joined in series added their pushes together, and that a column sealed in pitch and pliant could be carried. By 1540 the field-grade cell was sold in the western harbours of the Commonwealth and had reached Stranu, bought by yards and surveyors for work that wanted a small steady charge without a hose or a furnace: the proving of seams, the lighting of holds and bilges where an open flame invites the sprites of Fire, the sounding of a signal where no air line ran. It was understood well enough by then that no yard master thought it remarkable. It was weak, it ran down, and it could not be recharged; a spent column went back to the maker, who stripped it and built it again.

The aetheric form grew out of the hexcrafters' study of the Parallelism of Charges. A coil of drawn wire, varnished with insular gum and wound about a core, could be made to hold a working charge of Fulminance and to raise Tension in the core as it did so. It stored in great measure what a cell only made in small, and it was charged by a working of the Charge held against it, never by chemistry. In the cabinets of the 1420s it was the Tension-coil, an experiment in storing and carrying a working by aetheric means alone. In the arsenals and arcannon yards the same art had been in daily use since the twelfth century, where founders wound and proofed the coils that gave an arcannon its clean impulse, and the coilwrights' shops that grew up beside them carried the knowledge forward.

For its first century the coil's civil work was small. Fluidic logic spoke in breath: a channel pressurised or vented, a valve leaning one way or the other. From the early sixteenth century the coilwrights of the western Principalities gave the same decisions a second tongue, a pulse of Fulminance along a wire to a coil about an iron core, which drew a valve's pin faster than a breath could travel a tube. Brassback actuators of the period took their orders in breath and, increasingly, their triggers as pulses, while their power still came as air from the choir engine at the breastplate.

The change came in the 1550s. Commonwealth coilwrights wound a coil strong enough to drive a lodestone-cored plunger and, with it, a compressor, and the Chartered Companies, whose yards and Rim of Fire stations had every reason to want hands that did not tire, ordered a new generation of brassbacks built around it with no choir engine at all. Such a machine took no fuel, burned no flame for the sprites of Fire to find, and ran its shift on a charged coil exchanged at the charge-house as an air bottle is exchanged at a public point. The choir-hearted pattern survived beyond the Companies' yards, in Lavion above all, where officers who disliked the machines saw no reason to improve them.

## The Furnace Cities

A coil of that strength wanted a source of corresponding strength to charge it, and in the sixteenth century only one kind of installation could supply it. The aetheric furnace that won alumen from common earths, and in a few yards steel in the arc-furnace, ran a large and sustained working on the Charge of Air and Ice behind sealed walls, under artificers who had spent generations learning to hold the most temperamental relation on the hexagon steady for days together. They described it in the vocabulary of bath and burden. The academies would later give it its proper name. What a works did not need for its own bath it could spare, and the cities that owned a furnace came to own a charge-house, a licensed hall where coils were charged by the hour at the works' own price.

The furnaces themselves were few. Lavion built first and built most: twice as many as the Commonwealth through the long peace, and three times as many by the Great War, which kept its lead in alumen intact. The Commonwealth raised a handful between the first usable alumen and the Undeclared War, and no Principality that held one let it be forgotten. Barvella built hers beside the finest arcannon foundries outside the Academy yards, to charge and proof the coils that gave their guns a clean impulse. The molten-bath practice its artificers worked out there, distinct in lineage from the alumen furnace, was what Parliament's war administration found waiting when it convened the Barvella Project in the first years of the Great War. The older uses still stood in Lavion's orbit: the arc-furnaces of the Royal Works at Virell ran lines into the service galleries of the great bridges, and Balboa's Ascensori hauled their cars up the Via Salita on the arsenal's surplus.

Away from a furnace the charge had to be raised by hand. A Company station in the Rim of Fire kept its own artificers, who held a working against a coil through long shifts at their own cost in health and in sprites, and a station kept as many machines in the field as it had artificers to feed. The leash was short everywhere and shortest overseas.

Where a furnace city's lines ran out of its galleries, the first fulminant telegraph followed them. From the 1540s a clerk at the works could pulse a message along a line to the yards, the citadels and the harbour works a morning's ride away, and have it answered within the hour. Where no gallery ran, the line went overhead on poles, a sprite-lure and a mark of haste that every city engineer deplored, and storms cut it as often as enemies did. It was a local instrument, one furnace city and its outliers, never a network.

Public lighting came from the same lines, and came as towers. A light-tower in a furnace city was a mast of brass-bound stone or lattice many storeys high, crowned with a bank of sealed glow-wire lamps in airless glass, lighting a whole ward from above. No open arc was admitted. The glass was proved against the sprites of Air and Ice before a mast was raised, and a grounded rod stood above every lamp. Balboa fitted its airship pylons and sky beacons with the same banks, and Virell raised towers over the Royal Works and the Docks. The small street lamp and the domestic bulb waited for the sealed cell.

## The Tracing of the Charges

The explanation came with the Peace. The practice of the Charges was old and scattered: in the coilwrights' shops, the furnace works and the academy cabinets, each guild held a fragment and kept it. The congress of 1574 filled Balboa with the natural philosophers of every delegation, and the city's older instinct for being indispensable did the rest. Within a few years Balboa was licensing the collected fragments abroad on the same terms as any other Balboan expertise, and the tracing of the Charges passed from the guilds' benches into the common property of the academies.

What followed was rapid, because the practice had long been waiting for its theory. The Parallelism of Charges had stood in the hexal account for generations: Tension and Fulminance as siblings of one force, and the settled doctrine that disturbing one member of a paired force provokes an answer in its partner. The coilwrights had never needed the doctrine to wind a coil about iron and watch it answer. The academies, once the guilds' fragments were laid before them, went looking for the law behind the answer, and found it exactly where the geometry said it must be. A lodestone driven through a wound coil of drawn wire raises a Fulminant current in the wire; a Fulminant current driven through a coil wound about soft iron raises Tension in the iron. Both results were set down in print within a few years of the Peace, the first time the hexal account named what the coilwrights' benches had long done.

The quantitative laws came in the same generation, sibling texts to the Law of Harmonic Conversion: the fixed proportion between a column's push, the resistance of the path, and the current that flows; the fixed exchange between current passed and metal moved in a bath. That second law delivered the tracing's crowning recognition. The aetheric furnace, which had been winning alumen from common earths for two centuries, and at usable cost for one, by a sustained working through a molten bath, was measured and found to have been a Fulminant engine all along -- its working a current, its yield governed by the same exchange as any plating vat. Two hundred years of alumen, smelted without incident or offence, became overnight the tamed Charge's oldest and most reassuring credential: whatever Fulminance raised from a bath might be, it was demonstrably not a theft from the sky, for no sky had ever objected.

The first industries followed the furnace's example rather than the cell's: refining vats that deliver copper of a purity no fire-refiner can match, and plating baths that lay true brass, silver, or gold onto baser work in films of any fineness. The furnace cities had run such vats and baths on their surplus lines since the early sixteenth century, in small and jealous shops; the laws opened them to every yard that could buy a column of cells, and for a decade the tamed Charge was, to the trades, chiefly a new kind of metallurgy.

## Natrium and the Quarrels

It was the molten bath that produced the crisis. In the first years of the sixteenth century a furnace works, driving its working through molten common salt in place of its usual earths, won from the salt a metal no fire had ever loosened from it: soft as wax, light enough to float, bright as silver at the cut -- and igniting at the touch of water, burning with a violence that scattered the bath and the bench alike. The metal was named natrium, and its behaviour was received by the schools as something close to an affront. Here was a Stone-metal from which Fire leapt at Water's approach: a substance that seemed, on its face, to hold court with the wrong vertices entirely.

For the whole of those years natrium was a hazard before it was a metal. The works that raised it kept it under oil, sold it to no one, and counted a bath spent on salt a bath lost, since furnace time was the scarcest coin that Lavion or the Commonwealth owned.

The quarrels that followed occupied the academies for the better part of a century and are remembered without much affection. They came in three phases, and the questions changed between them. In the first, which ran to the 1540s, strict Fractalists held that nothing was wrong with the world, only with the degree-tables, and fought one another to exhaustion over rival recomputations of the metal's composition, each claimed as authoritative to the last minute of arc. No authority could rule between them, for the Church stood divided by Roburic's rupture and no council sat with the standing to hear the matter. In the second, as the first combatants died and their chairs passed, a younger school argued that natrium was not scandalous but born charged -- that a metal delivered by current carries the imbalance of its delivery within it, and merely repays that debt, through Volatility, at Water's touch -- a reading with the advantage of matching the bench and the disadvantage of issuing from men without chairs, until the chairs fell vacant. Antidiscordian voices throughout read the affair as plain warning: that the current had pried loose what the hexagon had sealed, and that a substance so disharmonious was itself the argument for stopping. And the mechanistic school, which had stood at its zenith in the pre-war academies and fallen with the lineage its impersonal idiom had nourished, entered the quarrels a diminished and unwelcome voice: its submission that the entire controversy was an artifact of speaking of the six as persons with characters capable of being violated, and that impersonal quantities would dissolve the paradox overnight, was heard less on its merits than through its associations, the idiom of mere quantities being, in most minds, inseparable from the men who had untethered Santra. The third phase was the war's, and it ended the quarrel by removing a party to it: the Desolation of Santra and the purge that followed silenced the mechanists, and the academies that reopened after the Peace had no appetite for the old combatants. The matter settled slowly and unevenly, and the durable synthesis owes less to any single school than to the accumulating discipline of the bench -- though it owes the disgraced mechanists rather more than anyone at the time cared to credit, and their quiet survival at the margins of the faculties dates from these years. The reputations made and unmade in the quarrels shaped the following generation more than the metal ever did.

## Faith and the Flash-Strider

The theological question was put early and answered late. Before the Peace nobody with the standing to answer it was placed to do so. The modulated installations of the Aetheric Age had been licensed by crown or council one at a time and kept sealed, since a working on the Charge of Air and Ice lays hands on Air itself, and the coil and the furnace stood on that same narrow ground. But the Church was divided by Roburic's rupture, the Chartered Companies licensed themselves, and the war gave no one leisure to object. The coil went into the field in a brassback's frame unruled, and the rulings, when they came, found a practice already standing. The coil, whose charging does lay hands on Air, was suffered on the old terms and licensed by the charge-house where it was charged. A cell addresses no Air at all: its working parts are metal, brine, and salt, its motive force the Reactivity of the couple, and the Fulminance that flows in the outer path is the Parallelism balancing itself -- no more a compulsion of the Flash-Strider than a forge is a compulsion of the Hearthkeeper. The rulings that followed drew the line where the craft had already drawn it: what is raised from the workbench is licit; what would be seized from the sky is not. No snare, tower, kite, or vessel may be raised for the purpose of harvesting the stroke, and no reputable practitioner has proposed one.

The one apparatus that approached the line was the guest vane: the tall grounded rod raised above spire and tower, offering the stroke a clean road to earth so that it need not smash the chimney on its way. The light-towers of the furnace cities had carried one above every lamp for a generation before the question was asked. Whether such a rod was snare or courtesy was briefly and genuinely scrutinised; but the courtesy reading -- a guest-path offered, never a trap laid, taking nothing and keeping nothing -- had the sextons of every storm-country on its side, and any sexton would rather the spires kept standing. Acceptance came quickly, and the guest vane is now as unremarkable on a chapterhouse as a weathercock.

## The Quiet Craft

If piety drew the boundary of the craft, the sprites drew its character. It is settled workshop doctrine that concentrated Fulminance in the open air is bait for the sprites of Air and Ice, exactly as burning powders once baited Fire's sprites into rendering them unusable: an open arc is a standing invitation, a spark-gap a dinner bell, and an unshielded oscillating current a song sung where the sky can hear it. The fulminant crafts accordingly grew up sealed, submerged, buried, and above all quiet. Contacts are made under oil or brine; currents run direct and steady in thick, enclosed conductors; nothing is permitted to spark, hum, or glow in open air. Where an oscillating current cannot be avoided -- as at the throat of a generating engine -- it is confined within the machine's own casing and rectified at the terminals, most commonly through the alumen cell, a bath-and-plate device that passes current one way and refuses it the other, and whose dependence on alumen closes a circle the trade finds pleasing.

The same doctrine settled the shape of the mains. Fulminant conduit goes underground, threaded through the pneumatic galleries whose rights-of-way already vein every mature Circulean city, and no line is strung overhead where a gallery can be had: a wire in the sky is at once a sprite-lure, a slow act of rudeness toward the Flash-Strider, and an advertisement of poverty. Overhead stringing survives only where the pneumatic baseline itself is wanting -- in shantytowns, frontier outposts, and the handful of polities that slept through the pneumatic centuries -- and the sagging pole-line has become, throughout the Circulean world, as legible a mark of the periphery as an open ditch.

Generation itself came from the engine the culture already trusted. A brushed and sparking wheel-machine was never seriously entertained; instead the choir engine, whose resonating throat is itself an oscillation, was married to the coil directly, a lodestone-cored plunger driven through a wound coil by the engine's own thermoacoustic pulse, sealed within the casing, sparkless by construction. The choir-alternator made every existing charging station a candidate fulminant works, and its raw output, rectified through alumen cells, feeds the mains as steady current.

## The Uneven Map of the Turn

The fulminant turn of the seventeenth century did not come evenly, and its geography says as much about the Great War's aftermath as about the craft itself. Balboa, the science's cradle, made of it what Balboa has always made of knowledge: a licensing trade, its arsenal-descended houses selling expertise, instruments, and certified corrosion-men to every yard and academy on the Circle Sea. Vel Kiriya took up the craft on principle as much as profit: a power that had refused the pipe jet on the ground that no machine should feed on the lesser kin of the elements found, in the stack, a motive force that burns nothing and consumes no one -- the bloodless power, in the phrase of its own Judges -- and scaled it with a convert's thoroughness. The Commonwealth followed as fast as its rubble allowed, and turned the rubble itself to advantage: districts rebuilt after the war were laid out with fulminant conduit in their new pneumatic galleries from the first course of brick, so that the reconstruction cities came up wired as older, intact cities could not affordably become, and the war's worst-hurt provinces entered the new century, by that one measure, ahead of the capitals that had escaped it.

Varsenne abstained. The constitutional monarchy that emerged from the Compact had watched a suppressed lineage of natural philosophers deliver first the pipe jet and then the Desolation, and had purged them in earnest; for a generation afterward, natural philosophy itself carried the smell of that lineage, and no ministry cared to be seen funding its next ambition. The kingdom kept its choir engines and its pneumatic mastery, watched the fulminant turn pass it by, and came late to the craft it might once have led -- buying back, in licences from Balboa, what its own academies had declined to build.

## The Stack and the Cell

At the district scale the mature form is the fulminance stack: banks of great rechargeable cells, their working metal natrium held in salts and returned to them, charged from the choir-alternators and discharged into the mains as demand asks. The warm-running chemistries are kept at temperature by the same heat vaults that have warmed charging-station districts since the pneumatic centuries, an economy of siting that made the existing stations the natural hosts of the new works. The stack suits the Circulean grid because the Circulean grid was born from a storage culture: a civilisation that has exchanged charged bottles at public points for four hundred years required no persuasion that power should be banked centrally and drawn as needed, and its fulminant mains were buffered by stored charge from the first year of their operation.

Below the district scale the same chemistry shrank, and quickly. The sealed fulminant cell -- exchanged at public points precisely as air bottles are, and increasingly recharged in place -- now drives hand tools, lamps, carriage-starters, and small machinery that once hung from the low-pressure main, and lights homes and streets through the glow-wire lamp, a sealed filament brought to steady incandescence in an airless bulb: a hearth without breath, sparkless, songless, and inoffensive to sky and sprite alike. Along the buried mains runs the fulminant telegraph, retiring the valve-code beacon lines on every route the conduit reaches, and in the newest workshops the fluidic logic of the pressure-clock era has begun to hybridise with the fulminant relay, valve and coil each doing what it does best.

The cell's deepest significance, however, is what it does not need. A choir engine, however small it is made, must still be fed; the cell asks only to be filled, from any main, any stack, any alternator, anywhere. In the cell the Circulean world gained its first motive vector wholly independent of fuel at the point of use -- a development whose consequences, at this writing, have scarcely begun to arrive.
