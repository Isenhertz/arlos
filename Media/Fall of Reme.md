The siege had been coming for a while, and the city had withdrawn into its limits. Reme, shining mistress of the Circulean for a thousand years, had recalled her legions from every frontier of the [[Circle Sea]] to man her walls, and the city breathed with the terrible, disciplined patience of an empire that knew it was facing its greatest challenge. But the granaries were full, the aqueducts ran clean, and the [[Shaping|Shapers]] had gone to work on the garrison, the last great flowering of their art turned not toward conquest but toward defense -- bones knit to iron hardness, sinew wound tight as ship's cable, eyes sharpened to see through smoke and night. Reme was, by every measure the city could take, prepared.

Only a handful within the Collegium understood what the Capitolinae had sanctioned in the deep vaults beneath the Emberium -- that in the desperation of a siege with no clear end, the boldest and most frightened of the Empire's ruling class had turned from the flesh of soldiers to the flesh of the world itself, seeking to bind the [[Noble Element]] of Fire as they had once bound muscle and bone. The Hearthkeeper, they reasoned, was no god to be worshipped but a force to be Shaped, as everything in Reme could be Shaped, given mastery enough and will enough. It was Reme's oldest claim dressed in the robes of necessity: that mastery over the body implied mastery over all things, that the Empire's genius knew no boundary it could not eventually cross. It was heresy even to their own beliefs, their old covenant with the Noble Element, who sheltered the city from the flows of the shield volcanoes dotting the coast, but over the course of a hundred years of being on the back foot, fighting retreat after retreat, losing first the western colonies and then the northern tributaries to a rising theocracy, any voices who called for calm and measure found they were no longer the ones who were heeded.

The people of the city knew nothing of this. To them, the Emberium served as the great temple of the hearth-fire, the place where the sacred flame that warmed every household in Reme was said to have its single, undying source. Parents sent children with offerings of oil and resin before dawn. Soldiers touched the temple's outer wall for luck before their watch. The philosophers who wrote of Right Form and Right Function did not trouble ordinary Remeans with talk of covenants and bindings; such things were the province of Shapers and diviners, spoken of in vaults, not in kitchens or barracks or the shaded colonnades where elders played at counters and argued about grain prices.

It came first as an absence. The gulls that wheeled endlessly over the harbor stopped their crying, all at once, as though a hand had closed over every throat in the sky. Dogs that had barked at the siege lines for three weeks fell to a crouch and would not be coaxed to their feet. In the Emberium itself, deep in the vault where the ritual proceeded, the officiating adjuncts felt the floor breathe beneath them -- not shake, not yet, but breathe, as though the mountain that cradled the city had drawn in air for the first time in a thousand years.

Then the ground lurched, and the world above ceased to be ordinary.

In the markets, urns of oil toppled and shattered across the flagstones. In the barracks nearest the walls, the legionariae braced for a siege engine's strike that never manifested, confused by a violence with no visible author. Up on the walls themselves the finest of Reme's Shaped soldiers,  ready to break the crusader host upon their own bodies, citizens who could shrug off blade-strokes and outrun horses, found themselves thrown flat by a force that overruled any anatomy, however perfected. The sky, which had been a hard bright noon only a breath before, went black; not clouded, not stormed, but extinguished, as if someone had reached up and smothered the sun with a great and merciless palm.

There was no warning cry, because there was nothing visible to the naked eye. There was only the sound that followed: a single, world-splitting concussion, as though the earth itself had been struck like a bell, and every soul in Reme felt it in their teeth and their marrow before they understood it in their minds.

Ash began to fall before the screaming started. It fell in sheets, fine and grey and endless, and within moments the proud streets of the world's mightiest city were reduced to groping half-light, strangers colliding with strangers, parents screaming names into a haze that swallowed sound as readily as sight. On the walls, the legions who had been Reme's answer to any siege -- women Shaped past the frailty of ordinary flesh, trained across a hundred campaigns, the finest expression of a thousand years of the Collegium's genius -- found their perfected bodies as helpless against what came next as any commoner's.

Because the mountain did not merely erupt; it answered in retribution to a blasphemy, and the answer was annihilation.

Those closest to the Emberium would have been the only witnesses, and none of them lived to tell it beyond the moment. But they would have seen, in the last moments before the Fall, a great face in the boiling black above the caldera. Not a face as mortals have faces, but a suggestion of one wrought in flame and pressure and unbearable weight, a frown vast enough to eclipse the ruin of the sky. They would have seen a form descending from that opened wound in the mountain, crowned in fire, clothed in the ashes of a toga as if in savage mockery of the very people it had come to unmake. The Hearthkeeper did not run. She -- for the Remeans naturally thought of the elements in exclusively female terms, just as they did of themselves -- did not rage as flame rages, wild and hungry. She came the way lava comes, patient, absolute, unrefusable, and where she passed, marble bowed and stone remembered it had once been fire. By the time she alighted on the courtyard by the Emberium, there were no lungs left to scream, no eyes left to see. The Scourge had come to the city, and the city buckled.

The soldiers on the walls, deathless in every way Reme's art could make them, burned regardless. Shaped hearts stopped in Shaped chests. Iron-hard sinew blackened and curled like any ordinary thing. Whatever the Collegium had achieved in its centuries of perfecting the body, it had never sought to Shape flesh to withstand the full divine fury of a god-element, and in the space of a few burning minutes the greatest concentration of martial mastery the world had ever assembled -- the last legions, recalled from every province to make their stand -- ceased to exist, not as defeated soldiers, but as ash indistinguishable from the ash of the bakers and weavers and children who fell beside them. A city of such scale as would not be seen in the world for another thousand years, more than a million souls sheltered within towering walls raised in perfect understanding of harmonic ratios and the volcanic landscape, turned into slag and acrid smoke in the span of moments.

No crusader witnessed it. Far to the north, the crusading fleets were weeks out at sea, their prows set for the Rush and the long-planned sack of Balboa as the start of their land-based campaign for Reme proper. It was there, in open water, that the fleets felt the world shudder through the hulls of their own ships -- the Western Gyre, which had turned in its slow and patient churn since time out of mind, wrenched sideways by the violence unleashed upon the coast to the south. Currents that had never wavered in living memory seized the fleets and dragged them far off their reckoning, scattering formations, snapping cables, driving prows south and west past the Ertran Peninsula. By the time the crusaders fought their ships back to any coast at all, Balboa lay far to their north. The Gyres were fully stilled, and would remain so for years; some even say it was the by the will of the Empress of Fathoms herself, the Guardian of Gyres mourning the loss of a favoured people.

When the crusaders finally came ashore and marched east over unfamiliar ground to find their bearings, they found, days later, a devastation with no name. Where a city had stood, a mountain now stood in its place, moved bodily down the coast in a single searing conflagration, its new slopes still ticking and settling, silent but for the wind. No walls. No gates. No enemy left to besiege, and no glory to claim in the taking, for there was nothing left to take.

They did not understand what they were looking at. They knew only that they had set out to break Reme by siege and had arrived to find Reme already broken by something far larger than any army, and so, lacking any other word for it, the crusaders called it divine vengeance, enacted on their behalf by a hand greater than their own, and that is what the story became, in the end.

Inside those vanished walls, in the last minutes before the mountain took its final shape, there had been no glory at all. There had only been a populace who had gone to bed the night before rehearsing which cellar to shelter in in case of arrow-fire, waking instead to the end of the world with no notion of what had been done in their name, because they had done nothing at all. They had lit their hearth-fires and murmured their small devotions to a temple they trusted, tended by masters they never thought to question, and the reckoning that fell upon them was for a sin they never knew had been committed on their behalf.

And so Reme fell. Not to siege, not to military genius or the righteousness of an enemy's cause. It fell but to a single, unimaginable rebuke that took with it, in one unwitnessed afternoon, everything the world had ever known of Shaping the flesh into glory.
