### **Excerpt from a Proscribed Manuscript, recovered in fragments from the Exarchical Library at Sillaire**

_Author unknown; presumed late 14th, early 15th century AR. Manuscript declared Apocryphal and Subversive._

It has long been accepted within orthodox Lurism that the practice of depicting saints as they were in life -- not as idealized figures but as weathered, wounded, limping, or marked -- represents a doctrinal fidelity to the Light's disdain for vanity, illusion, and false harmony. This, we are told, reflects a core tenet: that resonance is not beauty, and that truth echoes loudest through imperfection honestly borne.

And yet...

There exists the possibility, troubling though it may be, that this iconographic turn, which rose to prominence in the closing century of the First Hierophancy, may not be original to the faith at all.

One need only compare the terracotta funerary busts recovered from southern Ertra, many of which survived the Scourge of Reme, with the earliest sanctioned depictions of Saint Karessa of the Hollow Voice. The resemblance in gesture, modeling, and psychological austerity is… uncanny.

In Reme, portraiture was not a tool of aggrandizement, but a ritual of ethical transparency. To depict the wart, the twisted brow, the softened eye, was not indulgence -- it was declaration: _I have suffered, and remained myself._ The sculpture was not a mask but a map of experience.

If our saints are now rendered with broken teeth and swollen knees, we must ask whether we do this because the Light demands such reflection, or because our hands were trained by fallen masters to carve what is real.
