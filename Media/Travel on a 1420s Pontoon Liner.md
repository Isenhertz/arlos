*You arrive by water, as everyone does. The aeronaval behemoth sits out in the harbour like a patient whale -- long, pale envelope above, boat-bellied gondola below, twin pontoons flanking her with neat spray rails. A launch noses you to the floating pier. Dockhands in blue jerkins pass up lines, and a steward in cream linen checks your ticket under a striped awning that smells of orange oil and varnish. The bell strikes the half hour. Overhead the props idle with a beehive hum while the choir engines warm -- a civilized throb that speaks to you through the soles of your shoes.*

*A gangway rises on a double hinge and you step aboard into the **forward vestibule**, where brass rails curve like a river and the floor is hardwood laid in a wave pattern. To port, a glass case displays the route -- Virell to Balboa with polite stops -- and a barometer trimmed in gilt. A steward tips his cap and relieves you of your trunk with a practiced heave. A faint breath of cool, dry air slides past your cheek as the **air pumps** go about their quiet business.*

*Along the **promenade deck**, a run of oculi gives you the sea sliding away, green and silver. Wicker chairs bear striped cushions; a tea cart trundles past with cups that have little sunbursts painted inside the rims. The promenade wraps the gondola like a veranda, then opens into the **grand saloon**, which wants to be a riverboat's music room and almost is. Mahogany columns with nickel caps, a pianoforte lashed snugly against a bulkhead, and a carpet that yields underfoot just enough to say money without shouting it. Luncheon is set for second bell: cold salmon, pickles, omelettes, rolls warm enough to steam when broken. A boy with awe in his eyes leans at the rail and stares down at the pontoon step, already imagining the leap into the air.*

*Your **cabin** is compact and clever. Two berths, brass hooks, a writing shelf that folds down on a ratchet, and a washstand with cut-glass tumblers tethered by neat chains. The window has a leather strap and, when you draw it open, the envelope fills half the view like a pale hill. Somewhere between decks a steward whistles two notes, answered by a soft chime. All over the ship the day tightens into motion.*

*Curiosity takes you forward to the **bridge**, where the flight-master stands at a wheel bound in cord like a tiller. The air pilot, a local licensed for these waters, leans out to read the cat's-paws on the harbor lane. A chart table rides on gimbals; a speaking tube waits beside a row of signal toggles. The pilot rings one bell and you feel the engines climb to a firmer tone. Out through the windows the floating pier is already falling astern. A boatswain's whistle sings a descending interval -- the house signal for cast-off -- and crew on the pontoons coil lines as easily as if this were any harbor craft.*

*The liner takes the water for her run out of the roadstead. A bell, a soft call, and you feel the **Rigidity modulators** come on in the keel, a prickle of stiffness filtering up through the floor and causing guests on their first trip to draw a sharp breath of surprise. The aeronave seems to be poised for the leap the same way a dancer gathers herself, then the propellers rise in pitch. The pontoons kiss the lane, then skim; spray spears away and patters the windows in bright lines. Children gasp; old sailors nod their approval. The step breaks cleanly, a hush follows that feels like a held breath, and then the fieldmaster eases a hair of additional Attraction into the tractite panels and the whole ship sighs upward, not as a lark leaps but as a good horse rises into a canter. The harbor buoys slide aft; enraged quay skulks wheel away to return to the promenade; Balboa's news sheets in their slotted rack tremble and settle again.*

*From here a narrow stair leads up into the **catwalk**, a spine that runs the length of the hull beneath the envelope. Only escorted passengers may go, but your timing is good and the rigger chief has a generous streak. You keep to the painted line and feel the ship's long bones underfoot -- laminated arches, bronze nodes, pneumatic runs gleaming like harp strings. Through discrete ports you glimpse the **gas cells** swaying slightly in their bays, panel-seamed linen with a sheen of alchemica varnish. Ballonet telltales flutter red and green. It smells of clean resin, hot rope, and the salt that creeps everywhere at sea.*

*Aft again, you ask leave to see the **engine gallery**. The giant choir engines sit in a bright, tidy room behind wire guards -- no coal, no smoke, only the concentrated smell of spirits and the hum of harmonious tones being put to work. Flywheels turn under leather shrouds; dynamos purr; slotted commutators throw tiny comets that a careful steward wipes away with a sure hand. The chief explains, proud but not boastful, that everything heavy lives close to the keel for manners on the water; that the props sit on outriggers where their slipstreams do not beat the hull; that a touch of Purity modulation on the intake filters keeps away the salt haze; and that Attraction, when modulated at all, is used like a gentleman uses a walking stick -- helpful, yes, but never leaned on.*

*You wander aft to the **mail room**, all pigeonholes and stamps, where clerks sort letters for every port on the card. A glass door opens to the **smoking lounge**, a handsome box of paneling and brass spittoons where the talk is of trade, theater, and how much faster this is than the packet boat if the weather smiles. The steward brings chocolate to a mother and shows her how the cup sits in a soft ring so it will not dare to slop.*

*Afternoon the **reading room** opens its doors -- gazettes from Virell, pamphlets from Gaviatolis, the inevitable sermons, and a brass-bounded map where colored lines show other liners in the system. Children discover the **observation oriel** set low to watch the water peel away in sheets. A steward takes the shy ones by the hand and shows them how to read whitecaps for wind. In the **galley**, copper pans hang like medals and the cook balances a pot as if he were at sea, which in his heart he is.*

*At second dog-watch, a rumor of weather slides in with the dusk. You feel it first in the props -- a new undertone, a push against pressure. The pilot and flight-master share a look, but no one hurries because they never do. The signal toggles click; riggers haul inside from working the envelope lines; the bell sounds once, then twice. Tea things disappear and are discreetly placed in upholstered cabinets as the stewards point out the grips and grabrails. The fieldmaster in the engine gallery lays a hand on the governor and smooths it further. Rain rattles the promenade windows like thrown rice and then eases into pitter-patter. The liner does what she was built to do: take a little weather, show a little patience, and not spill a drop of someone's soup.*

*Evening in the **grand saloon** is all soft light and polished laughter. A trio plays a pavane. The lamps over each table wear little shades painted with fish and ships and suns. The captain makes a slow round and says nothing about speed or schedules, only asks after comfort and points out a lighthouse miles off like a candle in a far room. Someone proposes a toast to the engine boys; someone else to the riggers. A clerk ordinarily afraid of heights looks down at his hands and realizes they have stopped trembling.*

*You find your berth when the bell rings ten. The ship settles into her long-night voice -- dull, reassuring machinery, the hush of air along the skins, the ministering footsteps of stewards. Sleep comes as it does in a good inn. If you wake near dawn you may catch the **alighting**: a gentle slackening, frames braced again, a kiss to the water that shivers the floor and is gone. Deckhands murmur; capstan pawls clack; a pilot's whistle marks the turn into the buoyed lane. The oculi go from black to black-and-silver to the tender green of a harbor at morning.*

*Breakfast smells of coffee, orange peel, and fresh bread. The promenade fills with people in travel clothes that will, later, mean a story. Out the window, the city stands up out of its reflections and the seawall beckons. Lines are passed. The bell speaks one last time. You disembark to the same floating pier, and for an instant you look back. The liner shows you her length -- envelope taut as a sail in a fair wind, gondola composed, pontoons shedding a last necklace of drops -- and then she is already someone else's departure, somebody's first voyage, somebody's letter returning home.*
