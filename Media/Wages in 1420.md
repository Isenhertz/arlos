Beginning with an income of 12£ Livre Lavienese (36§ or 216₫) per annum, a man may be considered a gentleman, and when his income rises to twice that, his "wife" will increasingly be addressed as his "Lady". On 40£ a year the family enjoys the services of two maidservants, one horse, and a groom. On 70£ they keep one man and three maidservants and two horses. On 100£ they blossom out into an establishment of three female servants, a coachman and footman, a four-wheeled carriage and a pair of horses. On 500£ a year -- a salary achieved by, say, the most senior clerk of a banking establishment in Virell or Santra -- the establishment has grown to a good two dozen male and female servants, ten horses, a closed coach, as well as a two-seat and a four-seat carriage, and probably an overdeveloped sense of self-importance.

4§ are a workman's yearly wages, coming to 1₫ per 15-day Span (of which 12 are workdays, the three Lurdays excepted). A simple clerk might bring home 2₫ a Span (48₫ or 8§ a year). A wash-woman might earn 2₳ a day, an ironer 3₳ (coming to 1₫ and 1₫ 6₳ per Span similar to the working man's, but paid in piecemeal by each order rather than as a lump sum, unless on retainer). A seamstress or tailor, the perennial piece-worker, might make between 4-6₫ per Span if steady work comes their way, yielding 16-24§ in the year, hovering just below the pale of "respectable comfort" and one bad winter away from hunger.

Among servants, a maid-of-all-work aged sixteen must contend with barely 1§ a year, and a more experienced one might only earn 3§ (but with food and lodgings supplied by the employer). Is it any wonder many strive to become house- or parlour-maids, who easily earn 4-8§ a year (in other words, the fourfold), or cooks and governesses, who can earn 10-12§ a year? On the side of male servants, a page is barely better off than a maid-of-all-work; a footman, comparable to a cook; but a butler may earn from 3£ up to 8£ in a year (9-24§) depending on the household's standing and size, besides perquisites in the bottle-cellar.

A captain or engineer on a paddle-wheeler plying the major riverine or coastal routes might earn 1§ a Span (about 24§ or 8£ a year), where a crewmember would make 3₫ (12§ a year) and a cabin boy a mere 1₫ (4§ a year) and thus be in similarly dire straits as a workman or maid-of-all-work. On the prestigious aeroships, the numbers easily double, if not triple, making such job postings very sought after and remarkably good for one's lungs, nerves, and tendency to fall off things.

From there the ladder spreads out in all its dreary splendour:

**The labouring poor and casual trades**  
Dock-porters, wharf labourers, and cargo-haulers who present themselves at the river stairs before dawn and hope for a tally-stick may, in a decent year, scrape together 3-5§, with the unlucky falling below even the workman's 4§. Night-soil men, rag-pickers and bone-collectors earn 2-4§ in coin, the rest in stink and tetanus. Sweeps, chimney-boys and flue-runners, where such are still permitted, are paid by the job and may bring in 1-3§ in the year if they live that long.

In the growing manufactories and rope-walks of the ports, factory hands, match-fillers, paper-mill girls and other “hands” rarely exceed 3-4§ in wages, with fines for lateness and breakages helpfully returning coin to the employer. Older boys in such works might command 4-6§, provided their backs hold and no machinery eats them.

**Skilled craftsmen and shop-floor aristocracy**  
Journeymen carpenters, masons, coopers and ship-wrights can expect 8-12§ a year if attached to a reputable yard, rather more in wartime or boom years. Foremen, yard-overseers and master-journeymen who manage small gangs may take home 12-16§. A master craftsman with his own little workshop -- a cabinet-maker in a good street, say, or a reputable cooper -- might see 20-30§ in a year, depending on the custom and the amount of unpaid apprentice labour he can extract while insisting it is character-forming.

Printers' compositors, engravers, map-etchers and other literate artisans sit uneasily between shop and study. Apprentices may earn little beyond board and the privilege of lead-poisoning; a competent compositor might make 8-10§, a skilled engraver 12-18§, and a celebrated map-maker even more, though fame tends to arrive long after the lungs have gone.

Milliners, dress-makers and mantua-makers attached to fashionable houses often out-earn mere seamstresses. A senior hand in a modiste's shop may pull 12-20§ a year, with the mistress herself netting 30§ and upwards, provided a premiere fashion icon like the Principessa does not change her mind about sleeve widths halfway through the season.

**Retail, lodging and street trades**  
Inn-servants and tavern staff sit in that pleasant band where wages are poor and the temptation to skim is rich. A barmaid or tap-boy might receive 3-6§ in formal pay; a capable one doubles that in tips and "gifts" from regulars. Cooks in city inns earn on the same order as household cooks, 10-14§, with the addition of constant burns.

Shop-girls and counter-boys in the mercers', grocers' and haberdashers' establishments may draw 2-5§ a year, rising to 6-8§ once trusted with the keys and books. Small shopkeepers themselves, if not drowning in debt, may clear 10-30§ in profit; a prosperous vintner, spice-seller or bookseller in Virell might realise 40-80§, thereby joining the comfortable middle who insist they are not tradesmen at all, but “of the middling sort”.

Street vendors, costermongers, ballad-sellers and penny-sheet barkers live from hand to mouth. A noisy, vigorous one might reach 4-6§ in the year; most do not, though they enjoy the singular freedom of poverty: no one expects them to behave.

**Clerks, copyists and the bureaucratic swarm**  
The simple clerk on 2₫ a Span (8§ a year) is the foundation of the counting-house. A senior copying-clerk or book-keeper, after several years of ink and eyestrain, may rise to 12-16§. Clerks trusted with correspondence and minor decisions command 20-24§, enough to marry on if one is unambitious and one's spouse is very understanding.

Above them stand the managers and factors: a branch agent for a respectable mercantile house might earn 40-60§ (13-20£), sometimes with a share in profits. The chief clerk of a great banking establishment may run from 100-150§ (33-50£), and at the summit sits the fabled senior clerk on 500£ who controls an army of subordinates and does not, in his own opinion, work in “trade” at all.

**Public order, teaching and the polite professions**  
A watch-constable of Virell's city watch, uniformed and underpaid, receives perhaps 10-12§ a year, with serjeants and captains running to 16-24§. Bribes, naturally, are never mentioned in the official tables.

Schoolmasters and mistresses live by fees, charity and pessimism. A village schoolmaster may scrape 6-8§; a town school with a good endowment may pay its master 16-24§. Governesses in wealthy homes, as noted, make 10-12§, sometimes more if they bring fashionable tongues or music to the nursery instead of mere literacy.

Physicians and surgeons inhabit a different sphere. A modest practitioner in a middling quarter might earn 40-70§ (13-23£); a fashionable physician in the Garden Reaches or Santra's better streets might command 100-200§ (33-66£), plus the grateful offerings of patients who did not actually die. Advocates and pleaders of the law enjoy similar ranges, with the added advantage that their failures do not bleed on the carpets.

**Lurist clergy and other spiritual hazards**  
The Lurist chaplain's income depends heavily on the living he holds. A poor curate in a backwater parish might have 6-10§ a year in stipends, the rest in garden produce and parish hospitality. A town rector or master of a well-endowed chapel in Virell may see 18-30§. Cathedral canons, professors of divinity at the Academy, and chaplains to great houses often occupy the 40-70§ band, with lodging provided and their table kept generously supplied, in the pious hope they will not preach about greed.

Kharunite and other “Eastern-rite” clergy in the ambassadors' quarters are supported more by foreign treasuries than local coins; their stipends tend to track those of Lurist rectors, though their accounting is a matter for distant synods and creative book-keepers.

**Coaches, trams, riverboats and aeroships**  
Carters and carmen who drive horse-teams through city streets may earn 6-10§, depending on how many wheels they keep attached in a given year. Coachmen in gentry employ, with livery and regular hours, sit nearer 12-18§, with veteran postilions on the great roads sometimes matching them.

With the spread of compressed-air trams and public carriages in the larger cities, tram-drivers and brake-men enjoy 10-16§ in wages, plus the small change of passengers; conductors somewhat less in formal pay but richer in shouted insults.

On river and sea, ordinary hands have been mentioned; mates, bosuns and senior helmsmen on reputable paddle-lines may see 16-24§, and chief engineers 24-40§ (8-13£), bridging the gap between artisan and professional. On aeroships, these rates are often doubled: an aeroship bosun or steward may sit comfortably in the 24-40§ band, while captains and chief engineers of the better dynastats earn sums closer to 60-100§ (20-33£), plus the privilege of dying dramatically if anything fails.

**Hexcrafters, aetheric engineers and other technical sorceries**  
A hedge-hexcrafter scratching sigils on pots and door-lintels for neighbourhood clients might earn no more than a solid artisan: 12-20§ in a year, padded by payment in kind. A licensed city hexcrafter working on civic wards, carriage-stones and industrial charms commands far more: 24-60§ (8-20£), depending on guild rank and the number of accidents that cannot be reasonably attributed to "client misuse". Those attached to noble households or great trading houses can reach higher still, particularly if they specialise in discreet household protections.

Aetheric engineering, being newer, noisier and more terrifying, is better rewarded. A draughtsman or calculator in a design office might begin at 16-24§, comparable to a senior clerk. A qualified engineer overseeing a manufactory's choir-engines or a city's compressorium will expect 40-120§ (13-40£), the upper end reserved for those whose failures would level entire blocks. Chief engineers of major aeroship lines, or the masters responsible for great public works in Balboa and Virell, form a small and rather smug elite, matching or exceeding the 100-200§ bracket of lawyers and fashionable physicians.

Specialists in narrow fields -- an expert phonic-glass polisher, a tuner of choir-chambers, or a designer of safety-wards for dynastats -- may make wildly varying sums. In lean years they resemble journeymen artisans; in boom years they become very rich, very quickly, and then spend the proceeds proving that money cannot buy taste.

**Those above and below the ladder**  
At the very top, merchants-adventurer, great ship-owners, banking partners and a handful of noble houses may draw nominal “incomes” that strain the coinage: hundreds or thousands of pounds, measured more in estates, shares and monopolies than in tidy annual stipends. At the very bottom, there are the usual: beggars, cripples, widows, and those in “informal” trades, living on whatever falls through the cracks of all the salaries above. They have no wage at all, only what can be gleaned from alms, petty theft, and the occasional cousin who has not yet tired of them.

All told, the 1420s pay scale of Lavion and her cities looks commendably ordered on paper: neat bands of 4§ here, 12§ there, 100§ for the anointed few. In practice, illness, bad harvests, political panics and the whims of air and aether ensure that most people's incomes resemble a badly tuned choir-engine: prone to shrieking, occasionally booming, and always on the verge of coming apart.
