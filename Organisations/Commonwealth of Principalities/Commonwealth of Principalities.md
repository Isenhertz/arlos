## Definition and Character

The Commonwealth of Principalities -- called simply the Commonwealth or "the Principalities," either half standing pars pro toto for the whole, and known in oath and ceremony by the fuller style of the Sworn Common Weal of the Sovereign Princes of the West -- is less a nation than a living pact. It is a framework that lets sovereign rulers keep their independence while acting together where common cause demands it. Order here is negotiated, not imposed; precedence is ritual, not absolute; unity is built by charter and custom rather than hammered by a single hand. The patchwork of petty thrones and city polities owes much to gavelkind inheritance, an old Svenic habit by which a father's lands split evenly among heirs. In Stranu this practice seeded a fine-grained map of lordship; east of Stranu it never truly took root.

In everyday use the two common names are wholly interchangeable, and each does duty for the whole -- a Santran clerk and a Vallafont dockhand alike speak of "the Commonwealth" or "the Principalities" without distinction of register or rank. "Princes of the West," by contrast, is no mere ornament borrowed from the ceremonial title, but a poetic name for "Principalities" in its own right, reserved for oaths, patriotic exhortation, and the high style of Parliament and pulpit, and seldom heard in ordinary speech.

## Culture and Customs

The Stranic folk who fill the Commonwealth's halls and terraces are, by common admission on both sides of the Suurian Gates, what comes of leaving Anish merchants and Svenish adventurers around the same harbors for rather too long. An-Suur lies close enough to be seen from Stranu's own shore at the Gates, and the same western currents Svenic pilots charted through the Age of Corsairs carried Svenish crews into Stranic ports for generations before the mixing had any name at all. What settled out of it is a people of striking range in coloring -- olive through ruddy in complexion, brown in every shade from ash to chestnut in the hair -- with eyes gone startlingly light with a frequency no purely southern stock would produce on its own, the Svenish share of the blend showing plainest in the face whatever else went into it.

Circuleans elsewhere describe Commonwealthers as chatty, quarrelsome, and eerily efficient, and a Commonwealther rarely troubles to argue the point, since arguing the point would only prove it twice over. The temperament reads as exactly the fusion its two parent stocks would predict: Anish argument and a horror of settling by force what could instead be settled by contract, laid over Svenish competence and a plain willingness to get a difficult job done without much ceremony about it. A Santran clerk will out-talk a foreign judge and out-work a foreign quartermaster in the same afternoon, and the seeming paradox is no paradox at all to anyone who has sat through a session of Parliament: a Hall that argues without end and somehow still has the harbor dues ratified by autumn.

That efficiency rests on a communality that runs deeper than temperament, and traces straight back to the terrace. Much of the Commonwealth's food comes off stepped, flooded hillside paddies growing rice that no single household can work, or water, alone -- a neighbor's terrace shares your water whether either of you likes it or not, and Stranic agriculture has therefore always demanded a cooperation a purely private farmer would never need to learn. It is no accident that the First Parliament's founding business, in 201 AR, was the arbitration of water law before it was anything else: among terrace-holders, water rights were never a diplomatic nicety but the difference between a fed household and a starved one, and a polity that could not arbitrate them fairly would not have lived to arbitrate anything else. The same instinct shows again in guild mutual aid, in the cadet system's dense webs of family service, and above all in the Common Board, the standing hall every Stranic parish keeps, where the stranger passing through is fed before the parish feeds itself and the pot is never allowed to go cold. Once a year, at what the wider Lurist calendar knows as the Fallow Tables, the Board spills onto the parish green, kettles and ovens running in rows down it, and every ward joins the fair that it becomes.

But that communal instinct has a harder edge where the Triune Family is concerned. Stranic folk consider themselves, and largely are, egalitarian by both measures that matter to them at once: unearned rank commands no more automatic respect here than it does among the Anish across the Gate, and a woman inherits, judges, and commands with a matter-of-factness owed entirely to Svenu's own example. But Concordian doctrine reads marriage as a creedal act, the Father-Mother-Child triad as the smallest unit in which the faith's own "Three is Six" becomes lived reality rather than abstraction, and a Commonwealther raised to take that teaching seriously tends to regard anyone who steps outside the triad's shape -- as Balboans, under their own Orthodox dispensation, do without a second thought -- as somehow not quite properly raised. No law in the Commonwealth punishes an outlier for it, and no priest goes looking for one; Concordian canon leans toward conciliation over condemnation in every household dispute it touches, this one included. What actually enforces the norm is quieter and more durable than any court: inheritance customs, guild sponsorship, and a cadet's placement in the prelacy or the Guard's officer ranks are all built around the assumption of a recognized triune household, so that an outlier meets no punishment, only a slow, unremarked narrowing of doors that were never announced as closed.

## Historical Outline

c. 300 BR -- The Light sets Foot in Rimec-Suur. Remean stations along the Suurian Gate and Circle Sea littoral, already too far flung from the empire to see much support, are condemned and razed without much of a fight; the inland tribes convert in uneven waves, by choice or under pressure. Local clergy begin to blend Remean structural habits into Lurist rite, the first, faint strands of what will become Concordian thought.

0 AR -- Fall of Reme. The empire's center is destroyed; provincial outposts lose their tether. City captains and warlords consolidate by necessity, not permission.

56 AR -- The Accord of Salt-Mouth. Salt-Mouth, Arquillan, and Barvella settle shared harbor dues and mutual recognition of shipping rights. Maritime custom takes a first step toward law and will later inform parliamentary procedure.

143 AR -- The Liturgical Contention. Orthodox legates from An-Mirda collide with increasingly localist bishops in Stranu. The dispute foreshadows a western insistence on liturgical self-governance.

201 AR -- The First Parliament. Nine principalities bind themselves to mutual aid and the arbitration of water law, convening at Santra by custom rather than any fixed schedule, as the seasons and the rivers demand rather than as any calendar dictates. The court thus convened is remembered ever after as the first formal convocation of potentates and princely delegates, and is listed in the annals as the beginning of parliamentary history -- though history will show it a fitful beginning, kept alive by drought and flood more reliably than by any princely appetite for union.

336 AR -- Sack of An-Mirda. Ejitwe the Thronebreaker burns the capital of the First Hierophancy. Western clergy refuse to acknowledge a successor; at Santra a synod articulates the Covenant of Concord, giving formal shape to the Church of the West. Concordian doctrine flowers in Vallafont and Dros-Mera.

367 AR -- The Charter of Santra. Principalities from across the peninsula send bishops, magistrates, and princes alike to Santra, drawn less by any secular ambition than by the Church's own unfinished business: the Covenant of Concord, sworn a generation before in the shock of the Sack of An-Mirda, still has no permanent seat, and the assembled clergy settle on Santra at last, consecrating it the Synod's permanent home and fixing its autocephaly within the West in law and custom -- a Free Principality governed thereafter, in its own right, by a rotating council of prelates and magistrates. It is at this same gathering, swept up in the occasion's grandeur, that the assembled princes first swear to labor "for the Common Weal of the Princes of the West," a phrase that will long outlive the moment that produced it and furnish the whole polity its lasting name. But a name sworn in ceremony is not yet a government: no permanent secular court is founded alongside the Synod's that day, no common treasury or common army answers to any prince jointly, and the "Commonwealth" this Charter names will remain, for centuries yet, considerably more real in the mouths of its own bishops than in any hall where princes actually sit.

3rd & 4th Century -- The Long Turn away from Gavelkind. The First Parliament's water charters carved out indivisible "works of common necessity" and bound estates to keep obligations whole. That in turn pushed succession away from partible customs: in tight terrace basins and spring-towns, patrilineal primogeniture spreads; in the rockfall- and raid-prone marches, tanistry takes hold so the undivided duty falls to the line's most capable adult; in lagoon cities and port communes, elective customs let guilds and councils choose a captain-prince to hold both title and the entire slate of bonds. Cadets increasingly take dowries, stipends, or offices instead of gaining slices of land or water. By the time of the Charter of Santra, these "exemptions to partition" have hardened into local law and habit, and the map no longer splinters further every generation.

5th century AR -- The Parliament Falls Silent. The water-court that once bound the Nine to Santra meets ever less often as the generations pass: old feuds outlive the princes who began them, terrace basins quarrel with their neighbors over a dry spring with no court left willing to hear the case, and within a few generations of the Charter the First Parliament has lapsed into memory and little else. The "Commonwealth" of oath and ceremony endures only where the Church still gathers; the princes who swore its name at Santra now answer to no one but themselves.

448 AR -- The Pact of Steel and Scrip. Border raiding by emergent Lavienese warlords spurs a joint resolution for coordinated defense, levy quotas, and pooled finance through merchant leagues. For the first time, cooperation rests on principle rather than personal entourage -- a compact against a common outside threat, not yet a ban on the animosities the principalities still nurse against each other. The Pact does not outlive the threat that occasioned it: once the raiding ebbs, the principalities that mustered together under it drift back to their own courts and their own quarrels, and no standing body survives to keep the peace it briefly bought.

569 AR -- Founding of the Merseal Compact. Several of the Commonwealth's Circle Sea ports unite in a merchant league with semi-diplomatic standing of its own -- a private answer to common problems of toll, piracy, and shipping right, built because no common Parliament survives any longer to settle such things jointly. It will be centuries before the Compact has any Parliament left to send delegates to at all; when the Hall finally reconvenes, the Compact arrives already organized, already disciplined, and considerably harder to ignore for the practice.

888 AR -- Collapse of the Second Hierophancy. Schism and doctrinal fatigue unseat a single primate, and the Hundred Years' Vacancy begins: the Hierophant's own seat will stand empty for a century to come. Refugee clergy and laity spill west into a Commonwealth with no secular Parliament left to receive them, but the Synod of Santra -- autocephalous, and long practiced at governing itself by conciliar habit rather than any single far-off voice -- scarcely notices the vacancy at all. Against the spectacle of an empty Hierophancy, the Synod's own quiet continuity becomes, for the first time, a political argument in its own right: here, princes and prelates begin to murmur, is a body that has never needed a crowned head to keep functioning. It is this argument, more than any secular appetite for union, that the century to come will slowly prove.

Early 900s AR -- The Age of Corsairs dawns. Svenic pilots chart the western currents; Stranic ports thrive. Letters of passage go to privateers and explorers, and merchant-princes rise on the wealth of it -- wealth enough, before long, that men with no inherited seat start appearing at the Synod's own sessions at Santra, since that is the only hall in the Commonwealth still reliably open to anyone at all.

998 AR -- Founding of Lavion. Genovrast the Great crowns himself king in the same year the Hundred Years' Vacancy finally ends and a Hierophant sits again at Aramoor. The timing does the Commonwealth's princes a favor they are slow to admit: for a century they have kept meeting each other, Synod after Synod, in a Santran hall that never once stopped convening, and it takes only the sight of a unified crown rising at their own border to turn that long habit of talking into something harder and more deliberate. The Principalities note the danger and begin to moot a rotating High Prince as stabilizing figurehead -- the Synod's own conciliar instincts, worn smooth by a century of use, poured for the first time into a secular vessel.

1024 AR -- Elevation of the Guard of the Common Weal. Parliament empowers the old Guard of the Water -- first founded in 201 as a small group in charge of the water laws the First Parliament enshrined -- into a standing force to uphold those water-law decrees specifically, and to police Santra and other designated sites of common interest, renaming it the Guard of the Common Weal to mark its wider charge. Its charge does not yet extend to the general run of inter-princely dispute; a prince's quarrel with his neighbor over anything but water or a common-interest site remains, in 1024, entirely his own to settle. It is modest in number yet momentous in its historical momentum, building the foundation of a unified constabulary that grows from the Guard. In charter and in name it remains a constabulary still, a keeper of water law and public order; in muster and equipage it grows by slow degrees into something a foreign chancery would simply call an army, the distinction preserved in law long after it has ceased to hold in the field.

12th century AR -- The Maritime Revolution. In the middle of the Age of Corsairs, arcannons mature from fickle curiosity to serviceable artillery and, alongside deep-keel hulls, change the economy of war and trade on the Open Ocean. The western harbors -- Salt-Mouth, Vallafont, Arquillan, Barvella -- flourish while the Gyre-side east looks on in envy.

1178 AR -- The Great Disputation. A decade of formal debates in Santra and Dros-Mera clarifies precedence, capping a hundred and eighty years of gradual accretion since the 998 revival -- a rotating figurehead here, a standing Guard there -- into something that finally deserves the name of a constitution. The High Principate is codified as a presiding office, elected from reigning princes, expressly subordinate to Parliament.

1246 AR -- Maritime Reforms. Parliament harmonizes port dues, declares protected trade corridors, and empowers the Merseal Compact to convene neutral squadrons. Merchant influence peaks; the Chartered Companies era germinates.

Early 14th century AR -- The Fraying of Estrangement. The offices cadets have long taken up are by now not one channel but several at once: the same houses feed sons and daughters into the prelacy, the Scriptor's chancery, the officer ranks of the Guard, and the counting-houses of the merchant companies, often in the same generation. This is as much the Commonwealth's connective tissue as its charters are -- kinship binding organs that no central hand is strong enough to bind by decree -- but it is also the standing risk to every office built on the promise of impartiality. Custom has long answered this with an unwritten rule of estrangement, posting officials away from the courts and counting-houses where their own kin hold interest; the norm now shows visible strain, and whispers of a well-placed cousin steering a Guard writ or a chancery ruling toward family advantage are no longer confined to rumor. Where a cadet official's truest loyalty lies in such a case -- to the office and its oath, to the house that raised him, or to the Commonwealth entire -- is a question the Commonwealth's law has never settled, and one increasingly asked aloud.

1341 AR -- Crisis of the Commonwealth and the Siege of Santra. High Prince Ereador syn Emsar of Velgora, denied a hereditary High Principate, raises his own banners and those of allied marcher lords against Parliament and Synod alike -- a test, worth remembering, of an order not nearly so old as its pageantry suggests: the rotating Principate barely a hundred and sixty years codified, the revived Parliament itself only three and a half centuries removed from a silence that had once looked permanent. The fighting spreads across Velgora and its neighboring seats for the better part of two years before a mixed relief -- rival princes, Guild subsidies from the Merseal Compact, and the Guard of the Common Weal fighting as a single body -- lifts the siege of Santra and breaks Ereador's field army in the marches beyond. The Guard's unity through the crisis stands as the constabulary-fiction's first true test, and its first proof that the fiction holds: no company musters for Ereador, whatever cousins and patrons he claims within its ranks. Cornered at last in his own highland strongholds, Ereador is taken and tried jointly before Parliament and Synod -- the High Scriptor presiding over a tribunal whose procedure becomes, in later constitutional memory, as significant as its verdict -- and is condemned and executed. House Emsar keeps Velgora and its succession, but the peace settlement leaves the seat under harsh new oversight, the most exacting terms any principality has yet endured. No single faction emerges the stronger for the war; the claims of princes, Guild, and Synod alike are checked in the settlement, while the Scriptor's authority to certify, to record, and to gavel down even a Prince is tested in the crucible of civil war and emerges unquestioned. Emergency protocols are strengthened; rotation of the High Principate is reaffirmed in sharper ink; and in the same shaken mood the assembled princes swear, for the first time as a body, to bring grievance to the Hall before the field. It is an oath sworn in the heat of relief and grief alike, and Parliament as yet has neither the purse nor the standing force to make any prince keep it once the shock has cooled.

Late 15th to early 16th century AR -- The Furnace Cities. The long peace after the Crisis lets the Commonwealth do what Lavion's lead in alumen had once made look out of reach: a handful of Principalities, no more than a single hand's count in all, raise aetheric furnaces of their own, and each one that does becomes a place apart. Barvella builds hers beside the arcannon foundries that are its fortune, to charge and proof the coils of the guns; the others follow the same logic of trade and tolls. A furnace city runs lines, charge-houses and light-towers from its works' surplus years before its neighbors do, and the first local fulminant telegraph links such cities to their outlying yards by the 1540s. Lavion keeps twice the Commonwealth's furnaces, and three times as many by the Great War, so that the Principalities' advantage lies in the cells and coils their artificers make of what the furnaces give, never in the furnaces themselves.

1541-1561 AR -- The Undeclared War's Reckoning at Home. Two decades of proxy war in the Rim of Fire, fought under company colors by the Lavienese Companies Royal and the Commonwealth's own United Chartered Companies, drain princely treasuries that no formal peace was ever declared to justify draining. Principalities leaning on Company credit and Company-run trade find that credit, by the 1550s, running increasingly through Parliament's own coordinated subsidies rather than through any single prince's purse. It is this dependency, not sentiment, that finally arms the 1341 oath: a prince who needs Parliament's coin to keep his own companies afloat has, for the first time, real reason to let Parliament's word bind him before the field rather than after it. By the years just preceding the war's open declaration in 1561, "bring it to the Hall first" has begun to displace "settle it and sieve through the pieces afterward" as the Commonwealth's working custom, backed for the first time by a Guard of the Common Weal that can be sent out to stand between two quarreling princes as a peacekeeper, rather than mustered only as a last resort against outright rebellion. In the same decades the coilwrights of the furnace cities wind coils strong enough to drive a compressor, and the Chartered Companies begin to field brassbacks with no choir engine, so that the Companies' autonomy overseas rests on charge-houses at home as much as on their own credit.

## The High Principate

The High Prince or High Princess is primus inter pares, chosen only from sitting sovereigns, for a term of nine years, renewable once by vote. The office is expressly non-hereditary. In ordinance the High Prince merely presides; in practice the dignity of the seat and the breadth of its network give the office real mediating weight in crisis. The charge is service, not sovereignty -- to chair the Hall, to enact Parliament's will, to carry foreign policy in the Commonwealth's name, and to keep peace within the Concord. Yet the Prince presides in dignity, not in procedure: the conduct of the floor belongs to the High Scriptor, and it is to the Scriptor, not the Chair, that speech in Parliament is formally addressed. This division is the office's true safeguard. Because the High Prince commands the Hall's respect but not its record, and because the Scriptor alone certifies what has passed "in Concord," a Prince who overreaches -- as Ereador syn Emsar would in 1341 -- can be gaveled down and refused the floor by the very officer whose duty is merely to keep the minutes. The dignity of the Chair, in other words, is deliberately kept separate from the power of the pen.

In address the High Prince is styled His or Her Most Serene Excellency, a form that deliberately withholds any note of royalty -- "Serene" marking sovereign dignity without blood-right, "Excellency" marking an office held by grant rather than inheritance. The full ceremonial style, recited in full only at investiture and in the gravest instruments of state, runs to several clauses, each load-bearing. It opens by naming the manner of election -- by the Acclamation and Unanimous Will of the Most High Court, where the election was in fact unopposed, a mark of unusual prestige rather than a fixed requirement of the office. It then gives the Prince's own sovereign title in their home see or principality. The clause that follows varies by the nature of that seat: a Lord Temporal is Crowned, a Free City's magistrate is Invested, and a Lord Spiritual -- prince-bishop or prince-abbot -- is Ordained Voice of the Princely Parliament. Where a Lord Spiritual accedes, one further clause is added that no other seat-type carries: Anointed Shield of the Concord, signifying that such a Prince, uniquely among High Princes, holds temporal and spiritual power together in the same office and so answers doubly, for the term of their rule, to Church and Commonwealth both -- a burden the closing clause does not by itself already cover. That closing clause, by contrast, is fixed and universal: every High Prince, of whatever seat, is styled Sworn Protector of the Common Weal of the Princes of the West and Consecrated Defender of the Synodal Accords of Santra, the former binding the office to the Commonwealth's own founding oath at the Charter of Santra, the latter marking, for temporal and spiritual princes alike, the Commonwealth's standing guarantee of the Synod's autocephaly.

## The High Scriptor -- Keeper of the Record

Where the High Prince holds the Hall's dignity, the High Scriptor holds its procedure, an office that fuses the archivist's pen with the presiding officer's gavel. The Scriptor keeps the sole authoritative record of what has been moved, spoken, and passed, rules on order and precedence in debate, and -- most consequentially -- certifies whether a given resolution has in fact carried "in Concord." Since no vote in Parliament's own understanding of itself is truly passed until the Scriptor's record says so, the office commands a species of power no title as modest as "Scriptor" would suggest: the pen, in practice, outranks the Chair.

The office's origins are churchly. In the early centuries of the Commonwealth, when clergy held a near-monopoly on the literacy the post demanded, the Scriptor was reliably a synodal appointment, a cleric lent to secular business. Custom has since drifted toward a career civil office, filled increasingly from the ranks of trained lay chancery-men rather than the Church -- but the drift is neither complete nor uncontested. Concordian delegates hold that the Synod's confirmation, however reduced to formality, remains a live constitutional thread and not a dead courtesy; New Realists argue the office has long since become, and ought openly to be recognized as, a secular professional post answerable to Parliament alone. The dispute is not antiquarian. Whoever's candidate sits in the Scriptor's chair holds the office that can gavel down even a High Prince and can, in effect, decide what the Commonwealth has and has not agreed to.

## Parliament -- The Ever-Burning Table

The Most High Court of the Commonwealth of Principalities began as an occasional court; it lives now as a near-permanent chamber in Santra. Princes may still speak in person; more often their plenipotentiaries hold the floor. Parliament drafts and ratifies inter-princely compacts, coordinates shared works and levies, adjudicates precedence, registers oaths, and, by near-unanimity, may declare a State of Concord to compel action from member realms. It cannot tax or field troops in its own name; it moves purse and pike through coordinated quotas, subsidies, and warrants. A State of Concord's teeth have hardened with the centuries: where the earliest declarations carried only the moral weight of shunning and lost standing among peers, later practice added coordinated sanction -- closed ports, suspended tolls, withheld charters. Only once, in the whole of the 14th century, has that sanction escalated further: in 1341, when Ereador syn Emsar's bid for a hereditary High Principate was met by the Guard of the Common Weal itself, dispatched against a member realm. That precedent stands alone. It answered an attempt to seize the High Principate by force, not an ordinary quarrel over land or water, and Parliament has neither claimed nor been granted the standing to send the Guard out again on lesser cause; the routine, votable use of the Guard as an arbiter's arm belongs to a later age. That the Guard remains a constabulary in name is precisely what allowed Parliament to wield it even that once; a body that keeps water law and public order cannot, on paper, be an army sent to war against a prince, whatever it resembled once mustered in 1341.

Factions wax and wane with the century. Concordians hew closest to the Santran Synod and press for moral governance; Old Crowners favor narrow scope and robust local sovereignty; the Guilder League seeks tariff clarity, naval coordination, and uniform law for trade; New Realists argue for brighter statutes and a steadier Guard of the Common Weal and are eyed warily by all. None of these schools is a party, but a choreography of seats, city halls, and chancelleries that trade votes when aim and hour align. Where the princely seats fragment along a hundred lines of precedence and inheritance, the municipal delegations of the Merseal Compact vote as the Guilder League's disciplined core, and it is this cohesion, more than numbers, that lets a bloc of comparatively junior charters outweigh older and grander sees when the Hall divides.

Parliament is not a legislature in the modern sense, but a ceremonial instrument of balance. It is composed of envoys and plenipotentiaries sent by each of the 112 recognized principalities -- temporal, spiritual, and municipal -- who speak and vote on matters of mutual concern: trade privileges, port rights, precedence, and questions of faith and law. Decisions passed "in Concord" -- that is, by near-unanimous consent -- carry moral weight even when unenforceable. Parliament's strength lies in its ritual legitimacy, not coercive power, though it has grown some teeth over the long time it has governed.

The High Principate acts as Parliament's presiding officer, arbiter and representative rather than ruler. The High Prince presides over sessions and is bound by oath to "speak for the voices, not over them." Parliament's unwritten customs are as binding as law itself, their observance defended with near-religious devotion. These customs were codified in spirit, if not in writing, after the Crisis of 1341. The memory of that outrage reaffirmed ritual independence as the foundation of the Commonwealth's political faith. So too the princes' 1341 oath to bring grievance to the Hall before the field: it is repeated at every accession and invoked in every quarrel's opening rhetoric, and for the better part of two centuries it is honored chiefly in the breach, since neither purse nor Guard yet stands behind it, and a wronged prince who wants satisfaction still, more often than not, simply takes the field and settles his accounts with the Hall afterward.

## Santra -- Heart Without a Crown

Santra is the Commonwealth's legal capital and the Synod's neutral seat. It is a free principality ruled by prelates and magistrates in rotation. Its gates are open, its streets orderly, and its neutrality sacrosanct -- a status tempered but not broken by the siege of the 14th century. Here the Synod deliberates, Parliament debates, the High Prince resides when abroad from their home court, and laws are promulgated once passed. In the civic imagination, Santra is the city for all -- a hearth that keeps the Commonwealth's many kitchens warm without favoring any single table.

## Settlement and Ethos

The Commonwealth's settlement rests on three pillars: local sovereignty; common rule-craft; and a churchly order that answers Westward to councils rather than Eastward to a single see. Its law prefers charter to decree and precedent to flourish. In the quarrel between independence and identity the working answer is always the same: build the bridge you need, cross together, and then keep it in repair.
