> # di Lavion (family)
> ![[crest House di Lavion.png|Crest of House di Lavion]]
> Blazon: _gules a rose or en soleil_
>Achievements: _a royal helmet or with a crest of a sunburst; supported by two griffins passant argent armed langued and winged gules; ribbons royal or and sable_
> Motto: _"I Rise In Splendour"_

House de Lavion is the eponymous ruling dynasty of the Kingdom of Lavion, and has been since the Realm's inception in 998 AR. It takes its name from the location of the family castle near [[Virell]].
### Ruler of House de Lavion

1. **Genovrast "the Great" de Lavion (r. 998–1022; 970–1022).** Founder of the realm and setter of its tone, Genovrast forged Virell into a true capital and bound the southern coast to a single banner. He claimed wide sovereignty along the Circle Sea, even to ancient Balboa, a pompous legal fiction more than fact. His years were marked by fast building and faster campaigning. He died in battle securing those same borders, leaving a young dynasty with hard edges and a harder task. ^071a53
2. **Édric de Lavion (r. 1030–1058; 1014–1058).** Crowned at sixteen after the regency of Queen Dowager Aldira, Édric consolidated what his father won and made it governable. Under him, Virell’s court became a permanent seat rather than a travelling encampment; rolls of service and levy tallies replaced shouted promises, and the first standing schedules for coastal patrols kept the Circle Sea lanes steadier. He pressed south in measured steps but avoided a direct breach with proud Balboa, judging that a calm harbor could be more useful than a conquered one.
3. **Godwine de Lavion (r. 1067–1099; 1051–1099).** Raised to the throne after the careful regency of Queen Dowager Ismara, Godwine finished the fortification of Virell and lashed the realm’s river crossings into a coherent chain of keeps. His was a soldier’s kingship: steady garrisons, good roadstones, the patient choking of banditry. He kept the wolves from the Wolves’ Teeth, and the wolves in his baronage as well, preferring treaties that bought time over victories that spent it.
4. **Oswine de Lavion (r. 1099–1105; 1084–1105).** Young and unseasoned, Oswine entered a crown ringed by seasoned captains and sharper courtiers. A run of poor harvests and southern raiding made his short reign feel shorter still, and factional knives flashed in Virell’s corridors while outposts were gnawed to splinters. He died before thirty, and his failures taught the realm the price of youth without experience.
5. **Roburic “Ironfist” de Lavion (r. 1105–1142; 1079–1142).** Ironfist, uncle of Oswine, gave the realm its first taste of iron order after assuming the crown following Oswine's untimely death. The War of the Three Kings (1117-1121) pitted him against the self-styled King in the South and the independent Principe of Balboa, Giancarle di Balboa. Roburic broke the southern despot in the field and forced a settlement with Balboa that brought the city into Lavion as a privileged Crown Vassal rather than as a prize, such that by the end of his reign, Lavion spread from the coast to the mountains, and from Ertra to Stranu. He leaned heavily on the great orders of chivalry, not least the Order of the Silent String, and hammered law into something that could outlast a single fist. ^dc2977
6. **Wulfstan de Lavion (r. 1142–1175; 1120–1175).** Wulfstan’s long, orderly reign stitched shut the wounds Ironfist left behind. He arbitrated the Balboan compact into living practice, turning parchment clauses into habits of governance, and he regularized the musters and moneys owed by marchers and port lords alike. Peace under Wulfstan was not the absence of steel so much as the mastery of it; the realm learned to keep its sword sheathed without letting it rust.
7. **Genovrast II. "the Young" de Lavion (r. 1175–1196; 1161–1196).** A fourteen-year-old heir under the oversight of the Council of Virell until his sixteenth year, Genovrast II. grew into his crown while veterans kept the marches quiet. When his hands were finally free, he granted town charters and market protections that traded garrison costs for civic loyalty, and his small wars were the sort that ended at a table. He died young but left behind towns that could govern themselves and roads that paid for their own repairs.
8. **Cyneric de Lavion (r. 1196–1216; 1176–1216).** Cyneric was a tidier -- of statutes, of fealties, of the loose ends that make rope into noose. He clarified what the Crown could demand and what it must request, fixed tarrying vassals to calendars rather than tempers, and quietly cemented the recognition of Balboa’s singular status within the hierarchy of vassalage. His reign is remembered for making sense rather than noise.
9. **Éthelmound "the Lesser" de Lavion (r. 1216–1224; 1204–1224).** Crowned at twelve under a Marshal-Regency that lasted four years, Éthelmound emerged into rule amid competing tutors who never stopped teaching. Court factions multiplied and border captains learned to move between them. His death at twenty left more questions than answers and taught the baronage that a regency can linger even after it is pronounced finished.
10. **Léofric de Lavion (r. 1224–1257; 1205–1257).** Éthelmound's younger brother, Léofric steadied the helm with diplomacy and law. He cut trade compacts with Stranu and codified harbor dues and safe-conducts with his Crown Vassal at Balboa, which in turn calmed the Circle Sea’s quarrelsome piers. Under his seal, maritime law began to look like law rather than a list of bribes, and the realm’s coin moved with fewer daggers following behind it.
11. **Wulfric de Lavion (r. 1257–1293; 1237–1293).** Wulfric’s reign carries the thunderclap of 1277: The Revolt of the Commons and the clear air that followed. Tax protests and guild musters shook Virell hard enough to bring the Commons’ Chamber into being, a deliberative body that gave voice to those who paid the tolls and tilled the fields. Wulfric bent before he broke; by trading a portion of prerogative for the promise of revenues and quiet streets, he left the Crown stronger for being better balanced.
12. **Édmun "the Rich" de Lavion (r. 1293–1331; 1269–1331).** Édmund’s longer, steadier years saw towns fatten, guilds knit their own law, and the Commons’ Chamber grow from emergency valve to fixed feature. He reworked coinage and weights, patronized practical scholarship at home and the Grand Academy abroad, and proved that a crown can rise by lifting others -- provided its books add up.
13. **Osric de Lavion (r. 1331–1358; 1300–1358).** An older heir at accession, Osric governed like a clerk with a captain’s memory. He streamlined the chancery, clarified jurisdictions between royal courts and seignorial halls, and kept the Hierophancy close enough to bless but far enough to balk. His reforms made the machinery of rule more boring and therefore more durable.
14. **Héruard “the Steady” de Lavion (r. 1358–1401; 1328–1401).** Héruard presided over four decades of quiet strength, during which Lavion edged away from mercantilist feudal habits toward early modern commerce. He backed fairs, tolerated bourses, regularized customs, and made it possible for credit to cross a county without needing a convoy. By the end, his peace felt ordinary -- the surest sign of success. Dying without a blood heir, he left a clear preference for his niece Lurie-Mavrel as successor, trusting stability to outlive him.
15. **Queen Lurie-Mavrelle de Lavion (r. 1401-1469; b. 1385).** Crowned at sixteen after a hard succession, Lurie-Mavrelle governed a realm where Crown, Commons, and great vassals shared weight uneasily. As the first Queen Regnant of a realm that had known but two Queen Dowagers as regents, she found an ally in the Principessa Serenissima of Balboa of her time. The "Lurienne age", a half-century of steady compromise, drew Crown, Commons, and Church into a workable rhythm, if never a tranquil one. She tamed the great marches by charter, balanced the Privy Council against the growing Chamber of Commons, and fostered the learned arts through royal colleges that were patterned after the Grand Academy and its schools. Her reforms left Lavion civil and solvent, yet her caution bred a generation of younger nobles restless for sharper purpose. When she died, she left a prosperous realm -- and an army that had learned organization better than war. ^7ff292
16. **King Carlorus "the Soldier Scholar" (r. 1469-1498; b. 1438)** Grand-nephew and chosen heir of the queen, Carlorus had worn harness before crown. His early years on frontier inspection tours taught him discipline and the worth of precision. He reformed the officer corps, opening commissions to untitled cadets of good record; the sale of lesser patents to fund these reforms birthed the New Gentry, a class of title-holding but land-poor nobles who found advancement only through arms. Under him the Royal Ordinance Corps replaced feudal musters with a permanent soldiery drilled to aetheric cadence. Yet Carlorus loved order more than conquest; his reign closed with Lavion a barracks polished but at rest, its sword newly honed and waiting.
17. **Empereur Roburic II "the Iron Crown" (r. 1498-1524; b. 1471)** Roburic II inherited that sword and forged an empire with it. A general before he was a king, and a conqueror before he was an emperor, Roburic's rise marked the rebirth of Lavienese ambition. The early years of his reign saw swift victories along the littoral of Lu-An and Lu-Pto as well as across the Lupines into the basin of the Swicca, where his campaigns established protectorates and tributary ports that stretched Lavion's reach far beyond the western Circle Sea. Flush with triumph, he crowned himself **Empereur of the Lavienese and Protector of the Circulean** in a ceremony at Virell that excluded Hierophantic blessing entirely. The ensuing rupture with Aramoor was decisive: the Patriarchate of the Varsa, until then the traditional see of the Hierophant, was was brought to heel as the **Imperial Patriarchate**, whose Archprelate is now appointed -- and dismissed -- at the Empereur's pleasure. Aramoor fumes, but is powerless to stop the blatant nationalisation of its old core Patriarchate.
	Roburic styled himself as the living mediator between the Light and the Realm, a "Crowned Servant of Harmony" whose authority flowed from both sword and creed. His armies brought the Southlands to heel, tribute from Ulul-Azek, and dread to the Commonwealth and Vel Kiriya. Foundries and academies flowered under royal command, their inventions marching in step with his troops. Yet his empire, though brilliant, burned hot and brief. Exhaustion, intrigue, and the strain of unending war dimmed its splendour. When he died of fever returning from campaign, he left behind a kingdom richer in glory than peace -- and a throne that now claimed divine right not from the Church, but from itself.
18. **Imperatrice Ardelise de Lavion (r. 1524-1542; b. 1499)** Daughter of Roburic II, Ardelise ruled between parade and prayer. She saw clearly the cost of her father's victories and strove to reconcile empire with conscience. Her sponsorship of the Charter of Service defined the soldier as servant of the realm, not the old feudal order, a gesture that pleased the Commons but nettled the nobles. Industrial patronage flourished under her -- air-yards, foundries, academies -- but her peace policy faltered when mercantile guilds, fat from exploiting the southern trade, demanded new concessions. A failed expedition to Ulul-Azek ended her authority; illness and intrigue finished her.
19. **Empereur Edramar "the Thunderer" (r. 1542-1556; b. 1509)** Edramar, cousin of the late Imperatice, had been a soldier for a decade before he became Empereur. He embodied parade-ground precision gilded with operatic pomp. Under him Lavion's standing army dwarfed every ducal levy; uniforms shone like enamel, and the officer corps could read a map as easily as they could quote doctrine. He sponsored the Companies Royal, chartered ventures in the Rim of Fire whose fleets were half-merchant, half-naval, their private armies extensions of the state. Against the Commonwealth he waged a paper war of tariffs and spies that remained cold in the Circulean but turned hot in the Rim of Fire, fought through proxy by pitting the Lavienese Companies Royal against the United Chartered Companies of the Commonwealth. Expeditionary corps crossed the eastern plains toward the Hetmanate of Ulul-Azek, while others garrisoned the Southlands in the name of order. He died ignobly of a lung infection, though sentiment in the realm was quick to implicate supposed Commonwealth agents.
20. **Empereur Edramar II (r. 1556- ; b. 1532)** The son who succeeded him inherits not peace but momentum. The army is the state; industry its choir; faith its drum. Lavion's ships bear the rose-on-red on every sea, and the Principe calls himself an ally still, though whispers say otherwise. The Commons murmur of overreach, yet the forges of Virell sing night and day. The century of soldiers has begun, and the next war is on the horizon.
