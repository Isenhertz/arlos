The Church of the Light considers itself the guardian of Lurism in Arlos. It is also called the Hierophancy as a pars pro toto, using its highest office to stand in for the church as a whole. From the outside, it is refered to as the "Anish Rite" from its roots in ancient An-Mirda, and its adherents as "Virellite" from the figure of Saint Virellian, a pivotal figure in its early history. Terms such as First, Second or Third Hierophancy refer not to clear-cut organisational antecedents and successors, but are historiographic terms pointing to milestone changes in doctrine or polity the Church underwent in its nearly two millenia of existence.
## History

The revelation at An‑Mirda in -355 AR set the faith in motion, but only the rise of the theocratic First Hierophancy in the early centuries AR marks the beginning of the Church proper. When it fell in 336 AR with the Sack of An-Mirda by the [[Starkin]] under [[Ejitwe the Thronebreaker]], the Second Hierophancy extended ecclesiastical influence deep into [[Rimec-Suur]], but it in turn suffered deepening internal fractures leading to the Interregnum beginning in 888 AR. That unsettled season closed in the late tenth century AR as Genovrast [[de Lavion]] consolidated the realm and sought religious backing; the modern, Third Hierophancy thereafter retained spiritual authority while relinquishing most temporal reach. Through all these turns, the Eternal Dominion drove expansion -- sometimes by persuasion, often by steel.

### Early Lurism and the Judges

^fb2523

Pre-Hierophantic Lurism begins with the Prophet of Light and closes with the investiture of Maverel the Undimmed in -7 AR, in the closing years before the fall of Reme. In those centuries the Judges served as arbiters of doctrine and stewards of the faithful; their authority rested less on rule than on wisdom and on the recognition of their harmony with the Light.

- **The Prophet of Light (Lur-Illuhan) (-355 to -320 AR).** Seer, wanderer, vessel of revelation. Not counted among the Judges, Lur-Illuhan is the source of the *Book of Verses*; his death began the line of Judges as the faithful sought guidance in his absence. The only man holy enough, enlightened enough, that his name is no longer spoken in full, though his cognomen is what coined the phrase "Lur's Light". Folk belief holds that he conducts the Illuminated Choir himself.
- **Judge Het-Zaphar the Flame-Keeper (-320 to -288 AR).** Held to have been the Prophet's companion on the road, and the preserver of the original canticles that became the Verses. He convened the First Circle of Elders in An-Mirda and taught the tending of flame as spiritual practice. From his hands the later Judges took the *foculier*, at first a humble torch-staff that artisans of later ages would work into the Hierophants' gold-and-bronze brazier-staff of office. It stands metonymically for the highest office of the Church, and thus the Church itself, to this day.
- **Judge Bar-Samniya of the Outer Way (-288 to -251 AR).** A mystic preacher who first spoke of the harmony of the six, integrating the elemental order into early doctrine. Samniya -- who as a mere boy had been in the Prophet's presence when he was still alive -- carried the faith far beyond the Prophet's homeland and spoke of the "eternal dominion of the Light", seeding the later church militant. In his own mouth this was still a claim of triumphant hope, the Light's harmony someday prevailing rather than being imposed; the harder reading, dominion as a mandate to correct rather than merely await, would not take hold until Tar-Essari's generation, once An-Suur's own temple-arbiters had given the faithful real cause to think in those terms.
- **Judge Hadad-Phez the Listener (-251 to -213 AR).** Celebrated for prodigious memory and precise oral recitation of the Verses. His judgments in hard cases became the bones of later scriptural law. His dictum that the faithful are to approach one another with "clear brow and open face" forms the basis for much of the sumptuary laws later commentaries would codify.
- **Judge Saint Illut-Kharun of the Salt Road (-213 to -177 AR).** A scribe turned pilgrim who walked the routes to Lu-Pto. He drafted the first Communion of the Enlightened, recognizing scattered congregations as one communion. Kharun died at the stake, sentenced by a hostile tribunal convened by [[Remean Empire|Remean]] officials, the first spark of the long conflict between the Communion of the Enlightened and the Empire. His local disciples, fleeing north, became the apostles of the [[Eastern Rite|Kharunites]].
- **Judge Tar-Essari the Stern (-177 to -136 AR).** He formalized the Verse-Keepers and ordered the first copying of the [[777 Verses]]. Founded in the same years as Kharun's martyrdom, the Verse-Keepers were built to do two things at once: guard scripture and test orthodoxy, exactly what a newly persecuted faith needed most urgently. Where An-Suur's old temple-treasuries had held deposits, witnessed contracts, and stood surety between rival cities, the Verse-Keepers inherited all three functions as the temple-arbiters' own neutrality came under suspicion, not because the faith coveted wealth but cities that no longer trusted the arbiters of the elemental cults needed somewhere new to swear their binding oaths. In Anish moral terms the earlier arbiters were seen as oathbreakers, the most grievous crime the cities knew, and it is in this generation, rather than in Bar-Samniya's gentler original meaning, that "eternal dominion" hardened into a mandate of orthodoxy. His rigorous tests of that orthodoxy remain foundational to Antidiscordianism.
- **Judge Oremas of Il-î-Amah (-136 to -91 AR).** The first Judge born outside An-Suur proper. He carried the faith along the desert roads to the Drowned Coast, past Riv-i-Tep and into the Plains. By the time of his Judgeship, An-Suur's own transformation -- temple-arbiters into Judges, temple-treasuries into Verse-Keepers -- was substantially complete; the faith's remaining work lay beyond the Anish coast entirely.
- **Judge Nallume the Binder (-91 to -52 AR).** Unifier of liturgical melodies into a harmonic mode still heard in modern chant. Tradition says Nallume sang sunlight into flame during the siege of the Remean provincial capital of [[Paltos]], which liberal voices have always argued was no less than sanctioned invocation, though hardliners continue to squash this position.
- **Judge Mazuren the Patient (-52 to -12 AR).** Austere and deliberate. He led the faithful through the final generation before Reme's fall, fasting often and leading the crusading fleets against Remean outposts in the west.
- **Judge Saint Virellian the Scribe (-12 to -7 AR).** Though only in office for five years, Virellian was a most influential Judge, determining the shape of the Church for the centuries to come. A doctrinal clerk before his calling, he had long labored on a compilation of the Judges' writings; in office he completed this great work, giving it the name *Book of Judges*. Binding spiritual duty to the emerging needs of governance, he formed the bridge between the charismatic Judgeship and formal ecclesiastical office. Though he called on the faithful to elect a formal head, he declined the very office his work made possible and instead retired to a hermit's life. He is rightly named a Saint among the Enlightened, and the capital of Lavion is named in his honor.
- **Maverel the Undimmed, First Hierophant (installed -7 AR).** Formally enthroned by a congress of Dominion commanders and senior canonists. Maverel's acceptance of a permanent seat and a structured succession ended the fluid Judgeship and began the First Hierophancy under the Theocracy of Eternal Dominion. Barely a year into this new order, the Hierophancy commissioned the crusading fleet meant to deliver the war's culminating stroke against Reme; it was this same fleet that the Fall of Reme swept far off course, never to see the city it had sailed to destroy.

### The Age of Enlightenment (0-336 AR)

^97845f

#### The Zeal of Victory

When the First Hierophant was elected in -7 AR to lead the nascent Theocracy of Eternal Dominion, he found himself heir to an unfinished crusade. The Lurist forces, blooded and hungry from their campaigns against Reme, now sought to complete the Prophet's mandate by purging the last vestiges of disharmony from the world. Instead, the destruction of Reme at the hands of Phlogistophor robbed their campaign of its climax. That did not slake the zeal that had built those fleets. The Faith saw itself vindicated in the fiery doom that befell its once great enemy without even having seen it happen, and its hosts turned their hungry eyes to other heathens. In particular, they pushed into the Wolves' Teeth Mountains, where the Alvai Kin of the Wolf Mother lived, whose naturally half-animal bodies looked to the zealous like yet another result of shaping the flesh -- that inward art the Judges had condemned as the ultimate arrogance against the Light.

#### The Century of Crusades

The First Crusade (0-8 AR) was thus a direct continuation of the Remean war, a disciplined invasion led by the same war-priests who had sharpened their swords against the Shaped legions. Their banners bore no saints yet but the simple geometry of the creed's dot, triangle, hexagon, and circle, as the Moonkin highlands became a proving ground for sanctified brutality. Each successive crusade thereafter grew less orderly and more vindictive: the Second (21-25 AR) sought to root out "hidden Shapers" among the high valleys; the Third (40-47 AR) burned Alvai sanctuaries and necropoles that had stood since before the Prophet's birth; the Fourth (66-72 AR) was scarcely a campaign at all, but a sequence of punitive pogroms and forced baptisms that sealed the Scattering.

To the Hierophants who succeeded one another in An-Mirda, the wars were necessary purifications. To their chroniclers, they were victories of the Light over cacophony. To those Alvai who lived through them, they were a century of winter without end.

#### Prosperity and Pretense

Yet these were also the centuries of learning, rebuilding, and ordered faith that later historians would call the Age of Enlightenment. Monasteries became schools, pilgrim roads became trade roads, and the dozenal notation of the Creed, cleft from the sexagesimal order of the Empire yet compatible enough to be easily adopted, became the shared grammar of commerce. The Theocracy's inward rhetoric of harmony proved excellent for administration: hierarchy was recast as cosmic order, and every tithe, toll, and tribunal was justified as part of the great balance.

Under this banner, cities prospered. Guilds and abbots spoke the same numerate tongue; scholars recast the six elements in terms of divine subservience, censuring earlier invocative traditions. Outwardly, the Theocracy grew rational, luminous, and at peace. Inwardly, it rotted on its triumph. The more it promised unity, the more it required enemies to define it.

#### The Turning of the Light

By the mid-fourth century, the Theocracy had become what Reme once was: centralized, complacent, and deaf to its own cruelties. The Kin of the Wolf Mother who survived the crusades withdrew into diaspora, but they were not the only Kin.

In 327 AR the Starkin prince Ejitwe, later named the Thronebreaker, gathered exiles, outcasts, and disillusioned converts beneath a single banner. His armies struck not from the south but from within the faith's own backyard -- the jungles of the Ansuurese homeland, where the Theocracy had grown fat and ripe with the fruit of their three centuries of expansion. The partisans of the Thronebreaker marched under no banner, fought no open battle, and yet in 336 AR they breached An-Mirda itself. The city, seat of the Theocracy of Eternal Dominion and heart of the Hierophancy, worldly symbol of Lur's earthly order, fell after forty days of siege before relief could arrive.

The Thronebreaker did not hold it. He burned the archives, shattered the golden altars on the stepped pyramids, and left the ruins as monument and warning. The Theocracy died thus, choking on the acrid smoke of its burning riches, and Lurism learned that every creed, pushed past compassion, becomes its own opposite.

#### Aftermath

The Sack of An-Mirda ended the theocratic reign of the First Hierophancy, just as the Fall of Reme had ended the empire of the Shapers. In both, hubris met its echo. Out of the ashes would rise the Second Hierophancy and the long, bitter centuries of reform, schism, and reckoning that define the later Dark Ages -- when Lurism turned from the sword toward the sermon, seeking to justify its Light to a world it had once tried to conquer.

## The Dark Ages (336-998 AR)

^4d3257

After the Sack of An-Mirda in 336 AR the Church of the Light lost the effortless unison of its first centuries. The First Hierophancy had yoked crusading zeal to a tidy canon, but the fall of the old holy city cut the thread that held center and circumference together. Refugees, new courts, and victorious captains all professed the same creed, yet each heard a different harmony within it. Over the next five centuries the Second Hierophancy passes from strong unison to braided counter-melodies, then to local chant, and finally to a century of loaded silence at the top.

### Beginnings of the Second Hierophancy (336-390 AR)

In the first generation after the fall, rival obediences grew from the same single wound. Shaken by the destruction of the holy city, bishops and exarchs tried to stitch authority to new ground. Aramoor rose as the practical seat, having been the see that oversaw the southward expansion of the Theocracy in the centuries prior, and councils there issued sober rulings aimed at turning crusader discipline into civic habit.

But partisans of the ruined holy city insisted only an An-Mirda line could be lawful, while Aramoor's bishops and magistrates argued that the Light's harmony lived where the Church could still speak in full sentences in synod, statute, and seal.

### The Covenant of Concord (337 AR)

The immediate contest between An-Mirda and Aramoor gave Stranu's already independently minded bishops their moment. Meeting at the First Synod of Santra, they framed an autocephalic settlement called the Covenant of Concord: a church ordered by charter and council, jealous of its freedom to keep the peace at home. While properly known as the Autocephalous Church of the West, common parlance gives its thought the moniker "[[Concordianism]]". Its tone is conciliatory and civic, but its reach remains where it began -- in Stranu and what will become the Commonwealth's dioceses and prince-bishoprics. Concordianism does not share the earlier Lurist appetite for zealous expansion; it stays Western by choice and design.

### Crusaders without Borders (4th-5th centuries)

With the fall of An-Mirda, the Theocracy of Eternal Dominion lost its capital, but not its cadres. Its crusader companies -- sworn to an idea rather than a place -- continued to drive south and east in failing campaigns to bring the Light to the Southlands and the plains. A pretender hierophant even launches a crusade against the Sunkin, but without a strong primate to harness them, any such efforts turned ragged: victories that cannot be held, and defeats that teach nothing. Slowly the age learned to tame this confederation into the Church Militant, so that shortly after the Settlement of the Varsa, these bands under scattered banners were turned  orders under rule and budget, sworn to the Hierophant's warrant and answerable to synod. In the Dark Ages though, the sword was not yet sheathed, for it was looking for a body to be belted to again.

### Twinned Thrones (6th century)

The sixth century set the pattern of the age: twice in that hundred years a Hierophant chosen in Aramoor was met by a counter-election in the north, the rigorist northern patriarchate enthroning an Anti-Hierophant on the strength of Antidiscordian rigor and piety. Both sides preached harmony; both swung censures like hammers. Neither could influence the Western Church, which kept insisting that councils, not single hands, best expressed the Light in a complicated world. The result was not open war so much as constantly overlapping jurisdictions, duplicate fasts, and rival feast calendars -- a daily inconvenience that forced parish life to adjust. Rites diverged by region, and then wrote footnotes to justify the divergence. Everyone quoted the Lore of Light; everyone heard it in a different key.

### Habit hardened into Doctrine (7th-8th centuries)

What begins as theological dispute hardens into dogma -- confession by another name. Regional rites, adopted as stopgaps, have by now acquired champions and genealogies. In the heartlands of the Varsa the basilican houses teach a disciplined parish life that prizes conciliar discipline and civic duty; in An-Suur and the marches the sermon instead grows sharper, insisting that worldly discord must be cut away before souls can be made quiet. Everywhere, preachers seeking to mend the schisms lean into a blossoming Redemptionist language, focused on restitution and mutual obligation.

Councils on doctrine and dogma proliferate, each large enough to issue a syllabus, yet too small to bind more than its immediate neighbors. The commentaries fatten, and jurisdictional fences sprouted fine gates that only insiders knew how to open. Meanwhile the Church Militant found its lawful shape: crusading became campaigning by commission, with orders chartered for frontier defense, punitive excursion, or the relief of harried towns. For the first time since the Sack, the sword served the foculier in a recognizable chain of command, even if that foculier was still disputed.

### The Failure of the Second Hierophancy (9th century)

Though a reforming pulse gathered in the early 800s that tightened canons, cleaned books, and first demanded the crusaders' obedience to synod and purse, it could not heal the core fracture. Even a compromise primacy recognized on parchment would nonetheless get disputed in pulpits; anti-hierophantic pretenders bloomed and faded at the mercy of shifting ecclesiastical politics; excommunications churned like the mighty Gyres. Worse, clerical appointments became a currency in the hands of lay patrons who prefer docile pastors to principled ones. Chapterhouses, worn by endless jurisdictional quarrels, tended their own precincts and quietly left the high quarrel to tire itself out.

The end came not with a scandal, nor with a sack, but with a death. In 888 AR, consumption claimed Hierophant Solvrand III, the last exarch capable of bridging the divide between the Northern and Southern churches for an undisputed election, and for the first time since Marevel the Undimmed was enthroned, no successor followed. The machinery for election existed on paper; but in practice no conclave could be gathered that was not already suspect to half the church. The foculier of the Hierophant remained upright in the sacristy, unlit, and stayed there.

### The Hundred Years' Vacancy or Interregnum (888-998 AR)

^3be3ba

By the time the foculier stood idle, habit had already learned to live without a single hand upon it. Though leaderless at the top, the Church did not vanish; it localized. Parish life continued with the seasons, monastic houses kept the morning recitation and the evening examen, and guilds drilled meticulous care for their craft into their apprentices -- Right Habit in action. The Church of the West in Stranu remained politely separate and serenely itself, its Concordian compact durable and more concerned with matters within the Commonwealth than beyond its borders. What the century lacked was a single voice that could have reconciled doctrine when habits collided.

Theologically however, this long, leaderless steadiness sanded the final edge off earlier crusader ambition. The Church Militant, now thoroughly a creature of rule and budget, guarded holy sites, patrolled pilgrimage paths, and learned to measure victory in peace kept rather than land seized. Zeal became administration; administration became peace of a plain sort.

### The Settlement of the Varsa (998 AR)

It took a force from without to bring the silence to an end. Genovrast de Lavion, victorious conqueror of all lands from the Varsa to the Ertrese Peninsula, did not need for a primate to crown him; instead he crowned a primate so the Church could affirm his seized power as rightful and ordained.

In 998 AR, fresh from the subjugation of the last opposing princeling, he entered the basilica on the Varsa with his captains and jurists in train and laid upon the altar a packet of charters that recognized the Patriarch of the Varsa as Hierophant within all lands held in his peace. Then he knelt -- not to be made king, for he already was -- but to receive the sanction that only a single lawful voice could give. By offering recognition first, he made it possible for the Church to speak again in unison, and in that unison the primate rose with the foculier at last.

From that day the settlement was reciprocal but not equal. Primacy owed its reanimation to the king's shelter and the quieting of arms; the crown, in turn, bound itself to guard synod and rite. In public pageant the foculier blessed the realm; in chancery the realm guaranteed the foculier's reach. The Church of the West in Stranu remained politely separate, its Covenant of Concord acknowledged as neighbor rather than rival.

Later ages will call what begins here the Third Hierophancy. In tone it is modern Lurism's familiar voice: wary of spectacle, patient in remedy, tidy in book and rite -- born of a bargain in which a conqueror used worldly strength to make room for a single sacred voice, and in return took that voice as the holy seal upon his rule over the lands that would soon bear his name.

## Structure
### The Sacred Synod

The [[The Light|Lurist]] ecumene or *Communion of the Enlightened* maintains its transregional unity through a layered synod. At its center stands the Hierophant, supreme interpreter of the Lore of Light and guardian of doctrinal harmony. The present seat is the Sanctuary of [[Aramoor]], an independent, canon‑governed city‑state in [[Kingdom of Lavion|Lavion]]'s southwestern heartland.

The Hierophant is chosen by the College of Exarchs, typically from among senior clergy known for moderation or political neutrality. Modern custom restricts temporal power to the Sanctuary itself; beyond those walls the Hierophant acts as symbolic head of the faith, spiritual arbiter of orthodoxy, and nominal head of the [[Eternal Dominion|Church Militant]], a remnant of the Church's earliest, theocratic crusaders. Binding dogma may be promulgated, yet most pronouncements now take the gentler form of axiomatic illuminations -- doctrinal reflections received, debated, or declined by the Patriarchates.

The College of Exarchs gathers senior representatives from all churches in communion, with observers from sanctioned orders. It elects the Hierophant, confirms canonical interpretations, and mediates disputes across jurisdictions. Its weightiest acts require the traditional supermajority known as Twelve in Harmony, which binds diverse rites without erasing their lawful variety.

### The Patriarchates

Regional custodianship belongs to semi‑autonomous churches that interpret the Lore of Light through local custom, liturgy, and moral culture, under a Patriarch or equivalent prelate.

The **Patriarchate of the North**, encompassing [[An-Suur]] and [[Sarm]], is the primordial heart of Lurism. It adheres most closely to the rites and scriptural readings of the early Judges and remains interlaced with the Eternal Dominion, where faith and governance still intersect. Here Antidiscordianism burns brightest, still feeding off the missionary fervour of the early faith as it spread into frontier chapels and the strongholds of the Dominion. Crusading charters were first regularized here; their canons on musters, tithes, and the custody of relics became templates across the Communion; its episcopal courts are famous for fast fact-finding and elaborate penances. The North's rite is austere in character but rich in history, with the stepped pyramids where the early faith gathered still being used in personal devotions wherein one gazes at the sun in hopes of thusly glimpsing Lur's Light.

The **Patriarchate of the Varsa**, covering the western Varsenne basin up to the shores of the eponymous river, was a backwater until after the fractures of the Interregnum, when Genovrast the Great confirmed its Patriarch as the new Hierophant in exchange for theological backing of his rule. In the College of Exarchs it is represented by an Archprelate, as the nominal Patriarch of the Varsa is the Hierophant himself. Its seat at Aramoor places it within reach of court and Commons, and its jurisdiction covers the agrarian heartland of the realm. The Archprelate of the Varsa chairs inter-patriarchal convocations whenever the primatial seat is vacant, traveling, or tactically silent -- a custom hardened into law after the disorders of the 6th century. Orthodoxy here runs cool and exacting, tempered by Redemptionist habits in schools and chaplaincies. ^7a2033

The **Patriarchate of the Merhal** is another riverine church, shepherding the faithful of the delta's floodplains and the long levees upcountry along Mer and Hal both. Seated in ancient Omt-î-Thes (often spelled Omidides), where the Sorcerer-King Thesun-Khetep is said to have ruled, its reach extends as far as the rivers do, all the way into the distant highlands of Vel Kiriya to the north. Despite cleaving close to orthodoxy, its fasts follow the rivers' moods rather than ecclesiastical schedules, and it has a greater tolerance for rites appeasing the Noble Element Maregina in particular: the importance of the rivers for everyday life is simply too great to ignore.

The **Autonomous Church of the Eastern Gyre** -- governing the Drowned Coast region of [[Lu-An]], and [[Lu-Pto]] -- still breathes with a mystical cadence. While it keeps alignment with the greater Church of the Merhal for orthodox doctrine and court of appeals, vestiges of older voice-and-breath rites persist among fisherfolk and rural confraternities. The Ordinate of the Flame keeps a steady watch for drift, yet the people's piety remains loyal to the creed; if anything, the Eastern Gyre is even more keenly devoted to exhorting the saints as examples to beseech in prayer and imitate in everyday life. 

The **Autonomous Church of the Southern Reach** governs the church in [[Svenu]] and the lands south of the Lupines from its seat in Hjalmsvik, on the eastern coast of the Southern Gulf. It remains in full communion with Aramoor and accepts the Hierophant's primacy, yet its strength is modest. Alignment with Varsa gives it a doctrinal anchor and a court of appeal; in return, Varsa recognizes certain Southern Reach "ideosyncracies" as legitimate variants. For one thing, long habits of appeasing and exalting the harsh elements endure among the people, and Lurist practice here bends in an accommodation towards the local matrilineal custom forged across centuries, investing women as deaconesses, abbesses, and chapterwardens. The preaching style is plain, domestic, and practical; the strength of the Light, here, is less about the brilliancy of enlightened awareness but about the warmth of the hearth and the Eternal Fire. Here parish houses are built stout to serve as winter refuges, and the clergy knows to tread lightly around a populace that resisted missionary efforts and the [[Eternal Dominion]] alike.

The **Patriarchate of the Lupines** is seated at Engalas. This mountain see minds the high roads, mining towns, and pass-garrisons of the Marches that sit across Lavion's southeast like a girdle. Its parishes reach from the marches proper into the mountains, strung like prayer beads along avalanche gullies and river cuts, and its monasteries serve as winter hospices for caravans caught in early snow. Antidiscordianist by formation and longstanding martial necessity, but today moderated by an equally long Redemptionist practice in seasonal disaster relief, the Lupine See preaches a short gospel of duty and neighborliness -- save lives first, argue doctrine after the thaw. The rite here is simple; lay fraternities keep caches of grain and salt, or maintain avalanche bells and boundary shrines near treacherous ground. Relations with the marcher lords are cordial but frank, as befits a see defined by a history of dangerous terrain and enemies.

The **Autocephalous Church of Ertra** governs the entirety of the Ertrese Peninsula, from the tip of Balboa to the march of Fiurisci. Seated at Balboa proper, Ertra is a maritime church, granted autocephaly in recognition of the peninsula's long habit of governing itself. The birthplace of Harmonism, the academical bent of Ertra expects clergy to shine in their command of numbers and letters as much as with their command of the creed. At the southern edge, Fiurisci ties the church inland to the frontier parishes of the Wolves' Teeth; the two churches have a long-standing tradition of cooperation, and the communities of eastern Ertra cleave more closely to the Lupine See or that of the Eastern Gyre than the church that nominally governs the peninsula.

Lastly, the **Autonomous Church of the High Seas** is the youngest of the Communion's churches. It grew out of missionary chaplaincies that followed the caravels and sloops into the storm tracks of the Rim during the Age of Corsairs. It is seated on the Keystone Isles at the throat of the Suurian Gate, with the Patriarchate of the Varsa as its doctrinal anchor and court of appeal. Like other maritime rites, High Seas tunes its feast-day calendars to trade winds and cyclone seasons. The High Seas licenses ship-chaplains and dispatches missionaries, runs orphan houses for castaways, and its pilgrimage charts are treated as reliable itineraries by Circulean captains venturing beyond the Suurian Gates. Syncretic temptations in far harbors are met with patient preaching and the ever-effective trifecta of soup, shelter, and slow instruction in the Creed. ^d5a57f
