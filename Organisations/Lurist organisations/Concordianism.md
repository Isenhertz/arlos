# Concordianism and the Autocephalous Church of the West

Concordianism, as embodied by the Autocephalous Church of the West, is the self-governing communion of the [[Commonwealth of Principalities|Commonwealth]] that declines the primacy of the Third Hierophancy while remaining in doctrinal communion with the wider Lurist world. Its roots lie in early experiments with shared governance among the western dioceses, but its autocephaly was only settled in law through a series of compacts in the 10th and 11th centuries AR, as the Commonwealth's Most High Court matured and the Concordian habit of synod and charter hardened into constitutional practice. In these accords the Church of the West acknowledged the [[Church of the Light|Hierophant]] as a binding agent of unity and a mediator among patriarchs, yet politely and definitively refused to heed him simply ex officio. His office is honoured as that of a senior arbiter, not a universal primate.

Neither a schism nor a heresy, the Autocephalous Church nevertheless insists on its independent character within the wider Lurist communion. By courtesy the College of Exarchs in [[Aramoor]] continues to invite "the Patriarch of the West, or an Archprelate plenipotentiary of His choosing" to its gatherings, and by courtesy the Church of the West answers by sending delegates chosen by the Synod of [[Santra]], which has its seat in that city. The Synod of Santra originally referred to a singular gathering of bishops and learned clergy in the Age of Enlightenment, which first articulated the foundations of what later generations would come to call Concordianism. Over time it became the standing, inclusive pan-Western synod of the entire Commonwealth, drawing on the prince-bishops as well as selected clergy and lay deputies from across the western dioceses. In this function it embodies the Covenant of Concord, interpreted not only as a political settlement among bishops but as a spiritual principle: that the church's life should be ruled by concord rather than command.

Concordian polity is layered and visibly conciliar. A diocese consists of the see of its prince-bishop, directly ruling as lord temporal and spiritual, and whatever surrounding lands are joined to its spiritual oversight -- but not, crucially, under it in terms of temporal rule, which is why one diocese usually contains several principalities adjacent to the prince-bishopric. Several dioceses together form regional synods, above which sits the Synod of Santra as the great council. These bodies are necessarily visible in Parliament, but they are not of Parliament: all prince-bishops are seated as lords spiritual in the Most High Court of the Commonwealth, after all, though the Church carefully maintains that its conciliar acts rest on the Covenant of Concord and the Creed, not on civil statute. Within synod and parliament all prince-bishops are formally equal, and precedence is determined not by grandeur or wealth but by the age of the see's charter. A small, weather-beaten river diocese whose bishopric was first to sign the Covenant may outrank a glittering maritime see founded centuries later.

Conciliar governance is generally more than a mere habit in Concordian lands: it is doctrine. Theologians of the Church of the West cleave to the language of early Judges with unusual seriousness, citing how they spoke of the creed as a concord of truths, a measuring-line by which discordant errors might be heard. Stress is therefore placed on the Lurist creed as itself a concord: the One is Three is Six is All as a chain of relations that must each be kept in tune to hear the whole. Where Orthodox preachers read the creed's living weight in its opening clause -- "One is Three," the Light approached through the personal harmony of Body, Mind, and Soul -- and where the Kharunites place their emphasis on the closing clause, "Six is All," the Light as the saturated totality of the world, Concordian theology locates the creed's operative moment in the middle: "Three is Six." It is the passage from the personal triad outward into the social world, the hinge at which inner harmony opens into relation, that Concordian preachers return to again and again. The creed does not merely describe the cosmos; it describes the movement every faithful person is called to enact -- from individual soul, through the Triune Family, into the full Six Relations of social life. No single voice, they argue, can therefore claim to represent the many paths to harmony within and towards the Light. Instead, the health of the Church must be read in a different threefold harmony, one posited between the faithful of the communion, their teachers (both in the clergy and among the learned laity), and the shared conscience that emerges from their councils. This communal triad is not merely an organisational convenience; it mirrors the Heavenly Triad of Body, Mind, and Soul, and the Synod of Santra is, in this reading, the Church's attempt to instantiate at the communal level the same harmonising movement the creed calls individuals to achieve inwardly. When these three fall into accord, Concordians say the Light's reflection on earth has grown clearer; when they clash, the Church's tone is judged to have coarsened, even if outward obedience remains intact.

In ethics and spirituality Concordianism remains recognisably Lurist, grounded in Right Habit, Right Attention, and Right Appetites, yet its emphasis falls more heavily on structural and civic harmony than on the individual refinement beloved of orthodox preachers. It teaches that disordered guilds, unjust courts, and faithless covenants are as grave an offence against the Light as personal excess. The Concordian addition to the common Lurist ethical framework is not merely negative -- not merely that bad institutions obstruct personal refinement -- but positive: a person embedded in right relation with their Triune Family and their Six Relations is actively drawn toward triune harmony by the social fabric they inhabit. The relational web, when healthy, is soteriologically productive. The most fundamental node of that web is the Triune Family itself, which Concordian theology treats as the primary moral unit: not the individual and not the church, but the household formed by Father, Mother, and Child in faithful covenant. This is where the movement "Three is Six" is first enacted at human scale, and so it is here that the creed's social claims are most urgently at stake. Relations to the Noble Elements are read somewhat more flexibly than under Orthodoxy, though not with the confident syncretism of the Kharunites. Elemental influences upon the city and its works are treated as mutable, balances to be prudently managed, rather than as rigid metaphysical diagrams to be feared or adored. Rites of appeasement are therefore legalistically structured, permitted under clerical guidance, codified in charters and concords, and woven into civic observance without the layer of unease that so often colours Orthodox practice. At the same time, Kharunite arguments for understanding invoking as just another form of prayer are firmly rejected as a step beyond measure and into peril.

The weight placed on the Triune Family gives Concordian pastoral theology a specific shape. Marriage is not, in this tradition, merely a social arrangement governed by canon or a question of household management; it is a creedal act -- the deliberate formation of the first social triad, the smallest unit in which "Three is Six" becomes a lived reality rather than an abstraction. Concordian teaching accordingly treats the positions of Father, Mother, and Child not as a hierarchy of authority but as three mutually constitutive relations, none of which has meaning outside the others. A father is only a father in relation to his co-parent and child; the child is defined by those who tend it. Canon law in the West reflects this orientation: disputes over marriage and household are handled through conciliation and mediation before dissolution is countenanced, with something of the same Redemptionist sensibility that brings harmony tables to bear on broken covenants in other spheres of life. The household that holds its covenant -- spouses keeping faith with one another, parents tending their wards, children honouring their elders -- is not merely behaving well. It is enacting the creed at the root.

This civic and legal sensibility shapes Concordian liturgy and everyday practice, which leans strongly vernacular, with the Verses, prayers, and homilies commonly given in accessible language, and only the most solemn parts kept in the old ritual speech. Great weight is placed on voluntary lay work as an instrument of witness, and more almshouses, orphanages, and hospitals are in civic hands than the church's. The high feast days are more often characterized by processions that move from cathedral to town hall to guildhouse, enfolding the whole city in prayer and public generosity. Each station in this procession corresponds to a social relation: the cathedral enacts the upward relation to the Light; the town hall enacts the Elder and Liegelord relations, the obligations of those who hold authority to those over whom it is exercised; the guildhouse enacts the Peer relation, mutual obligation among equals in shared craft and commerce. A procession that moves through all three stations has therefore performed a creedal act -- walked the Six Relations in public, in sequence, in the company of the whole city -- as well as a civic one. This is the doctrinal basis for what Orthodox clergy sometimes decry as a lack of solemnity: the solemnity is structural rather than hieratic, present in the shape of the event rather than concentrated at an altar.

The Common Board is an instance of how Concordianism takes social customs and elevates them. Every parish of the West has one, usually a long hall of trestles and benches, a standing hearth, and a pot that is virtually never cold. Here the stranger passing through is fed just as well as the parish itself. The custom is older than the Church of the West and descends from the hospitality of the public house. Where in the wider Lurist calendar such a feeding of the commons survives only as the Fallow Tables, a single day at the close of harvest, the Commonwealth keeps it every day of the year, and the Fallow Tables is instead when the Board spills out of its hall onto the streets and the parish green, becoming a fair in the process.

The canon does not require a parish to keep a Board, but no synod has ever tried to abolish the practice. Instead the Church of the West contributes to the custom with patronage and a favorable doctrinal reading, a familiar Concordian one: Hospitality between host and guest is one of the Six Relations; scaled to a parish, every member of it becomes host to the stranger and guest of the parish in turn, at once. In keeping with common forms of hospitality the world over, the stranger, the child, and the infirm are served first. The elders of the parish and what wardens it chooses carry the dishes and serve the food, making seniority also show as duty. The lord, guild, or see that endows the pantry holds the Board's charter and may humbly sets its arms on the wall, but supporting the Common Board is understood as an obligation rather than a gift, so that it comes with no announcement and incurs no debt. The parish in its diverse web of the Six Relations sits communally, shoulder to shoulder, and all eat from the same pot.

Form varies by parish, such that in cities it may be found in a parish hall beside the chapel it shares a hearth-wall with it, while elsewhere in the country it may enclose the shrine entire, with the shrine at the head of the hall and the benches running down its length as a nave. Either arrangement serves the Concordian claim that solemnity lives in the shape of the event, since the meal is the observance. The Board's hearth tends to hold an unusually settled sprite, and the parish's courtesies to it are among the few parts of the custom the clergy have codified, as the Concordian habit with elemental appeasement would predict.

Owing to its departure from the mainstream of Orthodoxy in the wake of the fall of An-Mirda, Church and State in the West remain intricately entangled, though significantly less so than in the Theocracy of old. Each prince-bishop holds temporal power within the domain of his see, but not beyond it, and civil law in turn respects the liberties and properties of the Church under the Covenant of Concord. Canon changes proceed by a kind of gentlemen's agreement: when the Synod of Santra or a regional synod adopts a new ruling, the same prince-bishops and clerical deputies who framed it in council carry it into the Most High Court, where they use their places in Parliament to declare synodal consensus and to seek corresponding civil assent. In theory Church and Commonwealth are distinct, yet in practice they usually share titles and faces, as well as ledgers and letter-seals. The "Three is Six" framework supplies the doctrinal rationale for this entanglement that historical habit alone does not. The civic order is not, in Concordian teaching, a secular parallel to the sacred order; it is the institutional form of the Six Relations through which the creed is enacted in society. A corrupt Parliament is not merely a political problem but a disordered social relation -- a failure of the Elder and Liegelord obligations that Parliament exists to discharge. Prince-bishops are present in Parliament not as a residue of theocratic ambition but because the relations Parliament instantiates are themselves matters of creedal consequence. The Church's conciliar instinct -- its preference for deliberation over decree, synod over primate -- is therefore both an ecclesiological habit and a cosmological one: it reflects the belief that the Light becomes visible in a community through its collective harmonising, not through any single voice that claims to speak for it.

Socially, Concordianism tends to be somewhat less rigid in its expectations than orthodox Lurism. Long and close cultural exchange with Svenu to the south has worn down some of the sharper edges of gendered prescriptions, and the Commonwealth's own habits have moved in the same direction; but there is also a doctrinal basis for this latitude that is specific to Concordian theology. The relational reading of the Triune Family holds that Father, Mother, and Child are mutually constitutive positions rather than a rank-ordering of authority: each is defined by its relation to the others, and none is superior to the others in the creedal sense. The relevant question, when any position in the Six Relations is at stake, is always whether the relation is being faithfully discharged -- whether the Elder tends the Ward, whether the Peer keeps covenant with the Peer -- rather than what sex or station the person occupying that position happens to be. It is from this orientation, as much as from cultural exchange, that Concordian clergy find it less troubling when women serve as magistrates, heads of merchant houses, or guild-elders, so long as concord is strengthened thereby and Right Habit observed in public life. The West is hardly a land of easy equality, and the framework does not dissolve the positions themselves: a household in which no one occupies the Elder relation toward the children is not liberated but disordered. The aim is not to erase the distinctions but to ensure they are fulfilled faithfully by whoever holds them, with the relational obligation rather than the person's station as the measure.

In sum, Concordianism is a decentralized and decidedly civic-minded stream of Lurism, but it is not merely a procedural or political one. It has a creedal centre of its own: the movement the Lurist formula calls "Three is Six," the passage from the personal harmony of Body, Mind, and Soul outward through the Triune Family into the full web of the Six Social Relations. Where Orthodoxy reads the creed's weight in its first clause -- "One is Three," the Light approached inwardly -- and the Kharunites read it in the last -- "Six is All," the Light encountered in the world's saturated totality -- Concordianism insists that the hinge between the two is itself the sacred thing. The social world is not merely a consequence of inner harmony; it is the form in which inner harmony becomes fully real. Concordianism honours the Hierophant by name, but resists his decrees by nature; it shares creed and sacrament with orthodox Lurism, but insists that no single throne can adequately mirror the Light, no matter its location. Where Orthodoxy sees safety in a single well-tuned voice, the Autocephalous Church of the West insists that the true music of the faith emerges only when many voices keep in tune -- and that the keeping of that tune, across household and guild and council and court, is itself the act of worship.
