## Names and Setting

In the west the Velkirian communion is usually filed under one of three names: the "Eastern Rite", the "Velkirian Rite", or simply the "Kharunites" after their founding saint. In turn, Kharunite theologians speak of their western cousins as the "Anish Rite" and as "Virellites" after Saint Virellian, who codified the office of the Hierophant; Concordians, being in communion with Orthodoxy, are folded into the same nomenclature. The mutual habit of renaming is not spite so much as quiet declaration of perspective: each tradition is convinced it stands on the older, straighter continuation of the Prophet's road.

Both rites hold to the same Light and recite the same creed: "One is Three is Six is All". Both honor the Prophet Lur-Illuhan and the early Judges and take the Book of Verses and the Book of Judges as foundational scripture. Yet the Velkirian Rite has, from its first growth in the eastern highlands, read the Revelation through a different lens: panentheist where the west is more strictly monist, more legalist where the west became hierarchical, more at ease with animist habit and old elemental lore where western orthodoxy grew increasingly nervous.

Western catechisms stress the Triune Harmony of Body, Mind, and Soul, ordered toward a final union with the Light beyond the world; Kharunite preachers speak more readily of the Light as present in all things and of harmony as something to be enacted here and now within the world. In their sermons it is not so much that souls depart to join a distant chorus as that they are refined into clearer participation in the Light that already saturates creation.

## Origins along the Salt Road

The Eastern Rite traces its distinctive character to Judge Saint Illut-Kharun of the Salt Road, usually shortened to Judge Saint Kharun. Born a scribe in -213 AR and martyred in -177 AR, Kharun followed the caravan and coastal routes of what later ages call the Salt Road, walking as far as Lu-Pto. Along these routes he met scattered clusters of early converts and traders who had heard the Prophet's verses but stood far from An-Mirda's supervision.

It was Kharun who first connected these distant congregations as one communion and set out simple rules of mutual aid, shared teaching, and just dealing on the road. His work made explicit what had been implicit in the Prophet's preaching: that the Light did not belong only to the Prophet's homeland, and that the bond of common measure and conscience could bridge desert, river, and sea.

Kharun's end came at the hands of Reme. Summoned before a hostile tribunal for his role in stoking unrest among provincial subjects, he refused to end his preaching. His execution by fire became, in eastern remembrance, both a martyrdom and a sign that the old imperial order was already judged wanting.

But his story did not end at the pyre. A circle of his disciples survived, carrying copies of their master's judgements and commentaries. Under growing Remean suspicion they abandoned the coast and travelled upriver along the Mer with merchant caravans and riverboats. In the highland trade cities that would later cohere into Vel Kiriya they found a culture already obsessed with measure, quota, and tribute, a world of carefully layered obligations.

Here they met a people already trained to think in balances and tallies, and the result was something neither wholly Anish nor wholly local. Kharun's disciples adapted the creed to the habits of the highland courts, stressing account and reciprocity as much as pure devotion. When An-Mirda finally learned of this eastern settlement several centuries later, it found not a wayward mission but a second center of gravity.

## The Six Relations of Society

Early Kharunite preachers did not reject the Lurist ethic of Right Habit, Right Attention, and Right Appetites, but they rooted it in a deliberately social frame. Where the western catechism speaks of the Heavenly Triad and of personal refinement, the east adds a second triad and sets it in motion across society.

Kharun's sermons, preserved in the Book of the Road, already speak of the Six Relations: Light, liegelord, elder, peer, liegeman, ward. In western recensions, the list often appears only as a hierarchy of duties upward: from ward to liegeman, liegeman to lord, lord to Light. Eastern exegesis keeps the same sixfold structure but insists that it must be balanced in both directions. One's acts toward one's lessers are as weighty as those toward one's betters, and imbalance in any direction distorts the whole.

From this follows the Kharunite insistence on reciprocity in law. A liegelord who abuses a liegeman sins as gravely against the Light as a liegeman who rebels without cause. An elder who neglects a ward violates harmony as surely as a ward who refuses guidance. This ethic proved attractive in the highland courts, where tribute and tax bound valley to capital in precise figures. Kharunite judges brought with them a theology that could speak both to conscience and to the purse, and the Eastern Rite has never since been shy about treating questions of debt, contract, and just price as matters of doctrine rather than mere mundanity.

## Light in All: Eastern Panentheism

Both rites preach that the One Light is impersonal, ineffable, the harmony in and behind all things. Western orthodoxy, especially after the rise of the Hierophancy, has tended to emphasize the first step of the creed: "One is Three" -- the Light as a unity glimpsed through the triad of Body, Mind, and Soul and approached most nearly when those three are brought into harmony. In this frame, death is the moment of appraisal: where the triad is balanced, Body and Mind relinquish their hold so that the Soul may rejoin the Light; where it is not ready, a new triad forms and returns to the world to seek further refinement.

Kharunite preaching accepts this teaching but places its weight elsewhere. In their sermons the stress falls on the last turn of the creed: "Six is All". The Light is not only a goal beyond the world; it is the totality of the world as experienced through the sixfold harmony of the elements and the intricate mesh of relations among living things. To speak of drawing nearer to the Light is therefore not to speak of departure, but of waking more fully into what already surrounds and permeates one -- a mode explicitly posited as what the Prophet first achieved.

In this frame, union with the Light does not require a change of place. Proper balance in one's natures, and yes, also between Body, Mind, and Soul, allows an individual to become one with the Light in All here and now. The saint is one whose triad has become so transparent to the Light that the distinction between self and world thins almost to nothing. In death such a person does not "go" anywhere so much as they cease to obstruct what was always there. A Kharunite will rarely speak of "the departed", for the dead are, in their understanding, all around, participating in the same Light in which the living move. Funerary rites in the east can be shockingly lively affairs for someone used to the orderly pyres and sombre mourning of the west.

Where western hymns dream of the Illuminated Choir as a distant chorus, Velkirian hymns more often imagine the same choir as coextensive with the wind sighing in the trees, the mist dancing on the Mer, the ring of the sea against the shore. The difference is subtle but runs deep in metaphysics.

## The Three Natures

Orthodox catechisms focus on the Heavenly Triad. The Eastern Rite accepts the triad but does not center it. Instead Velkirian theology speaks of the Three Natures that must be brought into accord: Beast, Man, and Saint.

In this teaching "Beast" names not animality as such but appetite, instinct, and the body's unreflective reach for pleasure or safety. "Man" names reason, craft, and social habit, the layer of law and custom that keeps households and cities from tearing themselves apart. "Saint" names the nascent capacity in every person to align both appetite and reason with the Light in All.

Kharunite preachers insist that all three natures are necessary. A life in which Beast is denied or abused produces brittle saints and hypocritical morals; one in which Man dominates without listening produces clever cruelty; one in which aspirant Saint tramples the others produces zeal that burns more than it heals. Proper harmony is not the silencing of Beast, but its schooling; not the exaltation of Man's cleverness, but its subordination to truth; not the flight of Saint from the world, but its steady work within it.

This framework shapes Velkirian readings of sin and merit. To sin is to let one nature tyrannize the rest, whether in the self or in society. Merit lies in the patient rebalancing, again and again, of all three.

## Scripture and the Book of the Road

Kharunites honor the Book of Verses as the direct fruit of the Prophet's enlightenment and the Book of Judges as the collected rulings and commentaries of his earliest successors. Yet they read both through a distinctive set of lenses.

Foremost among these are the Kharunite commentaries, compiled over centuries as Judges of the Eastern Rite glossed the Verses and the earlier Judges' rulings in light of the Concord of Flame and the realities of highland life. These commentaries are often organized not by author but by topic: debt, hospitality, guardianship, war-levies, river tolls, the rights of caravan crews. The same verse may therefore appear under many headings, its implications teased out differently depending on the relation in question.

Alongside the western Book of Judges the Eastern Rite sets the Book of the Road. The first layers of this text are attributed to Saint Kharun himself: notes on his travels, rulings given in caravanserai courtyards, short verses composed to be recited on the march. Later Judges of the Velkirian Rite added their own itinerant jurisprudence: how to weigh the word of a factor against that of a drover, how to treat a shrine of Maregina at a ferry in a Lurist town, how to reckon the obligations of a warhost raised from tributary hill-villages.

After Kharun's martyrdom the Judgeship in the east never settled into the stationary princely form that later produced the Hierophants at An-Mirda and Aramoor. In fact the Velkirian Rite never accepted the notion of a single high pontiff at all. Its leadership instead remains formally mendicant: Judges are ordained precisely so that they may walk, hear, and decide. Even the senior Judges resident at Vel Kiriya's courts are expected to take to the road in regular rotation.

Where western canon law tends to pile rulings into thick codices, Velkirian law is still traditionally oral, carried in the memories of walking judges. This is not a mark of backwardness; it reflects the Rite's conviction that law detached from journey and encounter risks becoming mere self-regard. Naturally forms of scripture exist, and Kharunite stelae bearing a Judge's inscriptions are some of the earliest forms of eastern jurisprudence; but the focus in court remains on context-rich investigation and extrapolation of the wandering Judge, not merely delving into precedent.

## Saints, Elementals, and Syncretism

The early spread of the faith into An-Suur and the Merhal did not immediately erase older practices of elemental invocation. In the western lands these customs linger today mainly in rustic charms and half-proscribed rites at wells and fields. Lurist orthodoxy, especially in its Antidiscordian moods, has long been nervous about any practice that seems to treat the Noble Elements as if they were gods.

In the eastern highlands, by contrast, this nervousness never properly took root. Instead, Kharunite theology -- informed by a strong pre-Lurist animist tradition -- insists that the Noble Six and their courts are necessarily *of* the Light. They are potent concentrations of the sixfold harmony, not rivals to the Light that generates that harmony. Elemental beings, while not to be worshipped as ultimate, may still be lawfully petitioned. In the same breath, saints are understood as human natures so transparent to the Light that they, too, become potent intercessors. The distinction between venerating a saint and addressing an elemental is not the sharp barrier it became in the west.

In practice, this means that a single Velkirian shrine may bear a simple creed-stela, a niche for a saint's memory, and a basin where offerings to Maregina or Piatros are poured, without any tension or contradiction. Only a sailor freshly arrived from the Circle Sea would find the arrangement unsettling; a local sees a single act of address to the Light in its many modes.

This syncretism is not a mere matter of laxity. Shrine-wardens of the Eastern Rite are drilled in elemental lore and in the old grammar of invocations, and they are often able, when pressed, to conjure and bind sprites or even powerful courtiers into more orderly service. Where western clergy speak sternly about avoiding "heathen superstitions", Velkirian judges prefer to ensure that the inevitable dealings with spirits are conducted within a lawful frame.

Unsurprisingly, Antidiscordian zealots in the west foam at the mouth when contemplating this habit. The Velkirian response is typically dry: if the Light is truly All, then to refuse to acknowledge its operations in the Noble Six is a form of practical atheism.

## The Fall of Reme and the Question of Fire

The destruction of Reme at the hands of Phlogistophor is an inflection point in all Lurist historiography. Western sermons have long cast it as the archetype of hubris punished: Reme, arrogant in its Shaping and unbelief, sought to bind the Noble Fire as a beast to their yoke and was annihilated. The Scourge of Reme stands as warning against the inward arts and any attempt to compel the elements by force rather than by harmony.

The Eastern Rite does not deny the event, but it reads it differently. Velkirian commentaries agree that the last Remean rulers sinned in attempting to compel what cannot lawfully be compelled. Yet they note that the early Theocracy was quick to claim the event as its own victory, and that this haste blurs the catastrophe into simply a punishment of Reme, when it must more clearly be read as a rebuke of overreach and self-aggrandizing zealotry.

Velkirian sermons on the day of Ashen Remembrance speak more of measure than of triumph. The lesson, in their telling, is that any polity -- Remean, Theocratic, or indeed modern -- that attempts to bend the Noble Elements or human bodies to its own pride without regard for the wider harmony courts a similar correction. This reading does nothing to soften Antidiscordian ire.

## History with the Western Rite

In the early centuries the Velkirian Rite grew in relative obscurity. The faithful in An-Mirda knew of Judge Saint Kharun and honored his martyrdom; they did not fully grasp what was growing upriver. Busy first with the long conflict against Reme and later with the consolidation of the Theocracy of the Eternal Dominion, the western church had little attention to spare for what seemed a distant cluster of congregations in the mountains.

By the time the First Hierophancy took formal shape, the Eastern Rite had already had two centuries to harden its customs and develop its own jurisprudence. When news filtered back of these "Kharunites" who honored the Prophet yet refused to acknowledge the Hierophant's primacy, reaction in An-Mirda fluctuated between irritation and condescension. The zeal of the early Theocracy was turned mostly toward nearer targets: the suppression of old elemental cults along the river Varsa, the crusades in the Wolves' Teeth, the consolidation of both An-Suur and Stranu.

When Ejitwe the Thronebreaker sacked An-Mirda in 336 AR and the First Hierophancy collapsed, the western church entered its long age of fracture and embarrassed reform. From the pulpits of the Eastern Rite the event was interpreted, without much attempt at diplomacy, as the Light correcting an imbalance. The Theocracy, in Velkirian telling, had become as proud and deaf as Reme once was; the Thronebreaker merely provided the last needed nudge.

Through the centuries of the Second Hierophancy and the Interregnum, as rival primacies rose and fell and western synods shouted at one another over doctrine and jurisdiction, the Velkirian Rite quietly expanded. Judges of the Eastern Rite extended their influence along the upper Merhal and deep into Riv-î-Tep and the Drowned Coast, carried by caravans and river fleets into regions where resentment of western patriarchal authority was already ripening. Communities that felt ill served by the Patriarchates began experimenting with Velkirian practice, finding in it a model of piety that did not demand submission to Aramoor or to shadows of the old Theocracy.

When Genovrast de Lavion at last hammered the western church into the more modest pattern of the Third Hierophancy, the new communion formally listed the Velkirian Rite as schismatic, then quietly turned its attention elsewhere. No one in Virell or Aramoor had any appetite for marching armies across the Mer to argue metaphysics with the King of Kings in Paxar Vel. In practice the Patriarchate of the Merhal adopted a policy of strained cordiality, treating the Velkirian church as an awkward partner: clearly outside the fold, yet impossible to ignore.

By the 13th to 15th centuries, calling the Eastern Rite heretical in polite western company began to feel provincial, though the theological points of contention remain unresolved. However, Concordianist clergy in the Commonwealth even came to prize Velkirian scholars as sparring partners in questions of law, debt, and social justice. Only Antidiscordian die-hards persisted in shouting "Kharunite heresy" in earnest.

## Geography and Present Practice

Today the Velkirian Rite is the state faith of every land that pays tribute to the King of Kings in Paxhar Vel. In the highland capital itself the great courts erect stelae for the Prophetic Verses and libraries for their Kharunite commentaries, and the senior Judges sit alongside secular magistrates, as questions of law always also touch on questions of conscience.

Throughout the eastern marches the landscape of devotion is dense with shrines. Villages keep waystones inscribed with the creed and the Six Relations; larger cities host gardens and courtyards where sermons can be held and judgements rendered. In the heartlands of the Vel Kiriya, the Western Rite is found mainly in foreigners' quarters and in a few riverside basilicas maintained by western missionary orders.

Along the Merhal and in Riv-î-Tep the situation is more complicated. Centuries of overlap have produced a patchwork of parishes and obediences. Some cities seat paired clergy: a Virellite bishop and a Kharunite judge sharing choirs and fighting over appointments, their joint processions a carefully choreographed tangle of vestments. Other towns are functionally Velkirian while remaining nominally under the Merhal patriarch, because no one wishes to reopen old parish boundaries. In the frontier polities of the Drowned Coast, Velkirian clergy are often preferred: stricter on contract and debt, softer on old elemental customs, they offer an alternative to both Lavion's royal pretensions and the memory of the Theocracy.

In the western cities the Velkirian presence is quieter but persistent. In Balboa, Virell, and the ports of the Commonwealth, unassuming Kharunite houses can be found in dockside wards and merchant streets. Their facades are plain except for stone stelae bearing the creed and a carved road-mark. Inside, their courtyards are lavishly planted and tiled, for the shrine is the garden rather than a basilican hall. Local judges conduct their sermons as literal walkabouts: leading the congregation through the neighborhood when magistrates allow it, or along covered cloisters when they do not.

## Liturgical Tone and Devotional Life

Velkirian liturgy is less concerned with harmony than with continuity. Chanted recitations of the Verses follow the familiar sixfold and threefold patterns of the wider communion, but every homily is expected to return, sooner or later, to a question of relation. A sermon that does not name at least one of the Six Relations is regarded as unfinished.

The mendicant tradition of the Judges shapes the devotional calendar. Pilgrimage in the Eastern Rite is not only a journey to fixed holy sites, but also a patterned accompaniment of traveling judges along sections of the road. To walk three days with a Judge, hearing cases by the wayside and observing how he balances Beast, Man, and Saint in himself, is considered as meritorious as visiting three stationary shrines.

Household devotion focuses less on individual salvation and more on the steady tending of relations: between spouses, between pledgers and creditors, between parents and wards, between the household and its local sprites. Blessings over the hearth may invoke both the Light and Phlogistophor as Hearth-Warden; libations at a cistern may name both the Light and Maregina as Dancer-in-Rain. Western visitors often find such prayers dangerously close to the line; Velkirian hosts reply that the Light does not mind being greeted under many titles.

## Reputation and Self-Understanding

From the western side of the Mer, the Eastern Rite has worn many labels: heresy, schism, quaint hill piety, dangerous softness toward heathen rites, or, more recently, simply "that other church that happens to rule the east". Western Antidiscordianists still publish fervent denunciations of Kharunite syncretism and their refusal to renounce elemental invocation.

Privately, however, the cooler heads of the Third Hierophancy acknowledge what they will not say aloud: that in the east, only Vel Kiriya's church can look Lavion's crown and councils in the eye without blinking, in trade as in theology. Civic jurists in Virell grumble about Velkirian influence on debt reform even as they borrow its language. Concordianist scholars cite Velkirian commentaries approvingly in questions of social justice and the obligations of the powerful.

The Velkirian Rite, for its part, sees itself not as a rival church but as the kept promise of the early faith: a communion that refused to abandon the Judgeship to princely vanity, refused to sever law from conscience, and refused to pretend that the Light's presence could be neatly confined to church walls.
