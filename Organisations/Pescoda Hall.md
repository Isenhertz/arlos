## Setting and approach

Pescoda Hall, the estate of the Princely House of Balboa near the capital of Lavion, stands just beyond the city bounds of Virell, fronting the Royal Road that runs southwesterly toward the old town of Lavion and the Castle Royal, family seat of House de Lavion. The site sits on a gentle shelf above the Varsa's upper meanders, close enough to hear the distant thunder of the White Falls in certain winds, yet screened from road dust by formal hedges and a double line of plane trees. The main gatehouse faces east to greet arrivals travelling west from the capital. From there a straight, slightly rising carriage avenue leads <abbr title="ca. 8.26m">a quarter chain</abbr> to the Outer Court, giving processional dignity and separating arriving coaches from through traffic. This axial approach is very Lavienese in spirit, but what it reveals is distinctly Balboan at heart. The château was always meant as a place to connect the Principality's maritime heritage to Lavion's landward court, and its plan makes that plain.

## The plan in one glance

The house resolves as a square composition oriented true north, built around two successive courts. The first is the Outer Court, an open arrival quad bounded by the corps de logis to the east and low flanking galleries to north and south. Passing under the central loggia, one steps into the Cortile della Pescoda, the true Balboan heart of the house -- a square internal patio with a shallow rill and a circular basin at center, tiled in blue green and ringed by arcades. The whole great square is a measured unit: the outer front is a full <abbr title="69.12m by 51.84m">2 chains across and 1½ chains deep</abbr> from gatehouse to loggia; the cortile is a true square of 1 chain by 1 chain; the Parterre of Gyres extends <abbr title="207.36m">6 chains</abbr> south from the corps de logis; the precinct wall runs a square of <abbr title="345.60m">10 chains</abbr> on a side.

## Arrival and public rooms

Coaches turn off the Royal Road through the west gatehouse, passing a porter's lodge and guardroom, then draw up in the Outer Court. A deep, five bay loggia, frescoed with Balboan sea motifs and Lavienese sunbursts, opens into a broad, cool vestibule paved in alternating limestone and verd antique. To left and right, processional antechambers feed the great receiving hall, the Salle d'Honneur, a high room used for audiences and levees in season. Beyond lies the cortile arcade, whose shade and water temper Virell's summer heat. On gala nights guests are drawn upstairs by the principal stair -- a wide, easy Lavienese climb -- to a west facing Great Gallery that runs the length of the corps de logis, its windows catching the sunset. A chapel of the Light opens off the south side of the gallery for discreet devotions before or after court functions, its fittings following the quiet Harmonist line -- proportion, clarity, and spare ornament -- softened by local craft. More important is the Library -- not a collection of historiographies and vain exortations of the family line, but a proper research library, hosting works from every renowned scholar of the Circulean going back to the First Hierophancy and some that go further back still, deemed peculiar and even borderline heretical.

## The Balboan heart

The Cortile della Pescoda is the Principessa's favorite room without a roof. Its perimeter is a continuous arcade of square piers and shallow arches, each bay opening to a salon, studiolo, or cabinet. At center a round basin throws a low plume; from it, thin rills run in the paving to the four sides, cooling air as they go and whispering in the heat of noon. Balboan townhouses teach that a house is kept in right temper by water held at its core, and here that sensibility moderates Lavienese grandeur. In winter, removable glazing of finely cast panes closes the arcades against drafts; the panes are prized for their flawlessness, and the fittings are light enough for two footmen to set in place quickly.

## Private apartments

The Principessa's suite occupies the quiet northeast corner above the cortile: a morning room over the fountain, a withdrawing room with corner fireplace, a bedchamber in Balboan mode opening to a private loggia, a grand walk-in wardrobe for gowns and jewels, and a little studiolo lined with pale woods and white plaster, set up for reading, cataloguing, and everything else the Principessa's own archaeological interest needs. A narrow stair rises again to a secluded terrace garden on the roof, screened by low parapets and citrus tubs. Family and intimate guests occupy the southeast corner on the same level, with a parallel suite reserved in the northwest for royal or archducal visitors who choose to lodge rather than return to town after fêtes.

## Service axes and Ellios's domain

The house works because it moves quietly. A continuous service spine runs east to west behind the public rooms at ground level, tying kitchen court to sculleries, pantries, and the buttery, and continuing to the steward's counting house, the cellar stairs, and the staff bedrooms below. Ellios, the Principessa's imperturbable majordomo, keeps his office at the hinge of this spine and the cortile's north arcade, where he can see both formal and household currents without being seen. Service stairs with broad, shallow treads rise at each corner to the bedchambers and closets; separate tradesmen's doors open to the kitchen court so carts never wheel over the front stones.

## Kitchens, stores, and craft

Balboan cuisine travels with the household, so the kitchens sit near the cortile's cool shade, vented to tall flues. The cookroom uses charcoal and spirit stoves, with compressed air for bellows work and certain tools in the scullery and bakehouse. A small pressure house in the service yard keeps the lines charged, worked largely by wind and a discreet regenerator -- clean, quiet, and in fashion among Virell's better houses. The larder range includes a dairy, a stillroom, a confectioner's corner, and a long, low ice vault built into the garden bank. The vault's lining includes close set glass courses that hold chill well into late summer.

## Outbuildings and grounds

The estate precinct, a neat square of 10 chains by 10 chains, is walled in pale stone and planted in four great compartments around the house. To the south runs the Parterre of Gyres, a clipped embroidery of low hedges, gravel, and pools that mirror the sky when calm and shiver in the wind. To the north lies the Kitchen Close, with espaliers, herb knots, and an orchard that supplies the stillroom. West of the outer court stand the stables, coach house, wheelwright's shed, and a gryphery for the small singing house gryphs favored by Balboan households as alarms and companions. East of the house, beyond a bosquet, is the Landing Pond, a small water feature bare of ornamention safe for elaborate lily pads and sea-roses as well as a water fountain that gives way to a floating jetty and mooring winches when the Principessa's tiltwing courier sets down, should she choose to arrive by air. A hedged walk leads from jetty to a private door at the east loggia, so her step from air to salon can be as public or discreet as she wishes. A canalized rill runs along the south garden wall to feed the cortile basin and the reflecting pools before returning to a fishpond by the orchard, keeping water in slow, cooling conversation with stone.

## Ceremonial sequence, day and night

On ordinary days, callers are received west to east -- gate to court, court to vestibule, vestibule to cortile and salons. On nights that draw the great world, the route is turned vertical: carriages enter, set down under the loggia, ladies and gentlemen cross the vestibule under painted stars, and ascend the principal stair to the Great Gallery. Here card tables, musicians, and a buffet can run the length, while side doors open to the Conservatory Room and the Sea Room, where painted Gran Pescoda fishtails ride surf across stuccoed cornices in a wink to the Principessa's city. During festivities the cortile fountains are trimmed to a higher plume and lanterns are lit under the arcades; in that half indoor, half open square, guests can mingle and parlay without feeling boxed in as they listen to the play of the water.

## Working yards and discreet industry

Beyond the stables, a second, hidden service court takes deliveries. Charcoal, spirits, and oils arrive in casks, and market wagons bring greens and fish in the early hours. A small smithy and carpentry shop handle repairs; the smith also tends the airship moorings and the locking frames for the removable winter glazing. The laundry sits downwind in a long, low range with drying lofts. A narrow postern gives onto the Royal Road for messengers and for the manor guards to come and go without parading through the front.

A novelty is the addition of a garage to the stables; here is housed the Principessa's own aetheromobile, a gleaming horseless carriage of silver and alumin with three axles and a narrow, glass-fronted driver's cabin separated from the passenger cabin. Its hum is well known on the Royal Road, and people gather to gawk and babble when the Principessa decides to use it to make a grand entry to the city.

## Materials and expression

The façades toward the Outer Court are Lavienese -- rusticated ground floor, tall windows with crisp stone surrounds, a restrained cornice, and high roofs with dormers. The cortile is Balboan -- smooth plaster, painted soffits, blue green tile, and shadow. Floors under the arcades are cool to the foot; thresholds are shallow so gowns do not catch; door heads are a little higher than fashion in Virell, because the arcades teach the eye to love more air, but also because its current owner -- the Principessa Lauralotta di Balboa -- is famously tall. Glass throughout is clear and taut, with the winter closures deliberately flawless in their casting. The house reads as a marriage of styles that keeps both partners' dignity intact.

## A note on sympathies

Pescoda Hall's name and art nod to the Balboan sea and its fierce gryphs, while its outlook and approach speak Lavion. The chapel keeps a sober Harmonist line even though the current Principessa is known to be a scholar of heathen history. With the Principessa an absolute powerhouse in Lavienese politics -- moreso than any of her predecessors -- the place is therefore not merely a pied a terre but a working stage of politics and hospitality.

## Household establishment and staff
Pescoda Hall keeps a deliberately intimate establishment by princely standards. In ordinary months the resident staff numbers between three to four dozen. When feasts and great nights are proclaimed, the Hall scales up by hiring local hands of good character drawn from Virell’s guild families and reputable houses; on such occasions the household may rise to five or six dozen without losing its quiet composure.

### Stewardry and accounts
Ellios the majordomo directs the whole, with a single under steward and one clerk who keeps the books and wages in good order. The stewardry sets the daily rhythm -- bells, table, and roster -- and keeps the Hall balanced between ceremony and household peace.

### Front of house
A butler holds the hall and the wine keys, supported by one under butler, a hall porter, and four footmen in Balboa's blue and silver livery. By day, posts stand at gate, loggia, and stair; after dusk a reduced watch serves with soft shoes and dark coats. On gala nights, additional ushers and buffet men are hired from town so service appears to happen by itself.

### Housekeeping and chambers
Mrs. Harmeline Faine the housekeeper governs chamber and parlour. On an ordinary day three chambermaids keep the private floor in order, one parlourmaid tends the cortile arcades, and a resident seamstress repairs lace and liveries. The night duty rotates among the chambermaids; lamp tending is folded into the porter’s round. A stillroom maid works under the housekeeper but is counted with the kitchens when preserving is in season.

### Library and study rooms
There is no resident librarian or copyist. The Principessa, being a Magistra of the Academy, keeps her own catalogue and pursues her studies without intermediaries. An amanuensis manages correspondence, interlibrary loans, scheduling of scholars’ desks, and the care of commonplace books. When heavy work is required, assistance comes from either the footmen, or porters hired locally by the day.

### Chapel of the Light (Harmonist)
There is no resident chaplain. The Principessa dismissed the office -- scandalously, some say -- preferring keep the chapel as a place of proportion and clarity for private contemplation. The butler and housekeeper maintain linens and vessels, and a visiting cleric may be invited for memorials or state occasions.

### Kitchens and cellars
A chef de cuisine commands a sous chef, a pastry cook who doubles as baker, two kitchen maids, and two scullions. The stillroom answers to the housekeeper but works in concert with the pastry. The cellars are kept by the butler; there is no separate sommelier. Water from the cortile rill is filtered and cooled in the ice vault for table use. For state collation the kitchen scales by hiring reputable cooks and scullions from Virell.

### Laundry and stores
A laundress with one washer and one ironer keeps linens, laces, and uniforms in strict rotation. Stores are held by the under steward acting as quartermaster, aided by a larder man when preserving or butchering requires extra hands. Deliveries pass the hidden court; nothing coarse crosses the Outer Court by daylight if it can be helped.

### Stables, garage, and air service
A master of horse leads two grooms, with a single coachman. The garage holds the aetheromobile, tended by a chauffeur who is also a capable mechanic. The Landing Pond relies on a mast master alone in ordinary time; riggers and ground hands are pulled from the footmen or guards when the tiltwing courier is due.

### Grounds, gryphery, and security
A head gardener with two gardeners keeps parterres, espaliers, and citrus tubs immaculate. The gryphery is minded by a keeper whose gryphs’ voices form part of the Hall’s alarm. A captain of the guard with a dozen house guards maintains the watch at gate and postern, with a discreet rotation to accompany the Principessa in town without show; the men are not hired but come seconded from the Principality's great mercenary companies -- a posting understandably much sought after, selecting for men of skill, discretion, and a certain quiet flamboyance that behoofs the fame of Balboa's finest.

### Pressure house and aetheric cabinet
The pressure house and mechanicals are tended by a resident engineer with one oiler and turner. There is no curator or conservator for the aetheric cabinet. The Principessa supervises the cabinet herself; the amanuensis keeps the register and arranges demonstrations. When extra hands are prudent, trained assistants are hired locally for the day and then dismissed.

### Seasonal augmentation
When the Hall stands in full light -- receptions, musicales, diplomatic teas -- the household enlarges by hiring local hands of good character: extra footmen, buffet men, singers, florists, lamp men, cloakroom attendants, and ground crew. Numbers rise to five or six dozen for a night, then fold back to the intimate, three dozen core by morning.
