## Definition and Character

Varngard is the translupine republic south of the Wolves' Teeth Mountains -- called the Lupines by those who live in their shadow -- occupying the eastern shore of the Southern Gulf across the water from Svenu. Its people descend from the same Svenish boat-clans attested since the Remean era along the Open Ocean rim, and the kinship shows in speech, name-stock, and old custom alike; but generations of settled river and portage wealth, closer contact with Lurism, and a harder-won institutional history have pulled Varngard's political character toward something the Circulean world can recognize as a state, where Svenu's remains a looser confederation of clans under the All-Thing. The two peoples share blood and a long, half-affectionate, half-resentful memory of raiding each other's coasts, and are for that reason not so close as shared ancestry alone would suggest.

Varngard styles itself, in poetic register, the Holdfast of Oar and Axe -- oar for the river- and sea-trade that built its wealth, axe for the warband heritage its republic never fully set down. In everyday and diplomatic use it is simply Varngard.

## Geography

Varngard's territory is defined by two river systems and the marsh country that links them. The Varn, for which the land is named, rises in the Lupines and runs south before bending west into the Southern Gulf -- a short, dependable river that empties reliably into salt water every year regardless of season, and Varngard's steady link to Circulean maritime trade. The Swicca rises likewise in the Lupines but turns east on clearing the mountains, becoming navigable from that turn onward and running a long course toward the evaporative terminus of the Deserted Sea in Lu-An -- a river of far greater length and reach than the Varn, but an unreliable one: in wet years its waters reach the Deserted Sea proper and open a barge-route deep toward Karsova and the Lu-An litoral, while in dry years the river loses its long fight against the earth well short of any true sea, and eastern trade falls back on caravan roads instead.

Between the two rivers, well south of the Lupines and at the closest approach of their southward courses before each turns to its own compass point, lies a country of countless lakes and threading marsh: waters that expand and contract from year to year, channels that shift, and a portage between the Varn and Swicca watersheds that is navigated by local knowledge as much as by any fixed road. The settlement of Myrholm sits on higher ground within this country, commanding the carry between the two river systems and, with it, the tolls that made Varngard's consolidating river-houses wealthy.

Up-coast lie Haldorshavn and Vargness, fishing towns fattened into customs posts; inland stand lake-cities like Tervin and Albrunna, which load barges under the teeth-like spurs of the Lupines. The Gulf ports guard their harbor rights jealously, and keep chain-booms that seal their inner roads when trouble rides the wind.

To the north Varngard borders the Wolves' Teeth Mountains and, beyond them, Lavion's southern marches; to the west lies the Southern Gulf and Svenu on its far shore; to the east, along the lower Swicca and its litoral, Varngard borders Karsova, the horse-lord country with ties to the nomads of Lu-An. Toward Stranu in the north there is no direct frontier: the space between is understood to hold room for minor polities not yet named.

## Trade and Economy

Varngard's wealth rests on a trade surplus that made it, for centuries, a valued and unthreatening partner to the Circulean world rather than a target worth the cost of conquest. Foremost among its exports is bog iron, drawn from the marsh country's own soil -- a southern source of a metal scarce across the continent generally, and one that has always traded favorably, whether carried north by sea or over the passes into Lavion proper. The greater part of it, however, crosses the Gulf to Svenu, where the svartalfr -- a lone enclave of the Moonkin that never fell to the diaspora scattering their kin from the Wolves' Teeth, and whose long Alvai lives have made them without rival among smiths -- work it into steel with no equal anywhere in Arlos. That renown reflects back on Varngard only obliquely: it is Svenu that Circulean merchants credit with the finest blades, though those who deal directly in the ore know its true source.

The same forests that supply Varngard's timber also yield turpentine and pine resin in quantity, moving downriver and across the Gulf to shipwrights and preservers alike. A third product of those forests is varnite, a fossilized resin gathered along the Gulf shore and worked as jewelry for most of Varngard's history -- warm-hued, easily carved, and prized purely as ornament until Aetheric Age artificers discovered its unusual resonant properties and its worth to choir-engine and resonance-craft work. That discovery turned a modest decorative export into a strategic material, and is no small part of what drew Roburic II's eye south in earnest. Furs, hides, wax, honey, and salted or dried fish round out the list -- a solid, unglamorous trade that built Varngard's houses long before iron and varnite gave an emperor reason to want the country outright rather than merely trade with it.

Raids from Svenu and Lu-An taught the coast to drill: each town keeps a ready corvée of archers and demands a bill and axe in each household; each harbor watches the water with chain, fire-raft, and signal-mast. Trade policy is militant in its own way: tolls are tiered by hull width and draft, lighterage is a guild monopoly, and the Hall keeps a close eye on who travels through the portage.

## Faith

Varngard falls under the Autonomous Church of the Southern Reach, seated at Hjalmsvik on Varngard's own shore of the Gulf, whose writ extends across the water to Svenu's as readily as along Varngard's own -- a church that grew up among boat-clans and coastal parishes was never going to be bound by the water it crossed. Varngardi practice inherits the Southern Reach's character wholesale: a plain, domestic, hearth-centered preaching style that emphasizes the Eternal Fire's warmth over the North's sun-gazing enlightenment, long habits of appeasing and exalting the harsher elements, and a matrilineal accommodation that ordains women as deaconesses, abbesses, and chapterwardens in defiance of no doctrine, only of custom elsewhere.

Varngard is nonetheless markedly more male-centered in its secular life than Svenu, and more thoroughly Lurist in temper -- both read, within Varngard, as marks of Circulean standing, an association with Orthodoxy across the Lupines that the settled river-houses have cultivated as prestige. This is a matter of status rather than law. An old matriarch is not opposed for being a woman; a capable daughter is not barred from headship by any statute. But a daughter is more often quietly encouraged toward the chapterhouse, the household, or an advantageous marriage than toward the Hall, and the formal, public offices with the clearest Circulean association -- Hall representation, war-leadership, the republic's own headship -- trend male by custom, patronage, and the ordinary weight of who a house puts forward, not by rule. Ecclesiastical authority remains the clearest counter-current: a matriarch shut out of the council chamber may still hold real and unremarked institutional weight as an abbess or senior chapterwarden.

For all its Circulean-facing formality, Varngardi devotion keeps a Svenish tongue in its mouth: the Noble Six are as often addressed by their old kinship epithets, worn down into Varngardi from the Svenic tongue, as by any Circulean name -- Saimodar for Water, Daudisamma for Ice, Styrisystar for Air, Arnvördar for Fire, Sprottefadar for Soil, Jaurnibrodar for Stone. A progressive house reciting formal Lurist prayer in the Southern Reach's own cadence will still, in the same breath of ordinary speech, call on Sæmóðr's Varngardi tongue-cousin Saimodar the Sea-Mother without feeling any contradiction in it.

## Society and Succession

Varngardi clans practice tanistry, Dugsidar in their own tongue: headship passes not by strict primogeniture but to the line's most capable adult, a custom already attested elsewhere in the Circle Sea basin as an old Svenic habit taken up in Stranu's rockfall- and raid-prone marches, and very likely native to Varngard and Svenu before it ever reached the Commonwealth. Tanistry does not by rule exclude a capable daughter from her line's headship, but custom leans hard against it absent unusual circumstance -- the same status-not-law logic that governs Hall representation operating at the level of the clan. Varngardi speech still carries a word for the custom tanistry replaced -- Hluttesidar, gavelkind -- though the practice itself has not been followed in living memory; the word survives as a fossil of a custom the clans moved past, not as a live alternative.

### Two Axes of Standing

No single Varngardi temperament governs how a clan or person is actually read by their neighbors; two independent axes cut across each other instead, and a house's position on one says nothing about where it sits on the other.

The first axis runs progressive against traditionalist -- how far a house has bought into Circulean ideals as against the older, Svenu-descended reckoning of honor by deed alone. A traditionalist house keeps faith with the Svenish reading regardless of its wealth: capability shown is what tanistry answers to, a person's fitness to lead or marry into a line is read from their own deeds, and little else is thought to matter. A progressive house has absorbed something closer to the Circulean unease with anything unforeseeable in a legible line -- not through Lurist theology as such, since most Varngardi houses are not reasoning from doctrine directly, but through the same underlying anxiety about standing that Circulean respectability trades on generally. This reticence travels: from how a house judges its own prospective tanists, into how it negotiates a marriage where a bloodline is at stake outright, and from there into how it reads other clans as allies or rivals, where anything in a kinsman that resists easy accounting is felt, however unfairly, as a mark against that whole clan's reliability rather than against the one person alone.

The second axis is the Hall's own familiar tension between old lineage and new money, and it does not track the first. The two together produce four live combinations rather than one line to draw. An old lineage house that has bought fully into Circulean standing has the most invested in looking settled and the least patience for anything that complicates that image. An old lineage house of traditionalist bent, by contrast, often has the standing to not need to prove anything at all, keeping the strict honor-and-deeds accounting and regarding its progressive-leaning peers' anxieties with a measure of quiet contempt. A new-money house chasing Circulean respectability -- through a funded warband, an endowment to the Southern Reach, whatever the means -- has the least inherited credit to fall back on if anything calls its reliability into question, and is plausibly the most anxious bloc of the four for exactly that reason; a lineage house can absorb a bad season of gossip on reputation alone, a new house often cannot yet. A new-money house that never bought into the Circulean project at all, meanwhile, keeps the older accounting regardless of its coin, and sits, in temperament, closer to Svenu than to its own Hall colleagues.

This tension is felt most concretely wherever a clan's continuity is actually decided rather than merely discussed: in the weighing of prospective tanists, where a progressive house may quietly favor the more legible candidate over the more capable one; in marriage negotiations, where a bloodline's whole future reputation is what is actually being traded; and in how one house reads another's fitness as an ally, where a single unaccountable kinsman elsewhere can color a progressive house's judgment of an entire clan. It also complicates the gender-status norms already native to Varngard: the status-not-law preference for male headship and male Hall representation described above is itself best understood as a marker of the progressive pole specifically, cultivated as part of the same Circulean-facing prestige project. A traditionalist house, old blood or new coin alike, is considerably more relaxed about a capable daughter's claim, reading it the way Svenu would -- on the strength of her deeds, and nothing else.

## Bonding

Varngard practices Sammesidar -- Bonding, called samasiðr in the Svenic of the mother-tradition -- as an inheritance from Svenu, where the tradition properly belongs and is practiced in full; the mechanism itself, and the terms moling and adaling, are addressed there rather than repeated here. Varngard's version differs from that mother-tradition in three respects, all social rather than mechanical.

Where Svenu binds its molings and adalings along explicit elemental lines and extends the practice further still to skipling and aettling -- courtiers bonded to a ship or to a whole clan or crew rather than to any one person -- Varngard kept only the narrower, person-to-person form, with none of Svenu's ship- or clan-totem traditions surviving south of the Gulf.

A childhood adaling is also read differently. Where Svenu marks such a child out before the wider community, Varngard's version stays within the clan: the child is weighed specifically in that clan's own internal reckoning of who stands likeliest to succeed as tanist, a private matter of succession rather than a public omen.

Most sharply, Varngard reads a lapsed adaling far more severely than Svenu's sympathy-first mother-tradition does. The withdrawal of a courtier's favor is taken in Varngard as dishonor in its own right, immediate and unconditional, needing no further conduct to confirm it -- a standing question over the bonder's honor that no house or faction can argue away, precisely because none had any part in the courtier's original judgment or its reversal. It also cuts against Varngard's status-not-law gender custom in its own quiet way, since a courtier answers only to the deed itself, indifferent to which sons a house might otherwise prefer put forward. Varngardi tale-telling nonetheless favors the gentler of the two shapes such a lapse can take -- a bond that remains in name but withholds its favor, leaving room for the old courtesy to be won back -- over the rarer, cleaner severing a whole Hall would speak of for a generation.

## The Hall of Oar and Axe

Varngard's governing body took shape as its boat-clan confederation consolidated around control of the Varn, the Swicca, and above all the Myrholm portage, and it is at Myrholm that the Hall now sits. Its character is that of a closed council of notable houses rather than a broad franchise -- deliberately so, in contrast to Svenu's All-Thing, and a mark of the same Circulean-facing prestige that shapes Varngard's gender-status norms. Membership is drawn from the houses of recognized standing, and every house seated there can be placed, at least roughly, along the same two axes that divide Varngardi society generally: old river- and land-holding lineages against trade-fleet and merchant houses of newer wealth on one axis, and progressive, Circulean-facing houses against traditionalist, Svenu-descended ones on the other. The two do not track each other, and it is this, as much as the lineage-versus-coin tension itself, that gives the Hall's politics their particular, often counterintuitive shape.

The Hall's true currency, however, is honor rather than wealth or procedure, closer in spirit to a council of stature than to the Commonwealth's Parliament: new coin buys the means to earn standing -- a funded warband, a sponsored feast, an endowment to the Southern Reach, a ransomed captive -- but does not buy a seat outright, and an old house of modest purse can still outrank a wealthier rival in the Hall's actual regard.

Because two independent axes are in play, a proposal's fate in the Hall often turns less on which houses are wealthiest than on which axis the matter at hand happens to activate, and the resulting coalitions can look unnatural to an outsider even though they are entirely predictable to anyone who knows which question is actually on the floor. A matter of toll policy, portage rights, or funding for a warband reliably splits the Hall along old lineage against new money, exactly as familiar factional politics would suggest -- progressive and traditionalist houses alike falling in on whichever side their purse and their holdings put them. But a matter touching honor, legibility, or a clan's fitness to be trusted -- a contested tanistry, a disputed marriage, the standing of a kinsman whose conduct resists easy accounting -- splits the Hall instead along progressive against traditionalist, and this vote cuts clean through the lineage-and-coin bloc that held together on the previous question. An old, traditionalist lineage house may find its truest ally on such a matter not among its fellow old houses but in an upstart trade-fleet house that never bought into Circulean respectability either, the two making common cause against a progressive wing that includes both some of the oldest blood in the Hall and some of its newest coin. No single alliance in Varngardi politics is therefore permanent; a house that stands together with its neighbor on tolls may find itself opposed to that same neighbor within the same session once the question turns to honor, and a shrewd voice in the Hall learns to read which axis a proposal will be judged on before ever counting where the votes will fall.

### The Arameistar, Master of the Oar

The Hall's civil head -- properly the Arameistar, rendered in the Circulean tongues as the Master of the Oar -- is elected by the assembled houses for life, holding the office until death or recall rather than for any fixed term. The full, archaic form of the title is kept for formal address; everyday Varngardi speech clips it to Aremeistar, the regular outcome the language's own sound-changes would produce. The Arameistar treats with foreign powers, presides over trade law and the disputes of river-mouths and portage rights, and is the figure any settled power -- the Commonwealth, Karsova, Lavion -- would expect to negotiate with, not always correctly.

Recall runs by one of two distinct processes. Ordinary dissent -- an Arameistar judged incompetent, outmaneuvered, or simply fallen from the Hall's confidence -- is put to the Hall as a motion and settled by majority vote; an ousted Arameistar on this track keeps his personal stature and his clan headship untouched, since nothing has been alleged against his honor, only his fitness for office. A graver charge -- oathbreaking, cowardice, corrupt betrayal of the houses' trust -- is instead brought as a declaration before the Hall, which the accused must answer rather than merely survive a vote on. He may yield the charge, ending his tenure at once and costing him stature in proportion to what he concedes; the Hall may hear his denial and judge the charge false, in which case the accuser's own standing suffers instead; or, where the matter is genuinely contested and the Hall cannot or will not render judgment, it is settled in the Ring, by the accuser and accused or by champions standing for them, with no appeal from the verdict rendered there.

An honor-track ouster is a finding about the man, not merely the office, and an Arameistar's own clan is expected to act on it in turn, invoking tanistry to pass headship to the line's next most capable adult as it would for any tanist found unfit. A clan that instead shelters its disgraced head and refuses to invoke tanistry is understood to be contesting the Hall's judgment after the fact, and it is exactly this kind of standoff -- not succession disputes within a clan, which tanistry handles, and not policy disputes, which the dissent track handles -- that has produced Varngard's gravest internal conflicts. Defying a Ring verdict specifically is graver still: it is not merely defiance of the Hall but an honor-act in its own right, calling the defiant party's own honor into question whether or not the original charge was just.

### The Yxafyring, Commander of the Axe

Where the Arameistar is the Hall's own creature, the Yxafyring -- the Commander of the Axe -- is raised by acclamation -- shout and shield-beating among warriors of standing, sufficient in number that the act cannot be dismissed as the ambition of a desperate few -- and the Hall's role is to ratify or contest a claim already made rather than to grant it. The title is itself transparent in Varngardi: yxa, axe, and fyring, leader or commander, descend together from the Svenic exifyrstingr, "axe-commander." As with the Arameistar, everyday speech clips the archaic form to Yxefyring, the two registers -- Yxafyring for formal address, Yxefyring for ordinary talk -- existing side by side without confusion. Crucially, this acclamation need not occur in the Hall at all: a war-band or levy in the field can raise a Yxafyring by deeds done, presenting the Hall with a fact rather than a candidate. The office is thus reachable by a path the Hall's own notable houses do not control, which the Hall tolerates as the price of keeping Varngard's warrior heritage alive within, rather than outside, its institutions.

The parallel is not lost on Varngard's neighbors: Genovrast de Lavion, founder of the kingdom that bears his name, was himself nothing more than such a war-leader before he became a king, and no Lavienese court can dismiss the Yxafyring as mere barbarian irregularity without undercutting its own dynasty's founding myth.

### Arameistar and Yxafyring in Union

Nothing bars one figure from holding both offices at once, a war-leader's acclaimed standing in the field proving compatible with the Hall's own election to the Oar rather than superseding it. Such a union is rare -- most Arameistars are men of the council chamber and most Yxafyrings men of the warband, and the two paths seldom converge in a single career -- but where it has occurred, the figure in question is remembered simply as Master and Commander, a style reserved in popular memory for Varngard's greatest historical figures rather than granted as a matter of course. It is exactly this kind of union that Varngardi memory is likeliest to reach for in describing whoever emerges to lead resistance against Roburic II's Imperial campaign, should the crisis produce a leader who commands both the Hall's confidence and the warbands' acclaim at once.

## Historical Sketch

The earliest Svenish settlements south of the Lupines were boat-clan footholds of exactly the raiding-and-trading character attested of Svenu's ancestors generally, with little yet to distinguish Varngard culturally from its cousins across the Gulf. As the Varn, the navigable reaches of the Swicca, and above all the Myrholm portage matured into real trade arteries, a smaller set of clans controlling these chokepoints pulled ahead economically, and the Southern Reach's spreading influence gave these consolidating houses a vocabulary of settled, propertied legitimacy to draw on against their still-raiding kin. The confederation's council of clan-heads hardened in time into the Hall of Oar and Axe proper, membership tied to houses of recognized standing rather than to any broad franchise -- a republic in fact, but never mistaking itself for Svenu's All-Thing.

## Names

Varngardi names run in an old-fashioned Svenic cant markedly distinct from the melodious Virellic or vibrant Balboan names of the north: men called Hrodmar, Varnabog, Jernaldr, Siguord, Hjallaric; women called Brynild, Alet, Gudrana, Skara, Hristja.

## Open Threads

The Varngard-Karsova relationship along the lower Swicca and Deserted Sea litoral remains to be developed in full, properly belonging to a future Karsova lorebook alongside the Wolves' Teeth Patriarchate's eastern march. Roburic II's Imperial-age campaign against Varngard and Karsova -- already attested in the general chronology as his first protectorate-making venture along "the Eastern Gyre littoral and the Southlands along the Swicca" -- is understood to be a costly, multi-year affair given Varngard's terrain, logistics, and honor-driven warband culture, likely to produce a field-acclaimed Yxafyring -- perhaps an Arameistar and Yxafyring in union, given the stakes -- as the resistance's rallying point; the campaign's detailed course is not yet drafted. The marsh-and-lake country around Myrholm has not yet been given a name distinct from Myrholm itself. The naming of Hall ceremonies -- the declaration-and-response act, the Ring's formal convening -- has been deliberately deferred.
