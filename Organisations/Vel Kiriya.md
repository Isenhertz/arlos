Vel Kiriya lies north of the [[Circle Sea]], where the high Kiriyan plateau leans out toward the jungle and the first navigable reaches of the [[Riv-î-Tep|Mer]]. The Mer itself is the realm's defining fact. It begins as a many-headed torrent in the cloud forests, gathers its strength in a labyrinth of channels and flooded groves, then cuts a broad trench along the foot of the plateau before spreading out into the Merhal floodplains far to the south. It is too wide to bridge in its middle course, too deep to ford, too unruly to tame with levees for more than a few seasons at a time. To live along the Mer is to negotiate with a thing that cannot be overruled.

Between plateau and river sprawls a belt of dense, humid jungle. There are tracks and causeways, to be sure, but every road is a campaign fought against rot, creepers, and slides. Stone pavements vanish in a season if left unattended. For that reason, inland travel tends to drop from the terraces to the river in a few hard stair-roads, rather than creak along its banks. The Mer is the highway, not a boundary. Cities in the Kiriyan heartland cling to islands and artificial bars, their maps drawn from the docks inward rather than from a landward gate outwards. Quays, ferry steps, and boat-pools are their true streets.

Above all this, the Kiriyan plateau rises as a vast, stepped upland of terraced fields, pastures, and stone roads. Its air is cooler, its nights sharper, its horizon broken by ridges and volcanic cones. Here, roads can actually endure. The Kiriyan causeways run along the spine of the heights, linked by stair-roads that dive toward the Mer or climb toward sacred springs and fortified promontories. Where Lavion and its neighbours boast of courier horses galloping along coastal roads, Vel Kiriya's pride lies in its runners on the high roads, its processions of pack beasts, and the stone milestones that have watched generations pass.

From an Arlosian map-maker's perspective, Vel Kiriya is less a patch of colour than a long, irregular band: the reaches of the Mer that can float serious traffic, their jungle margins, the lifted terrace-lands that feed them, and the ports that face the Drowned Coast and Eastern Gyre. It is no accident that many of the successor states of Riv-î-Tep, down in the Merhal, count themselves tributary to the King of Kings. The Mer defines them too, and Vel Kiriya knows the river better than any of them.

## History and the Velkirian idea of restorations

### Antiquity

The Velkirians themselves speak of their realm as the oldest continuously existing sovereign in the Circulean world. They will trace an unbroken line from the "First Covenant of Measure" to the present King of Kings, and if awkward questions about dead dynasties, usurpations, and long interregna are raised, they are met with a mild smile and the phrase: "There was a Restoration."

Behind this serene fiction, the history is older and more fractured. Long before Kharun walked the Salt Road, the Kiriyan plateau held a patchwork of highland kingdoms and clan confederations, each anchored in a cluster of terraces and guarded passes. Their wealth came from high crops, metals, and the ability to send caravan trains down to the river and back again without losing half of them to fever. Along the Mer itself, early cities grew on sandbars and islands, paying tribute to whichever plateau lord could keep their approaches clear and their rival ports nervous.

[[Riv-î-Tep]] at this time was rising toward its own terrifying splendour downstream, its temple-cities pouring labour, blood, and stone into designs that later ages scarcely comprehend. Vel Kiriya's ancestors traded up and downriver with these sorcerous powers and quietly armed themselves against them. When [[Sorceror-King|Emôn-Dju-Bâ-Mhâhat-Nmu]] fell and the flying throne of Riv-î-Tep lost its moorings, the highland kings took note. They learned that even the First-and-Only could be toppled, and they resolved that their own power would rest on less esoteric foundations.

The first Velkirian "realm" properly so called was a league of plateau kings and river cities that bound themselves to a single high king in Paxhar Vel, a rocky shoulder where plateau and river almost touch. He took the old highland title that is translated as "King of Kings", and his peers accepted the word "satrap" for themselves, though they never stopped thinking of their offices in terms of terraces and roads. Satrapies at this stage were loose and quarrelsome. Their loyalties were to the person of the high king, not yet to any enduring institution.

Then Kharun's disciples came up the Mer.

### The Kharunite Restoration

The [[Eastern Rite|Kharunite]] preaching of the Light in All, the sanctity of covenant, and the dignity of measure found unusually fertile soil in Kiriyan minds already obsessed with weights, quotas, and the fairness of tribute. What began as a new cult in a few river shrines became, over the course of two centuries, the moral language of the realm. Judges of the new Rite travelled the caravan roads with slates and scrolls, recording local customs, arbitrating disputes, and gradually recasting them in terms of one overarching Covenant. The King of Kings discovered that a neutral class of roving cleric-jurists, answerable to an ideal of Measure rather than to any satrap, was a useful tool.

It is in this period that Velkirians place their first "Restoration" -- a great legal and moral refounding in which old charters were recopied in Kharunite terms and the kingship of Paxhar Vel was wrapped in a mantle of quasi-sacred guardianship of the Covenant. In truth, it was also a time of war and suppression, as plateaus that preferred their old cults and temple-lords that resisted external judges were brought to heel. Later chronicles speak of "bringing them back into Measure", with a studied refusal to admit that there had ever been an "out" from which to return.

Subsequent centuries saw Vel Kiriya and [[Remean Empire|Ancient Reme]] face each other at long range, separated by the width of the Eastern Gyre and Riv-î-Tep's shadow. When Reme overreached and tried to master the circle of the seas, Vel Kiriya stayed largely inland, content to rule the Mer and its tributaries. When [[Noble Element#^798992|Phlogistophor]] buried Reme in his purifying fire, the King of Kings' scribes added a neat note in the margins: "The Western Empire is concluded." The eastern books did not need a new column.

Yet internal upheaval came, again and again. Dynasties failed; satraps grew too mighty; the Merhal cities flirted with independence; highland clans refused to rebuild roads or terraces after landslides. Each time, a successful warlord or reforming regent marched on Paxhar Vel, took the palace, pledged renewed fealty to the Covenant of Measure, and promulgated fresh laws "restoring the realm to its ancient form". That this ancient form had, curiously, never existed in just this way before was not something to dwell upon. Genealogies were adjusted, regnal counts quietly restarted from the Restoration, and the story resumed.

The present Velkirian system is the child of several such restorations. A scholar of Lavion, chasing actual continuity, would call them new regimes. A Velkirian jurist will explain, patient and unyielding, that the Measure itself has never broken. Kings and satraps may fail, but when they do, someone must return the scales to balance. That act of return is what matters. The realm survives because the covenant survives.

## Internal character and governance

From within, Vel Kiriya is best understood as a network of satrapies wrapped around the river, with the plateau roads and jungle tracks feeding that central artery. A satrapy is defined not by a neat border on a map, but by three intertwined domains. There is its *Reach*: the stretch of river it is responsible for keeping navigable, watched, and profitable. There is its *Catchment*: the upland terraces, jungle villages, and mine-towns whose produce, labour, and coin must flow toward that reach. Lastly there are its *Fronts*: its obligations toward the sea, toward hostile neighbours, or toward rebellious highlanders.

The satrap is expected to keep the river safe along his reach: sandbars marked, channels dredged where possible, tower beacons tended for foggy nights, war-boats maintained and crewed. He must keep the caravan roads down from the plateau paved and passable, and ensure that jungle tracks to his river ports are patrolled against bandits and worse. Above all, he must meet his quotas to Paxhar Vel. Grain in kind, timber and ores, conscript labour for great works, coin in the sexagesimal tallies that the central treasury insists upon: these are the measures by which a satrap's success is judged.

The King of Kings, perched in Paxhar Vel and surrounded by his household guard and river fleet, does not try to micro-manage each terrace and grove. His power rests on three institutions. The first is the riverine navy: squadrons of heavy barges and swift cutters that can move downstream faster than any rebel can run, delivering fire and spear to disobedient ports without a single jungle road being cut. The second is the corps of royal jurists and inspectors, dispatched from the capital to audit accounts, hear complaints against satraps, and remind all parties that the Covenant's eyes reach further than any mortal's. The third is the bureaucracy of scribes who actually make sense of the realm's numbers.

In Velkirian officialdom, measure is both a practical and a moral category. Grain is recorded in sexagesimal fractions of a standard field's yield; distances along the high roads are counted in a neat ladder of base-60 units; time itself is structured in Belana's Passes and their subdivisions. A satrap who "plays with the base" in his accounts is not merely a cheat, but a blasphemer, attempting to distort a pattern that is believed to echo the Light's own harmony. It is for this reason as much as simple habit that base-60 mathematics endures so stubbornly in the Kiriyan sphere, even as [[Kingdom of Lavion|Lavion]] and the [[Commonwealth of Principalities|Commonwealth]] boast of their [[Arlosian Dozenal|dozenal]] refinements.

Socially, Vel Kiriya is layered but permeable. Highland nobles claim descent from pre-Kharunite lineages and measure their dignity in terraces and herds. Riverine merchant houses, many of them with Rivian or coastal blood, command fleets and wharves and sponsor judges and scribes. In the Merhal cities, ancient temple-families, now long stripped of their more extravagant sorcerous trappings, sit in councils that must bow to the King of Kings' decrees while remembering that their walls were old when Paxhar Vel was a hill fort. What holds them all within the same system is not sentiment, but the combination of river, fleet, and law. So long as the Mer flows and the King's war-barges ply it, the satrapies have little choice but to play their part.

## Religious stance and perception from the Circulean

The state religion of Vel Kiriya is the Eastern, or Kiriyan, Rite of Lurism founded upon the teachings of Judge Saint Kharun of the Salt Road. In the Kiriyan tongue it is not "Eastern" at all, simply "the Church". Its key distinctives are panentheism, legalism, and a serene willingness to weave older animist and elemental practices into its frame.

To a Kharunite, the Light is present in all things, not abstracted far above them. The Noble Six are not distant symbols but living presences whose elemental courts interpenetrate the world. Shrines to [[Noble Element|Maregina]] of the Waters, [[Noble Element|Kuapakalipa]] of Soil, and the lesser river and hill spirits are not seen as rival altars but as local expressions of the one Light's bounty. What matters is that dealings with them be honest and bounded by covenant. The typical Kiriyan village shrine will therefore contain Scripture passages carved in stone, a niche for one or more saints who exemplified good Measure in life, and a basin or channel where libations and offerings to elementals are poured, all under the care of a warden who has at least some training from the Kharunite judges.

This religious habit informs the state's famous tolerance. The King of Kings' law grants formal protection to all worship that accepts the basic tenets of the Covenant -- no human sacrifice, no forced conversion, no denial of the courts' authority, no direct incitement to break tribute or law. Within those bounds, local cults may flourish. In the jungle margins, ancestral altars and tree-spirits receive offerings; in the Merhal, old Rivian gods have been demoted to a kind of honoured, if slightly suspect, citizenry of the unseen; in the ports, foreign merchants maintain orthodox Lurist chapels or even Concordian guild shrines. The official view is that the Light will sort out the truth of these devotions; the state's business is to ensure that they do not disrupt Measure.

From the Circulean west, this looks very different depending on who is watching. The Third [[Church of the Light|Hierophancy]] and its allies in Lavion tend to speak of the Kiriyan Rite as heretical yet uncomfortably familiar: they share the same core creed, "One is Three is Six is All", but the Eastern commentaries on that mystery incline toward legal argument and cosmic interpenetration rather than the hieratic clarity preferred in Virell. The Hierophant's theologians write learned condemnations of Kharunite panentheism and its relaxed stance toward elemental spirits, and then sigh with relief that the King of Kings is too far away to enforce his opinions on their dioceses.

Lavienese secular observers find other points of contention. To a Lavienese noble or merchant used to charters, estates, and negotiated feudal compacts, Velkirian governance looks pedantic and suffocating, full of auditors who care more about whether the numbers reconcile than about whether a house has held a title "since the days of [[de Lavion|Genovrast]]". The sexagesimal books of the Kiriyan treasuries are a constant source of mockery in western caricatures, filled with mystic circles and base-60 fractions that no proper modern mind should be bothered to decipher. At the same time, Lavion's more honest ministers quietly admire a realm that has kept the Mer open to trade, held together a patchwork of very different peoples, and survived the fall of empires by treating history itself as a series of restorations rather than breaks.

Among the merchant houses of the Circle Sea, Vel Kiriya's reputation is more practical. The King of Kings' customs posts are tedious, but their rates are generally predictable. The river fleet is officious, but it suppresses piracy efficiently. The jurists are stern, but they will hear a stranger's complaint against a satrap's clerk, provided the complaint is well documented.

In any case, Vel Kiriya does not particularly care how the West labels it. From Paxhar Vel's terraces, the Mer is visible as a broad band of moving light, and all the satrapal banners of the realm are seen as reflections in that current. So long as the river flows, the courts sit, and the next Restoration, when it comes, can be written as a return rather than a fall, the Velkirians consider themselves to have kept their oldest promise.
