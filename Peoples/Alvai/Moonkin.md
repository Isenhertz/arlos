"Moonkin" is a cognomen of the Alvai associated with **Lumal-Nâri the Wolf Mother**, said by them to be the patron of white Aural -- Lur's Mirror, the moon that sits resplendent in the night next to patient green Belana, the Old Ambler, and the ember pinprick of the Reckoner, Langea. Like all Alvai, the Kin of Lumal-Nâri are marked by their furred abdomen and legs, though a Moonkin's tells include tufted ears, and -- in a stark difference from other Circulean Kin -- fully planted, though clawed, feet. This has enabled them to use sandals and soft shoes, which in turn contributed to their assimilation into the many societies of mankind after the Scattering, the diaspora following the destruction of their ancestral homeland in the Wolves' Teeth Mountains through a series of Lurist crusades during the Age of Enlightenment.

## Prehistory and Antiquity

Before the Scattering, the Moonkin of the Wolves' Teeth lived as highland confederates -- long-lived and jealously protective of their valleys. Clans gathered under war captains and priest-mothers, each holding a cirque or a string of hanging valleys in the Lupine ranges. Oath and feud defined their politics; guest-right, wergild, and ring gifts held them together. Their planted gait and furred legs made them sure on scree and ice. Summer saw trade with human tribes at the lower passes; winter brought the courts, the hunt, and the long recitation of lineages by firelight. Arms were fashioned of bronze and seasoned wood: long knives, hook spears, round shields faced in rawhide, and javelins flung from the hand or with thong-loops. War was taken in the old manner of wolves -- prowl, feint, and break -- and the howl-horn and the drum called kin across the ridges. Thus lived the Kin of the Moon for ages untold in the prehistory of the world.

When the western outposts of ancient Reme pushed south toward the high country after their subjugation of the Merhal and their expansion into the lands adjoining the Western Gyre, they found not a virgin frontier to be surveyed, but opposition that struck by night. Early charges against Shaped legion lines were ruinous for the Moonkin, who soon learned to decline the open field. Under warqueen Baddhiga, raised from the warrior caste by council and deed, the clans turned to a fighting grammar of ambuscade and attrition, cutting roads, harrying foragers, burning wagons and bridges, and striking camps at moonrise only to vanish before dawn. Baddhiga's Ring-Pacts bound rival clans and valleys; her rule and leadership enabled the Moonkin to push back against an enemy that had bested all human opposition, such that after decades of bruising marches and skirmish loss, the Remeans abandoned their plans for the highland passes and, remembering their thalassocratic ideals, fortified the coastline further north, paying for passage rather than bleeding for it.

The Moonkin reputation was stamped in those campaigns and endured thereafter: fierce to folly when pride was pricked, but patient as snow when a season's patience would break a foe for them. Their elders lived long enough to teach three generations the same hills; their captains counted winters rather than summers and held fast to the rule that a living clan is victory enough. This wild strength, honed against empire, carried their name into the lowlands and kept their homeland free until the crusades of a later age unmade it.

## After the Scattering

The Scattering itself belongs to the long shadow of the Theocracy's crusades into the Wolves' Teeth, when Lurist hosts drove the highland bands from hearth and necropole. In later liturgy these campaigns were framed as purifications; in Moonkin memory they remain a winter without end.

The Moonkin's planted gait, with the sole laid fully along the ground rather than on lifted heel or splayed claw, was key to their assimilation. Sandals and soft shoes adapted for wide clawed feet made city streets theirs, and cobbled courts and timber floors took less offense at their passage than at the gouge of other Kin. In the early centuries after the Scattering, this small mercy of anatomy met the great need of exile: to blend without begging pardon for every footprint. 

Urban life altered Moonkin ways more than conquest ever could. The clans were broken on the frontier, but bands of kinship re-cohered in towns as houses of work and fellowship, taking shape around a master craft or a scholar's stipend rather than a chieftain's fire. Longevity leaned the Moonkin toward the slow excellences -- fine joinery, gold- and silverwork, conservation of manuscripts, the patient arts of dye and inlay -- where decades accrue great skill as surely as they do modest wealth. In riverine capitals and port cities, nascent guilds offered a new choreography to replace the old; oaths to ward, master, and work-bench softened the memory of oaths to hill and hearth and rewarded steadiness and quiet skill, the very currency the Alvai carry in abundance when the world allows it. 

Their household grammar follows the needs of diaspora. Children are often fostered out across allied houses to spread risk; apprenticeships run long and are paid back with a share of future fees rather than coin alone; widows and the orphaned are seldom left to stand alone, but folded in as a matter of course. The old highland habit of night watches survives as a civic courtesy in their communities -- a quiet circuit of doors that wins them small credit with wardens.

Relations with Lurist polities have been uneven, and memory makes them more so. In the strict heartlands, a Moonkin body still reads too easily as an old affront, especially where sermons recall the first centuries in a key of zeal. Yet the modern temper of the Third Hierophancy prefers ordered towns to triumphant persecutions, and where charter, guild, and parish pull in one direction, coexistence becomes the dull habit of a decent city. In the Concordian West this civic mood was long ago made doctrine, and there a Moonkin's good standing is seldom subject to polemic. Even so, suspicion resurges with every scandal, and it will be the visible Alvai who are made to answer first for the faults of their wilder kin.

Names, too, bend to setting. The old valley styles -- terse, alliterative, heavy with lineage -- yields in towns to the house-first fashion of human clerks and guildhalls. Many Moonkin keep an old Ngerhemäir name for family rite and a plain name for the world. The ear tufts that betray them to any practiced eye are groomed as carefully as their beards. Tailoring de-emphasizes the furred abdomen without pretending it does not exist. In polite wards, scent is handled like speech -- modest, deliberate, designed to pass unremarked. This desire to avoid giving offense means that across the centuries, only a few names rise from the footnotes -- Moonkin secretaries who outlived patrons and preserved a polity, antiquarians who re-stitched a torn archive, treasurers who taught a court the virtue of exactness. For every such figure there are hundreds for whom the record is merely a rent roll, a clerical record of birth, a guild mark on a lintel. This, more than any single law, defines the Moonkin condition after the Scattering: to live within other peoples' institutions without surrendering the few old habits that they carried with them into the world.

## The Svartalfr

Some Moonkin, however, never left the old cold. The Svartalfr, the black-haired kin of Lumal-Nâri living in the far southern Lupines of Svenu, kept to the harsh marches and boulder-fields below the ice, holding on to ancestral ways even as the world to windward forgot them. Trade brings them down with bone and horn inlays, plaits, smoked meat, and the rare and skillfully wrought axe or blade, for they learned the coveted secrets of steel before the lowlanders did; but ordinarily they can only be found well beyond the last plowed field or pasture. To a city-born Moonkin they are kin and strangers both, as opaque and enviable in their self-possession as any cloistered order. Though they share a common language and ancient rites with their kin, many Svartalfr no longer identify as part of the broader Moonkin diaspora, seeing themselves instead as keepers of a purer memory untouched by the compromises of exile. Of their traditions little is known outside their snowy keeps, but travellers from Svenu, where they are highly respected, speak of the custom of the "axe-brides", female warriors foreswearing family and clan to focus their long lives on the mastery of the whirling war-hatchets or cleaving pole-axes.
