The Alvai that call themselves the Kin of the Stars are a people of Brother Bat, whom they name Ghâl-Vekhu. Since time immemorial they dwell in An-Suur, where heat, shade, and water trade places by the hour -- jungles that steam at noon, mangrove coasts whose tides help fish climb trunks like monkeys. Long before the Prophet's moment of clarity bound the quarrelsome Anish cities into a single theocracy, the Starkin shared this land with them and kept to the deep green the Anish shunned. They are the shortest and lithest of the Kin: eighty-five fingers can be called average height; a Sunkin beside them might stand a head and a half taller. Their feet are raptorial with the weight carried on fore-toes and a raised heelspike for grip on bark and root; their ears are large and mobile; and their eyes are black-glossed dishes that drink the night. Where a Moonkin's planted sole was meant for scree and sleet and now takes to cobble and stair, Starkin talons are for bough and buttress-root, and their poise is the quiet of hunters by habit and intent.

## Homeland and early history

In the old rivers and along the long coasts, Starkin bands moved with the seasons and the fruiting of trees, fishing creeks in the rains and trading bush-meat and resin at the dry-season fringes. Their record-keeping was, and remains, the charge of memory-keepers who recite their epics whole -- a discipline of silence by day and song by night. The coming of early Lurist zeal -- first the consolidation under the Prophet and the Judges, then the Theocracy's expansion -- pressed them further under the canopy. Human hands felled groves and raised shrines; patrols followed, and the Kin followed the same grammar that old enemies had taught the Moonkin deep in the south: prowl, feint, and break, and vanish into green shadow to strike anew from another angle. 

## The Green War and the burn of An-Mirda

What the Theocracy called pacification, the Starkin remember as the Green War, a long uncounted season of raids, razed clearings, and disappeared patrols. From that fight rose Ejitwe the Thronebreaker, prince and partisan, who gathered exiles and oath-bands and led them not to conquest but to unmaking: the sack of An-Mirda in 336 AR, forty days of siege followed by flame and flight. For the faith, especially the popular imagination, he is the face of vile heathenry victorious; for his people he is a liberator and a caution both -- the last Batkin to take righteous vengeance openly and prevail, and proof that even a just victory can still leave a people without a heartland. When warrior bands name a fierce youth "child of Ejitwe" because they hear that old fire in the voice, the elders caution that the same flame that cleanses old blood feuds will also feed on new shoots.

## Customs of silence and song

Starkin speech is sparse when the sun is up. Ears that swivel make for a quick and vivid sign-language of posture, finger-taps, and ear-tilts; a camp can pass a day of craft in a hush broken only by the rasp of twine on wood. But at night the same band will find song in a call-and-response that binds memory to meter so a single keeper's recitation can braid the names of travels, kin bands, feuds, and treaties without a chisel to hand. Their houses are where the food is, lean-tos and canopy-tents, easily hidden and as easily abandoned. A band's anchor is not timber but the circuit of favored shallows and fruit stands, a web of kin oaths and guest-rights with neighboring bands. Starkin life is more caravan than village, and yet thick with belonging.

## The Dreamsong

Most misunderstood of their rites is the Dreamsong, a feast for Brother Bat. Offerings of blood stand at its center -- beast most often, and by consent on rare occasions the blood of kin -- infused with the fruits and shrooms that wake the mind to color and pattern. The band drinks  until the pulse of drum and voice lifts the camp into a single listening. What follows is a communal trance in which visions rise and repeat across many minds at once, a memory shared rather than proclaimed. Outsiders, hearing only of blood and sleep, made of it dark cannibalism; the Starkin hold it as a pledge of care, a way to carry the elders' seeing into the bodies of the young so they might remember as one.

## War by necessity, night by right

Guerrilla craft is not romance but bread. Starkin avoid the open and the noon. They strike at dusk, cut rope and bridge in rain, set lures where a patrol must cross, and teach the young that a fight you are not seen to begin is a fight you can afford to abandon. The Theocracy is gone, but the Third Hierophancy, for all its modern restraint, still keeps its old monastery enclaves across An-Suur, and those, in turn, keep old and zealous habits. The Starkin have not forgotten. They refuse proselytization, and while bands that came out to the fringes mostly keep their knives sheathed and sell their knowledge as wardens and guides, others never came out at all. Smugglers and relic-hunters speak in low voices of the Deep Clans -- the bands that stayed in the jungle after the Theocratic collapse and still hold the old vendettas, reading the outer world as infection to be cauterized, not bargained with.

## Present settlements at the fringe

In recent decades, a younger generation has tested the tree line. Some Starkin live now at the jungle's edge in market hamlets and river-towns; others hire as pilots and wardens for scholars who can be taught to listen to the green. It is a cautious commerce. Guides lay down terms that read like oaths -- no preaching, no cutting sacred trees, no prying at kin graves, no maps sold to third hands -- and will vanish into the green with a client's silver untouched if so much as a single term is broken. Bands that visit the fringes in their year-round travels trade resin, dyes, nuts, and bush-meat and buy iron, salt, bronze, and oils. But even those that trade readily with any that would call them heathens are not pacified -- only at rest. A wrong word, a step too far, and the forest will close around the offender as surely as water closes around a stone.

## Faith and refusal

Modern Lurism speaks with a cooler tongue than the old crusader cant, and in its best temper it prizes harmonious civic order. Even so, the Starkin have centuries of cause to mistrust the Light's emissaries, and the living memories to know better than to be reconciled by sermons. That they continue to succeed with this refusal is not for chance but for structure: Starkin bands are small, kin-stitched, and perpetually in motion; their law lives in memory-keepers and in oaths that travel with them. Food, water, and medicine are inside the canopy, and the canopy itself is a fortress and a shelter. A missionary cannot simply enter a part of the jungle as one enters a village; he must be received to be heard. The bands simply choose not to receive any. In such a society, proselytization has no lever: promises of courts, poorhouses, and written surety mean little where justice is settled by elders who can recite three generations of debt and guest-right from memory, and where hunger is solved by feet and ear and net rather than by alms.

## Bearing and look

A Starkin's presence is a study in economy. The stance is forward on the toes; the heelspike barely kisses the earth until it is needed. The ears, ever moving, work much like hands do in the speech of a Balboan or a Fiuriscian, such that whole sentences can be changed by the ears tilting just so; in conversation with outsiders they go still so as not to give offense, if they aren't used to hold a second conversation with nearby Kin the outsider is not privy to. Fur is dark, sparse on the upper body and thicker along the abdomen and thighs; arm tufts are slight, and the hands are deft rather than heavy. Night-eyes show a dome of black that catches every glint of lamp-light; in bright noon many wear shade veils or brimmed hats that let them look without squinting. The whole posture reads as quiet intent, and the wise learn to treat that quiet not as humility but as readiness.

## Names and renown

The world outside calls them Starkin or Batkin, and they accept both without much fuss. Within the bands, names are earned; a child might be called River-Turn one season and Mangrove-Monkey the next, until a true name is set by his deeds and the memory-keeper weaves it into the chant at the coming-of-age feast. To outsiders they often give short commerce names in the fashion of the ports. The greatest name among them remains Ejitwe. Yet even there the elders insist on the old balance: invoke his fire when you must, but remember that the outside world sees not the liberator, only the conflagration.
