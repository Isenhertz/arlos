The Lion-Blood Alvai of Lu-An are the Kin of the Sun Lion Rasha-Tarô, a proud and perilous people, and among the most storied warrior bands of Arlos. Native to the sun-blasted and gold-grassed savannahs of the [[Lu-An|Lonely Plains]], they are one of the few Alvai peoples never brought under heel by the Remeans, the Theocracy of [[Eternal Dominion]], or any later polities -- neither by open conquest nor quiet efforts of conversion. Unlike the scattered and assimilated [[Moonkin]] or the still jungle-woven [[Starkin]] of [[An-Suur]], the Sunkin present themselves without apology. Their kinship with the Spirit of the Sun Lion, a beast of fire, pride, and glory, is read in leonine traits and in bearing alike: manes of ruddy gold or copper on men and women both, fierce amber eyes that do not dim with age, digitigrade legs and claws on hands and feet that come out at a moment's notice. The mark of their lineage is as much demeanor as anatomy: regal, radiant, communal, and intense enough to feel like standing too long in direct sun; awe-inspiring, exhausting, impossible to ignore.

In their belief the Sun Lion is First Among Many of the [[Animal Spirit|Animal Spirits]], and his children are avatars not as solitary champions but as prides. The pride is thus the atom of their society: a semi-sacred bond-group equal parts family, household, and battlefield formation. Every Lion-Blood is born to a natal pride and is expected, at maturity, to enter or form an adult pride through oath, ordeal, and affinity. Such prides commonly number five to nine adults with dependents; odd counts are considered quick and decisive, even counts balanced but deliberative. They share food, territory, offspring, craft, and risk; they deliberate collectively; they act as economic cooperatives; and honor, shame, and legal standing attach to the pride before they attach to any single member. The result is that a single Sunkin warrior may be formidable, but a pride -- three, five, seven moving with predatorial anticipation -- is an opponent few troops can match without numerical advantage.

Because the pride bond is central, Sunkin intimacy is polygamous in rite and unabashedly polyamorous in practice. The forms vary: some prides maintain layered pairings, others keep celibate warrior companies, still others blend generational kin with oath-companions. Childrearing is communal; biological parentage is known but secondary to the pride bond. Among these Alvai, romantic love is praised but not idealized in singularity -- passion burns best in a hearth, not a matchstick, as their proverb has it. These customs sit at sharp angles to [[The Light|Lurist]] orthodoxy, which reads monogamy as a social more, demands sacramental legitimation of children and traceable lineage, and treats polyamory as indulgence at odds with proper Right Appetites. Missionary attempts to break kin-circles by enticing individuals into monogamous unions rarely succeed and have on occasion provoked formal pride-duels. For the Sunkin, Lurist monogamy is brittle, possessive, and unnatural -- a flame that burns for one thing alone is but a mere candle, and they are the sun.

At home, vast clan-holds ring high savannah oases, and the lion-spirit that is their totem is honored at sunrise, midday, and sunset through offerings of oil and smoke, antiphonal chants, and ritual combat kept to first blood. Gold is sacred as the sun made solid, and their heraldry is circular and radiant -- sun-disks crowned with a lion's face, sigils set as mane-flames or paw-suns, cloaks in burnt ochre, gold, and crimson. They do not follow Lur and speak of the Light as pale beside their blaze; the [[Church of the Light|Hierophancy]]'s cautions, to them, are the talk of shadow-dwellers that fear noon.

Many prides range outward from Lu-An. In [[Kingdom of Lavion|Lavion]], the coastal city-states of [[Riv-î-Tep]], and even  the [[Commonwealth of Principalities|Principalities of the Commonwealth]], one meets their mercenary companies who hire only as intact prides and refuse to be broken into scattered retainers. They do not disdain coin, for gold is sacral, but they serve only those deemed worthy of the Lion's gaze -- not necessarily the richest, but the boldest, the most radiant in purpose. Weak-willed nobles can bid tenfold and be refused where a fierce rebel matron secures their claws for fame alone.

Their tactics are an extension of pride-craft. Training begins in adolescence, and the resulting cohesion borders on the uncanny. Circulean scholars may debate whether the unity is residual animal kinship, an undeclared resonance, or some cultural survivance of something akin to Remean group-shaping, but for the prides the cause matters less than the deed. On the field they prefer encirclement and collapse, baiting and flanking until a weak point can be struck with ruthless concentration, and practice harmony feints in which one member draws the enemy to a false opening and the others cut in.

The pride ideal also governs their commerce in war. Contracts are negotiated with the pride as principal; service terms specify that a pride will not be broken; and penalties for insult scale to the injury done to the bond itself. A client who separates a pride by order or arrears risks more than lost service -- feuds have begun over lesser trespass. This contractual clarity is one reason courts across the Circle Sea, even in pious jurisdictions, tolerate their otherwise heterogenous forms; another is simple prudence.

The intersection of pride and family does bring tensions, internal and external. A small enough birth-pride may dissolve when youths come of age; adults join or form functional prides according to temperament, skill, and desire rather than blood. Siblings commonly live in different prides; children are as readily fostered across oaths as across kin. Friction arises when blood and pride pull at odds -- a parent disapproves a child's chosen pride, or rival prides include feuding cousins -- and such cases are usually steered to ritual arbitration or to adjudicated pride-duels under the eye of Dawnbinders, the elders and shamans charged with keeping the circle unbroken. Ritual life hews to the embodied and the evident. They dismiss Lurist aniconism as fear of naming what stands before one. They have a saying for that habit, offered with shrug and smile. "The Sun does not argue. It rises above."

In law and diplomacy, the independence of the Kin of the Sun Lion is guarded by warrior-envoys who negotiate contracts, keep score of owed honors, and call for formal duels when clients fail their bonds. Attempts to convert them have failed repeatedly and sometimes bloodily; even at the height of crusading zeal no Lion-Blood banner marched as Lurist levy. To be guarded by a pride in the present age is therefore not a mere token of wealth; it advertises a will that is not ashamed to shine.

The fiercest pressure, however, comes from outside when authorities attempt to impose family forms for taxation, inheritance, or moral policing. Where prides settle in jurisdictions that will not recognize their bonds, they adapt by fiction and craft: pride-mates register as wards and retainers of a nominal couple; polyamorous households pass as extended kin with cousins and servants; extended pride-groups style themselves as chartered companies or partnerships to preserve legal personality. On rare occasions reform-minded clergy have offered full recognition if a pride will accept monogamous vows and spiritual oaths; when such projects do not collapse at the threshold, they tend to produce internal fracture rather than the intended harmony.

Their own voices express the same truths with more music. Tsolari of the Burning Step, a warrior-philosopher whose pride took service beside peasant rebels in the Merhal, phrased it thus: "You come to me with one ring, one vow, one truth. We come with seven songs, and none are the same, yet all are ours." In this one hears the Sunkin's disdain of Lurism as a monotheism of the heart and the soul, a narrowing of the bonds of kinship and a starving of the hearth for the sake of a single flame. To them this is gravest heresy, for a lion is not born alone; a lion belongs to the pride.
