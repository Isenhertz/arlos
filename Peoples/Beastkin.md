The heritage known colloquially as the "Beastkin" is an oft-seen but ill-understood phenomenon, connected intrinsically to the [[Animal Spirit|Animal Spirits]] of Arlos that also give the Kin of the [[Alvai]] their forms. Why these spirits also seem to randomly mark human children is a mystery.

These "beastkin", "beastblooded" or "beastmarked" people are thus not an ethnicity nor a stable social group, but merely individuals who exhibit animal traits -- such as fur, claws, altered eyes, or other morphologies -- which often emerge at puberty. Such traits can be inherited, but most appear unpredictably across all known human populations.

Because of their visible differences and the mystery surrounding their origin, beastkin are often marginalized or ostracized in structured societies. Many gravitate toward "found family" communal structures that offer mutual protection and understanding, even when they are not ethnically, socially, or genealogically related to one another. Their shared experience of difference creates solidarity more than any shared heritage.

The same society that keeps a wary balance of Right Habit, Right Attention, and Right Appetite will sometimes lift a beastmarked person to high office, and at other times hound another into the shadows for traits no more or less explicit. The difference is rarely the anatomy itself. It is the reading of the anatomy through context -- era, office, patronage, profession, grooming, and the story a body tells about appetite and self-mastery.

## Nature of the Mark

The beastmark expresses in features of beast and folk mixing and intermingling. For example, a heritage bearing the mark of Cat will express features that read as feline, such as slit eyes, striped or spotted fur, pronounced canines or snouts, a tail, and so forth, and these features might be roughly analogous in appearance to those of tigers, lions, panthers, cheetahs, or housecats, without any indication that one of these is more powerful than another. The exact expression can differ greatly between individual expressions of the same mark: one catmark might present in full form of fur, muzzle, and feline clawed paws, while another might only have tufted ears and a tail.

Stories of beastkin shifting fully into animal form are more likely tall tales and wilful misinterpretations. However, historically some beastkin did find a way to express their mark even more pronouncedly: in "paragon form" the beastly attributes of the mark are exaggerated to grotesque levels, and hulking brutes are usually the result. The talk of "berserkers" in ancient days -- when not speaking of the horrors of the [[Bâlghubäerhe|Fallen Kin]] of the Iron Bear --  must probably be considered to be a reference to bearmarked warriors using their paragon form to perform feats of incredible power and ferocity. Dangerous to the individual not least because of impaired risk assessment when employing the form, it has never been of much use militarily, though it remains a concern when confronting beastkin face to face.

## On the Stigma of the Mark

### Soteriological
The centuries-old prejudice against people bearing a mark of the beast is best understood from the perspective of three cognate anxieties. Each of the great Lurist rites names the trouble in its own idiom, and all three settle on the same underlying grievance: the mark, whenever and however it comes, arrives outside anyone's choosing and then never changes again, its features fixed forever the instant it appears. Whether carried from birth, from puberty, or from a single moment of terror or rage, the combination of an event no one can foresee with a result no one can later undo is one each current of the creed finds hard to absorb into its picture of a rightly ordered soul, household, or self.

In the Orthodox, Anish rite, the anxiety can be tied to Lurism's neat syllogism about souls and order. Generally in Lurism the path of a soul is refinement toward the Light through the triune harmony of Body, Mind, and Soul, so that at death the latter may rejoin the Light of Lur. Where this balance is not achieved, the Soul returns to the world, heavy with unworked appetites and habits. Orthodox reasoning reads a mark carried from birth is taken as visible proof of a soul's prior turn through the world, unrefined appetite worn openly rather than merely inherited in temperament; a mark that waits for puberty is read as the Body asserting itself at precisely the age appetite first truly tests a person, an omen that the coming adulthood will be a harder contest than most; and lastly a mark that arrives in a single dreadful moment of adult crisis is the starkest case of all, in that it represents not merely an echo of some old failure but a moment when the Body plainly overruled Mind and Soul entirely, thereafter fixed in flesh forever amd unable to soften with time or be quietly outgrown, the way an ordinary lapse might be. Hence a common soteriological rationalisation goes that a beastmark displays the body's pull so boldly that the soul it houses must be further from harmony, and not refined enough to ascend. The conclusion, circular but persuasive to the pious, is that beastmarked persons bear the outward and permanent proof of a failure of the Soul, whether old or new, and hence they are less likely to succeed in the future, a doomed spiral only ever leading further from the Light. Neither hard-won refinement of the soul nor even a saintly state of triune harmony cannot erase the mark's evidence from the body that carries it.

While Concordian theology has little patience for talk of an individual soul weighed down by an animal past, it has its own version of the same unease, rooted in its emphasis on the Triune Family and the Six Relations as the lived, civic scale on which "Three is Six" is enacted. In the Church of the West, marriage is a sacred creedal act of high significance, the deliberate formation of a household meant to sit legibly within a foreseeable web of inheritance, alliance, and obligation. A beastmark unsettles exactly that legibility for the uncertainty it introduces. Whatever emerges, at birth, at puberty, or in some future crisis no one can predict, is final the moment it appears, an irrevocable fact folded into the line forever after, as the mark is inherited as often as it is not. Unlike the Alvai Kin, whose animality is a settled ethnic fact with generations of civic accommodation already built around it, a mark within one's own bloodline cannot be planned for or around. It is this irreducible combination of unforeseeable timing and permanent result, not the fur or the claws themselves, that a Concordian household and the magistrates who serve it find difficult to countenance.

The Eastern, Kharunite rite might be expected to read a beastmark most gently, given its ease with interactions with the spiritual and primordial, and its refusal to treat animal nature as inherently base. Indeed while Kharunite doctrine holds that each of the Three Natures of Beast, Man, and Saint are necessary, necessary is not the same as untethered, and it is precisely this sense of a nature unmoored from its proper place that in fact makes the Kharunite reading the harshest of the three. Though its harshness falls unevenly by the mark's timing: a mark carried from birth is closer to simple constitution, something a person is born to work with rather than evidence of anything; Kharunite pastoral teaching treats it much as it treats any native temperament, a starting condition for schooling rather than a verdict. A mark that waits for puberty is graver, since adolescence is precisely the trial in which the Three Natures are meant to be brought toward their adult order, and a Beast-mark that crystallizes permanently at that exact threshold intimates that the contest was lost at the one moment it most needed winning. Gravest of all is the mark that arrives in a single moment of crisis, in someone who by rights should already have schooled their natures into balance; Kharunism reads this as proof that Beast, however briefly, seized total precedence even in a supposedly disciplined adult, and that this capacity, once shown, is now a permanent feature of the person rather than a passing lapse to be corrected. Where Orthodoxy fears what the mark implies about a soul's history, and Concordianism fears what it implies about a bloodline's future, the Eastern Rite fears what it proves, unerasably, about a particular person's self-mastery on the one occasion it was tested and found wanting.

### In Society

Each rite's social ethic gives its soteriological unease a civic face, though the face differs by tradition. Lurism broadly teaches harmony not only within the person but within society, social hierarchy as a mirror of divine choreography, and in such a worldview conspicuous difference reads as potential discord in the ensemble. Prejudice thus recasts itself as guardianship of order. Guilds and magistracies presuppose that a mark will negatively interfere with a beastkin's ability to enact Right Habit into Right Action; marriage-minded families discriminate against the marked for fear of introducing an imbalance into their heirs, and unpredictability in inheritance offends an Enlightened's desire for tidy lines and placid households. None of this need be written in law to be effective.

This runs sharpest and most explicitly in Concordian lands, where the unease is not a vague civic mood but a fairly direct application of settled doctrine. Because the Triune Family is treated as the primary moral unit and marriage as the founding act of "Three is Six" at human scale, a beastmark's unpredictability strikes at the exact thing Concordian household theology is built to secure: a legible, foreseeable line of inheritance and alliance. A Concordian family need not believe anything at all about souls or the Void to find a beastmark troubling; it need only recognize that the trait defies the kind of planning a creedal marriage is supposed to make possible. This gives Concordian magistrates and guild-masters a tidier, less superstitious language for the same exclusion practiced elsewhere on soteriological grounds, and it is why Concordian unease can persist in courts and charters that have otherwise grown formally uncomfortable with talk of tainted souls.

The ripple effects reach deep into everyday life regardless of rite. To gain an apprenticeship in reputable houses often demands recommendations, a "character" of references for steadiness and good example. If a body that marks you as beastkin is already read as liable to turn away from Right Action, masters can turn you away without further elaboration. Where doors shut, you drift to the margins; there, found families grow as beastmarked lads and lasses find easier kin among even other marks than other unmarked people.

But of course the prejudice only justifies itself all the more through this reaction to exclusion: When beastmarked communities withdraw into their own courtyards and crews, critics point to the withdrawal as proof of intractable difference. When they excel at rough work or risky trades, that success is noted as animal knack rather than human skill, and used to fence them into those roles. Even when a sermon on harmony softens a parish for a season, a single conflict with one of the marked is enough to reset the tolerances to zero.

### Historical Seeds

The faith in Lur long ago displaced most older customs, and while rustic appeals to Noble Elements persist in speech and practice, orthodox clergy have disapproved of them for generations. In such a climate, a body that reads as close to wild Animal Spirits can be taken as a walking hint of heterodoxy, an embodied appeal to powers outside the Light. This memory belongs to the Anish rite specifically, and to the Theocracy of Eternal Dominion above all. In the first centuries after Reme fell, Lurism's crusader zeal turned on peoples whose bodies were half-beast by nature. The crusades in the Wolves' Teeth mistook the Moonkin for living examples of the proscribed somatic arts of Shaping; their naturally half-animal bodies looked to the zealous like yet another result of shaping the flesh, that inward art the Judges had condemned as the ultimate arrogance against the Light, and the campaigns against them were preached as purifications.

Neither the Concordian nor the Eastern rite carries this particular memory. Concordianism did not yet exist in any organized form during the Century of Crusades, but its bishoprics predate the crusade so that the fervor of the pogroms did not kindle here, and its later civic unease over beastkin, whatever its intensity, was never forged in that fire. The Eastern Rite, already an independent communion along the Salt Road since Judge Kharun's own ministry, stood apart from the Theocracy of Eternal Dominion's campaigns entirely; its harsher reading of the mark, where it exists, is a matter of technical doctrine on the Three Natures rather than inherited crusading memory. Only in Anish lands, and above all the Patriarchate of the North, does the beastmark carry the specific historical freight of the Wolves' Teeth crusades, layered on top of the soteriological reasoning common to all three rites.

That memory lingers, and regional church politics might modulate the volume, but they rarely change the tune. The modern, Third Hierophancy speaks in cooler tones than the theocracy of old, yet the same moral physics still applies in Anish lands: animalistic deviation is read through the soteriology of lacking refinement, through memories of wars against shaped flesh, and through a public doctrine that equates sameness with safety.

Though Lurist doctrine has always left room for redemption, a debt owed above all to Redemptionism's insistence that correcting the discordant sinner, not casting them out, is a faithful's most important charge, even among the myriad exalted saints we can find those that bore the mark of an animal and still reached triune harmony. In the absence of an explicit theological settlement that severs the imagined tie between visible animality and spiritual unworthiness, the old rationalisations, in whichever rite's idiom, will keep doing their quiet work, no matter how modern the society or how luminous the creed.

### Uneven Reception

Still, even in deeply pious Lurist lands, the public reading of a beastmark is never only about its anatomy. It is a judgment filtered through era, office, patronage, animal semiotics, presentation, risk narratives, and the social terrain where the marked person stands. Because Lurist soteriology so prizes the refinement of Body, Mind, and Soul into a harmonious one so the Soul may approach the Light, onlookers are taught to wonder whether a visible animality signals appetite in the saddle. They then answer that question by consulting context rather than the person. This is why a cruel hound-marked constable in a port town might draw praise (for upholding order) while am honest bat-marked lamplighter in a cathedral district will forever draw whispers about hidden vice (because his mark is seen to be at odds with Right Appetites, no matter his conduct).

Moreover, presentation often serves to reframe the mark as concession or as challenge to the mores of the Enlightened. Lurist catechesis praises virtue in action, performed in teachable ways. Hence groomed fur, modest gloves, polite scents rather than raw musk, a prayer-knot tucked at the belt where the tail extrudes -- such choices might cue the audience to see a concession to Right Habit, Right Attention, and Right Appetites. Refusing these concessions is not a doctrinal fault; however in practice it is read as a refusal of these structural pillars of society. Even this very refusal might still be celebrated as a form of bravery in a frontier speakeasy when it would be condemned as scornful insolence in a cathedral ward.
#### Beyond the Creed

Svenu has no theological reflex that would find an animal trait shameful. A people who venerate the Noble Six outright as kin, and who hold every lesser sprite and courtier as that kin's own household, are simply not primed to read the animal in a body as a lesser thing pressing up through a greater one. According to Svenish folk tradition a marked child may be watched more closely, yes, maybe scrutinized more often and judged along the lines of whatever semiotics the animal in question invites. But the mark itself is not a taint of the body, nor a condemnation of the soul. However and whenever the mark comes, at birth, at the turn of puberty, or fixed permanently in a single dreadful moment under duress, it reads as portent rather than disorder. It may yet be a mark of ill fortune, but that is a consequence of the bearer's acts, not something writting indelibly into the skein of fate.

That reflex has a name and a history behind it, though the history is more precise than the popular sense of the word abroad would suggest. The "berserkers" mentioned above root in a word originally from Svenic, which in Svenish tradition names not the mark itself but a trained mastery built on top of it. As paragon form does not come naturally to a marked person, any more than swordwork does, a berserker proper is a beastmarked veteran who has drilled the trick of calling paragon form up at will, a skill practiced that some marked individuals may show a natural gift for it, achieving a dramatic paragon effect with little training, and Svenish storytelling rates such gifts all the more highly for being the rare exception. For all that the form endangers most who use it, Svenish honor reserves its highest regard for the rarer thing still: a berserker skilled enough to enter paragon form at will and remain disciplined enough against the pull of beastly instinct to keep fighting with judgment while in it. Svenish tale-tellers are agreed that the second is by far the harder-won.

Varngard inherits this thread whole where an individual's personal honor is concerned; a berserker's feat in the Ring or the field, and the years of drilling that made it possible, are weighed there as they would be across the Gulf. But Varngard is not Svenu, and the same civilization that keeps the berserker's honor intact has spent generations cultivating a Circulean, Lurist-adjacent respectability as a mark of standing in its own right, and that project pulls directly against the inherited tolerance wherever the question shifts from a person's own deeds to a clan's continuity. The result is not a single Varngardi position but a spectrum, running along the two axes familiar to anyone with insight into Varngard's internal workings.

That means that a traditionalist house, whatever its wealth, keeps faith with the Svenish reading: the mark, however and whenever it came, says nothing about a person's fitness to lead, to marry into, or to be allied with, and leadership is decided the way it has always been decided, on capability shown. A progressive house has absorbed something closer to the Anish reasoning and underlying anxiety regarding a trait whose timing and shape cannot be foreseen, appearing without warning, reading it as a threat to a legible line precisely because it cannot be planned around in advance, whatever certainty it settles into once it has come.

What this means in practice is that a beastmarked person's prospects in Varngard turn less on any single Varngardi consensus than on which house they are born or married into, and on which of that house's two live axes is doing the deciding in a given case: a berserker's feat may be sung the same season a different branch of the same clan quietly passes tanistry to an unmarked cousin, and neither judgment need feel, to the people rendering it, like a contradiction.

### Fiendblood and Voidmark

Among the beastmarked, certain traits draw even sharper censure under Lurist dogma, and are commonly called fiendblooded or voidmarked in everyday speech. In the stricter patriarchates -- especially across An-Suur -- the label carries heavy penalties, for there the theology of refinement is taught with fewer allowances and more alarms.

The voidmark, however, is not a lineage but a reading imposed upon a body. Any beastmark that echoes the canon's vague cautions may be so named, if it but expresses traits associated with that canonical passage: horns; patches of scale; ratlike or barbed tails; cloven hooves; pupils or irises that strike the eye as inhuman; features judged too exact or too grotesque, as if geometry or excess had overruled the gentle asymmetries of ordinary folk. Some marked in this way hide what they can with caps, gloves, scents, and paint; many cannot, or refuse to make their bodies more palatable to a society that then marginalizes them regardless of their efforts.

In popular imagination a strong voidmark is easily implicated in corruption, shapeshifting, or forbidden arts regardless of the bearer's conduct. Consequences differ by what rite rules the land: In conservative heartlands the voidmarked may be denied temple entry, barred from guild seating or inheritance, or freely harried under the cover of piety -- at times even lynched when panic runs ahead of proof. In the more tolerant ports of Concordian or Harmonist sees, jurists and magistrates increasingly challenge these associations as confusions of heraldry with habit, yet the cultural stigma remains powerful, and custom may still close the door even where law would leave it ajar.

But folk tales turn the same fear into fascination. The fiendblooded recur as tragic figures, dangerous lovers, or cursed sages; a most common trope is the Horned Bride, who veils her mark until the wedding night and is either burned as a demon or redeemed by steadfast love. The endings vary with audience and era, but the lesson is constant -- the voidmark is made to stand for appetite unruled until ritual, duty, or devotion intervenes.
