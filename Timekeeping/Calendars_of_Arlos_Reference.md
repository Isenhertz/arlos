# The Calendars of Arlos

Three calendrical traditions are attested in the historical and present-day record of the Circle Sea basin: the extinct **Auralian** calendar of Remea, the **Belanar** calendar of the Ukili'ata, and the **Lurist (Solar)** calendar that has, in various regional recensions, become the near-universal civil and liturgical standard of the modern age. All three ultimately partition a 360-day year, though they arrive at that figure by different routes and divide it differently for different reasons -- Auralian reckoning grew out of lunar observation, Belanar reckoning out of a fixed astronomical cycle, and Lurist reckoning out of liturgical convenience layered atop an inherited solar count.

---

## 1. Comparative Overview

| Feature | Auralian (Remean) | Belanar (Ukili'ata) | Lurist (Solar) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Status today | Extinct; archaeological interest only | Living, but only official within Ukili'ata enclaves/fleets | Dominant civil and liturgical calendar of the Circle Sea |
| Year length | 360 days | 360 days (under Lurist influence) | 360 days |
| Primary large unit | Month (24 days) | Pass (60 days) | Span (15 days) |
| Units per year | 15 months | 6 Passes | 24 Spans |
| Secondary unit | Week (6 days) | Phase (15 days) | Week (5 days) |
| Weeks/Phases per large unit | 4 weeks per month | 4 Phases per Pass | 3 weeks per Span |
| Days per week/Phase | 6 | 15 | 5 |
| Year start | Summer Solstice | New moon following the Hiemal Solstice (Lurist day 289, within Winterbreak) | Spans center on solstices/equinoxes (day 8), rather than opening on them |
| Epoch | "Cadence" of a given Capitolina (no fixed universal epoch attested) | Originally none (Pass-counting only); now uses Lurist year | 0 AR = Fall of Reme |

*Note: the Auralian week (6 days) and Lurist week (5 days) are different subdivisions of the same 360-day year (360 ÷ 6 = 60 weeks; 360 ÷ 5 = 72 weeks) -- this is a genuine structural difference between the two systems, not an error.*

## 2. The Auralian Calendar (Remean)

Reckoned to the rhythm of Aural, the moon. Each 24-day lunar month divides into four 6-day weeks; fifteen months make a year, beginning at the Summer Solstice. Months are named for the zodiac signs accompanying the moon through the seasons.

## 3. The Belanar Calendar (Ukili'ata)

Anchored to the 60-day astronomical cycle of the Tortoise ("the Pass"), itself divided into four 15-day Phases of Belana. Originally the Ukili'ata counted Passes rather than solar years at all; under sustained Lurist contact, hybrid year-counting forms have become common.

- **1 Pass** = 60 days = 4 Phases
- **1 Phase** = 15 days
- **Modern civil use:** 6 Passes (24 Phases) = 1 Lurist year (360 days)

The Belanar calendar has no official standing outside Ukili'ata enclaves and fleets, but wide cultural familiarity with Belana across Circulean folk tradition means a "Belanar Pass" is a commonly understood unit of time, and its Phases are described as aligning "well enough" with Lurist Spans for practical date translation -- though the two are not exactly congruent.

The offset arises from two different anchoring conventions. The Lurist calendar centers its Spans on high feast days rather than opening on them: each equinox and solstice falls on day 8 of its Span (the middle day of the second week), a deliberate expression of the Lurist taste for balance, with the Span's first week serving as run-up and its third as wind-down. The Belanar new year, by contrast, begins outright on the first new moon of Belana following the Hiemal Solstice -- which falls within Winterbreak, on day 289 of the Lurist year. Since that date sits neither on a Span boundary nor on day 1 of one, every subsequent Belanar Phase and Pass is carried forward at a fixed offset from the Lurist Span structure, rather than drifting unpredictably year to year.

**Belana's New Moons Across the Lurist Year**

Belana's new moon is the most portentous phase in Ukili'ata reckoning -- the sky's own dark waypoint, read as a clean slate for star-reckoning and for setting a fleet's course anew. With a fixed 60-day cycle and the confirmed new moon opening the Belanar year on day 289, the new moon recurs every 60 days, six times across the 360-day year, one to open each Pass:

| Pass # | New Moon Day |
|---|---|
| 1 | 289 (Belanar New Year) |
| 2 | 349 |
| 3 | 49 |
| 4 | 109 |
| 5 | 169 |
| 6 | 229 |

Ukili'ata navigators hold the new moon in particular regard because it strips the sky bare of Belana's light, leaving the fixed stars -- by which their fleets set their courses -- at their clearest. A new moon falling mid-voyage is traditionally read as a favorable moment to correct a heading or take a fresh star-sight, and the six new moons of the year are accordingly the anchor points around which Ukili'ata sailing almanacs are built, rather than the Passes' nominal start-and-end dates alone.

## 4. The Lurist (Solar) Calendar

The dominant civil and liturgical calendar of the modern Circle Sea. 360 days, divided into 24 Spans of 15 days each; each Span further divides into three 5-day weeks. Spans are arranged so that equinoxes and solstices fall on the middle day of the second week (day 8 of the Span), allowing a build-up and wind-down of festivity around the high feast day itself.

**Days of the Week**

| # | Name | Character |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Isday | - |
| 2 | Ersday | Traditional pay-day (last Ersday of a Span) |
| 3 | Lurday | Sermons, prayer, rest -- the middle Lurday of a Span is highest in rank |
| 4 | Varday | Market day in many regions |
| 5 | Wynday | - |

**Date format:** *[Weekday], [Day Number] of [Span Name], in the [Year]th Year (AR)*

**Regional epoch variants**

| Tradition | Epoch | Offset from AR | Structural unit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Standard Lurist | Fall of Reme | AR + 0 | 24 Spans of 15 days (3×5-day weeks) |
| Concordianism | Enlightenment of the Prophet (355 BR) | AR + 355 | Same Span structure as standard Lurist |
| Velkirian Rite (Kharunite) | Martyrdom of Saint Kharun (177 BR) | AR + 177 | 6 Passes of 60 days (4×15-day Phases of 3×5-day weeks) |

### The Kharunite (Velkirian) Calendar

Where Concordianism keeps the Lurist Span structure intact and merely shifts the epoch, the Velkirian Rite departs more fundamentally: it discards the 24 Spans in favor of the Belanar framework described in Section 3, keeping only the five-day week, reflecting the Eastern Rite's origins among the riverine and highland communities of Vel Kiriya rather than the cathedral cities of the west. A Kharunite year is not divided into Spans but into **six 60-day Passes**, each made of **four 15-day Phases** of three five-day weeks apiece -- the Ukili'ata's own units, with the western week carried inside them.

This is more than a cosmetic difference. The western Lurist calendar is built around the Span as a liturgical container: each Span centers on a single high point (an equinox, solstice, or ordinary observance) at day 8, with the surrounding days serving as approach and withdrawal -- an arrangement of the calendar itself around triadic balance, in keeping with the Anish Rite's habit of thinking in threes. The Kharunite Pass has no such center. Its four Phases are undifferentiated in structure, and Velkirian feast-reckoning instead follows the mendicant, itinerant character of Eastern Rite practice: observances attach to the Judges' calendar of travel and to the Six Relations rather than to a fixed point within a subdivision of fixed length. Where a western parish organizes its year around the rhythm of a Span swinging toward and away from its middle day, an eastern congregation organizes its year around the passage of Phases and the arrival and departure of circuit-riding Judges.

The two calendars are further offset by their differing epochs and by the mismatch already noted between Belanar's lunar-anchored new year (see Section 3) and the Lurist Span's day-8-centered structure -- meaning that even where a Kharunite and a western Lurist date nominally fall "in the same season," translating one into the other requires accounting for both the 177-year epoch shift and the underlying structural offset between Pass/Phase and Span/week. In practice, western clergy operating in mixed-rite borderlands (the Merhal, Riv-î-Tep, the Drowned Coast) keep conversion tables rather than attempt the arithmetic case by case.

## 5. The Span Calendar and Feast Days

### Lunar and Planetary Conjunctions

Because the Lurist year is a clean 360 days, and both moons keep fixed cycles against it (Aural's 24-day month, Belana's 60-day Pass), their alignments recur on the same calendar days every year rather than drifting. Given the two cycles' relative phase, it is the **full moons** of Aural and Belana that fall together, rather than their new moons: this full-moon conjunction recurs every 120 days (the lowest common multiple of the two cycles), landing three times each year -- **day 79** (Grainwater), **day 199** (Fallbreak), and **day 319** (Greatsnow). Aural's independent full moon (not in conjunction with Belana) falls on **day 271**, within the Hiemal Solstice span, consistent with the "full Aural" already noted for Lanterns of First Bright. Belana's new moon, meanwhile, keeps its own separate rhythm, landing on day 289 to open the Belanar New Year -- a date that does not coincide with an Aural phase-match at all, which is itself considered a small omen of the year's ordinariness.

Of the year's three full-moon conjunctions, the one on day 319 carries the greatest weight. Local reckoning names this date's conjunction the **Grand Conjunction**, though the name is fully earned only once every twelve years, when the slow wandering light of **Langea** -- whose stately circuit closes every 4,320 days, a clean twelve Lurist years -- returns to the same station in the sky and joins the two full moons in alignment. Unlike a conjunction of new moons, in which both lights vanish from the sky together, the Grand Conjunction is a conjunction of fullness: Aural and Belana stand at their brightest simultaneously, and once every twelfth year Langea's light joins them, making the night the most luminous of the calendar rather than the darkest. In the intervening years the day-319 conjunction is observed as an ordinary, if still auspicious, doubling of the two moons at their fullest; only in the twelfth year does Langea complete the triad, and it is this rarer event that popular memory and court astrologers alike watch for.

## 6. Feast Days in Detail

What follows is the full run of the calendar's feast days in order, gathering the fuller descriptions abbreviated in the Notes column above alongside the three new Orthodox winter observances proposed to fill the long silence between the Hiemal Solstice and the Vernal Equinox.

### Day of Names (Vernal Equinox, day 14)

Traditionally the day from which a person's age was counted, regardless of actual birth-date -- a single shared reckoning-point rather than a scatter of individual anniversaries. Modern practice has largely shifted to counting age from the day of birth itself, but the age of majority is still determined from the most recent Day of Names, leaving a small, persistent legal quirk in an otherwise superseded custom.

### Stone Charter and First Furrow (Springbreak, days 16-30)

Stone Charter opens the building season: lines are struck, foundations blessed, and contracts haggled over at the nave door, binding the year's construction to the Church's oversight from the very first stake in the ground. First Furrow follows close behind, weather permitting (and falls instead in Rainwash if it does not): households carry their smallest tools to the nave steps for a blessing of hands and implements, boundary stones are chalked at noon, and a scattering of ash marks the first tilled ridge. Older habits still show through the liturgical surface here and there -- a thread of green tied round a sapling, a murmured word to the Furrow Folk and the Dewlasses, quietly recast as emissaries of the Light rather than the old Lord of Loam whose name once stood where the creed now does.

### Shore's Blessing (Rainwash, days 31-45)

Known in Balboa as the "Sagra della Pescoda," Shore's Blessing sees sluices planed true and weirs scrubbed clean, with prayers spoken for the water's bounty and its mercy alike. Riverfolk hang blue ribbons at the foot of bridges where no one will trip on them, regattas are run, and fish markets sponsor free stew for the poor. The homily speaks only of right stewardship, but no one in the crowd is under any illusion about whose favor is actually being courted.

### The Great Fast of Alignment and the High Day of the Light (Estival Solstice, days 1-15)

The summer solstice is the High Day of the Light, and the occasion when the Church lets itself blaze. The Great Fast has pared the city down for seven days -- cisterns skimmed, lamp glass polished, scales checked, tongues reined, each evening broken by Nightly Verses -- and the Night of Harmony has just folded quarrels and petty debts away. Now the hinge of light swings open on Day 8, and the rite remembers the Prophet's climb toward enlightenment not as a tale to admire but as a path to tread.

Dawn begins in white and water. Households wash in basins set in the street, three pourings for Mind, Soul, Body; beadles chalk the old geometry on the flagstones so feet will keep measure. The first sun breaks and a disciplined sun-gazing follows, brief and veiled by thin linen so eyes are guided rather than scorched -- an heirloom practice the Church tolerates when it is sober and supervised. The city faces east, recites the creed by triplets, and then turns inward for the first procession of three.

Those processions are the day's spine. Three circuits thread the basilica and its courts, each with a station that echoes a step of the Prophet. First is the Gate of Mind: a reading from the Book of Verses on truth named plainly, with a homily that braids law to clarity and bids the Enlightened to cleanse the inward lens. Second is the Door of Soul: the chant lengthens, choristers and lay voices answering antiphon by antiphon, and the homily presses mercy and concord until the crowd can feel its own anger loosen. Third is the Threshold of Body: tools and hands are blessed, oaths of right habit are spoken aloud, and the homily ties appetite to measure so strength will serve harmony rather than hunger. At each station water is lifted in shallow bowls to catch the sun and cast moving lattices across stone, to simulate the Light moving through all reality.

By noon the basilica is dressed as richly as an aniconic church dares. Awnings of white and cloth-of-gold throw shade; long mirror-plates send quiet flashes from tower to tower; braziers of fragrant resin stand not to smoke the air but to thin it, so throats will carry. The High Homily of the Light is the most elaborate sermon of the year -- three parts, three returns, three charges. First truth: the Light is the source of all knowing, and to taste it is to be responsible for what one knows. Then harmony: the One is Three is Six is All, and the task of the Enlightened is to reconcile their inner triad until it sings. Then measure: power is lawful only when yoked to right appetite, and joy is safest when yoked to care. Each movement ends with a rising cadence the congregations know by heart, a long breath that feels like a step taken upward.

Music makes the ascent audible. Throughout the day the city becomes an instrument. Children's choirs ring the nave at first light; women's voices carry the Gate of Mind; men's voices answer at the Door of Soul; and at the Threshold of Body the full assembly sings. In the streets after the sermon, guild choruses stand under their eaves and trade verses in celebration. The harmonies chase the rumor of the Illuminated Choir -- mortal throats making a broken echo of the cosmic order -- and the custom is to leave the last chord unresolved so the sky can finish it.

Splendour follows, not spectacle. There are processions in white and gold, carrying likenesses of the saints that walked the path before and now show the way. Water wardens sprinkle the front ranks where the crowd presses, a benediction as practical on the longest day of the year as it is pious. Fire brigades post pump carts at the corners and carry their axes peace-sheathed, a reminder that strength attends joy. Bakers pass out honeyed wheat loaves scored with three cuts; vintners pour a measured cup in the plaza's shade; physicians and midwives receive a public blessing for the work of keeping bodies in lawful accord. In some parishes the old heliotropic walk survives -- three slow arcs paced at dawn, noon, and sunset -- mostly where basilicas are far and quiet introspection is more achievable than a procession.

As the sun leans west the rite descends with care. The Sunfall Litany is spoken facing the brightness, lamps shaded by hand so the last light can pass through finger-gates and segment the glare into clean bands. The final homily is quiet and pointed: to carry the feast into the week without swagger; to let fasting's clarity govern celebration's warmth; to remember that harmony is practiced most credibly when no one is watching. The closing chant is the simplest in the book -- three verses, plain as bread -- and the city answers in one voice with the creed.

Only then do the night markets bloom. The seven days after the High Day belong to joy and to muster. Stalls open under white awnings at dusk; guilds run games in their halls and the parish green; parades run on streets and canals alike with lanterns and music; registrars sit in the porches to seal pilgrim letters; parish staves and banners are aired and mended. On the first day of Summerbreak the pilgrims step off in ordered waves, still scented faintly of resin and bread.

### Pilgrim Send-off (Summerbreak, day 1)

Lurist pilgrimages are meant as walking meditations rather than parades, and yet the morning's departure is a beloved moment: bells and gongs step them out, river stairs fill with well-wishers, and the last hymn on the quay is a fond farewell. Those who cannot travel keep a local boundary circuit in the week ahead, touching steles and bridge stones, sweeping shrines and church steps, and leaving a cleaner city under their feet.

### Lonely Lamps (Seedbeards, day 139)

Held on the dark of Aural, when no moon competes with them, paper lanterns are set afloat on streams or released to the wind, guiding the souls of the Unfinished -- those not yet settled into harmony -- either toward salvation in the Light or safe return to life.

### Ashen Remembrance (Greatheat, first Lurday, day 168)

A remembrance of the Fall of Reme. Chimneys are swept and firebreaks inspected in the days before, and neighborhood fire drills -- which have a habit of turning competitive and a little raucous -- are run in earnest. At the height of noon's heat, bells and gongs ring together across the city and the names of those lost to conflagration over the past year are read aloud. A priest delivers the expected sermon on law, measure, and the sin of bending created powers to appetite, while beneath the solemnity a soft, unsanctioned murmur of courtesy runs through the crowd toward the Cinder-King who scourged Reme from the world.

### Gleaners' Peace (Fallbreak, harvest-dependent)

Stubble fields are thrown open to the poor once the harvest proper is in, a customary grace period timed to the fields' own readiness rather than a fixed calendar date.

### Festival of Pennants (Lastheat, beginning day 211)

Wind-ribbons and pennants are strung from mastheads in bright braids, left knotted and flying until the first snow of the year finally brings them down.

### Fallow Tables (Cooldew, last market day after harvest)

Whatever will not keep through winter is cooked all at once: kettles and ovens run in rows along the parish green, and every ward lays its trestle for strangers before its own. Smokehouses and pickle sheds work without pause, tithe barns are sealed with wax, and herds are culled so the winter hay will stretch. In orchard towns an older custom survives alongside the civic feast -- a wreath hung on the oldest tree, a nod to the Treefather recast as simple gratitude rather than an appeal to the old Wood-Lord.

### Coldward Vigil (Frostfall)

A watch kept as the year turns toward its coldest stretch; parish practice varies and no fixed liturgical form has been recorded beyond the name itself.

### Lanterns of First Bright (Hiemal Solstice, days 271-285, timed to Aural's full moon on day 271)

White lamps are set in windows and along canals for absent kin and the unremembered dead, while children recite couplets of the creed at doorways in exchange for sugared flatbread -- a gentler, more domestic counterpart to the High Day's high pageantry, timed to Aural's own light rather than the Lurist calendar's day-8 convention.

### New Fire (Winterbreak, night of day 286)

Once the heathen beginning of a new year, the western New Fire is now a civic rite held on the first night of Winterbreak. All lanterns remain unlit as night falls; then a single taper blessed at noon is carried forth, and the lamplighters carry its flame through alleys and squares, relighting lamps in a growing procession that ends at large communal bonfires. Children's choirs walk for collections and leave ash-marks for the hearths.

### New Fire, the Kharunite New Year (Winterbreak, dusk into night of day 289)

The Kharunite New Year shares its name with the western civic rite three nights earlier but not its descent: a distinctly Velkirian observance that has nonetheless taken root wherever Kharunite communities have settled in the west, from dockside wards in Balboa to the Merhal's mixed parishes, where two New Fires three nights apart are a standing source of confusion. It descends from the same household hearth-blessings recorded elsewhere in Eastern Rite practice, where the Light and Phlogistophor are invoked together as Hearth-Warden, scaled up here to the whole of a Kharunite quarter for the turning of the year. Every lantern is left unlit through the day; then, as the sun's last and palest light of the year fails at dusk, a single taper is blessed in that final glow and carried forth, relighting the quarter lamp by lamp in a procession that lengthens and brightens as full dark falls, ending at communal bonfires held long into the night. Children's choirs walk for collections and leave ash-marks at hearths, much as they do in the west, though the Judges leading the rite frame it in distinctly Kharunite terms: the Light in All persisting even at the year's darkest hour, requiring only a single spark carried faithfully from hand to hand to be seen again.

New Fire falls on the same night Belana's new moon would otherwise open the Ukili'ata year, but the two peoples keep it differently. The Ukili'ata mark their own new year by their own rites, distinct from anything Kharunite quarters do -- though Ukili'ata communities long settled among the Kharunites, or otherwise well assimilated into Velkirian city life, will sometimes join New Fire's procession in place of or alongside their own observance. That the same lunar node draws two related but separately-owned responses, with a porous border between them wherever the two peoples live close together, is a detail better explored on its own terms than folded fully into either.

### The Vigil of Saint Meraline (Winterbreak, night of day 292)

An unofficially canonized martyr of the Long War against Reme, venerated as a watcher who lit a warning-lamp to turn a column of refugees from disaster in the mountain dark and was found frozen at her post the next morning. Her vigil gives Orthodoxy its own quiet answer to the same midwinter darkness the Kharunite New Fire responds to three nights before, though the two share no lineage: lamps here are carried unlit-then-lit, hand to hand, through a parish's own houses rather than to any quarter-wide procession, and the night ends in silence rather than in bonfire and song.

### The Feast of Rekindled Light (Greatsnow, day 319)

Where New Fire answers the dark with flame carried by human hands, the Feast of Rekindled Light answers it with light that needs no carrying at all -- the day is fixed by Aural and Belana standing full together, not by anything a household or a Judge has to kindle. It is kept as a quiet nighttime watch rather than a blessing of any object: a household sets out a shallow bowl of water in a window or courtyard to catch the doubled moonlight and simply sits with it in silence, no procession, nothing carried out of the house. Where the High Day of the Light is the year's great daylight rite -- sun-gazing, sun-lifted water, a city moving in full view of the summer sun -- the Feast of Rekindled Light is its deliberate nighttime counterpart: the one occasion in the calendar when the Light is met in darkness rather than in brightness, and found to be present there just the same.

In a Grand Conjunction year, the watch does not change in kind, only in scale. Households still set out their bowls, but now empty their own lamps and hearth-fires as well, deliberately darkening their homes and streets so that the three lights overhead -- Aural, Belana, and, once in twelve years, Langea -- stand as the only illumination in the sky. Whole parishes keep the vigil together outdoors, in courtyards, on rooftops, along riverbanks, watching the water-bowls and the sky at once rather than any single fixed point of architecture. Orthodox clergy read the moment as the creed's own "One is Three" appearing unasked and unbidden, briefly outshining even the High Day of the Light itself -- a feast built around the year's greatest fast and effort, answered once in twelve years by a light the household did nothing to earn and, for one night, nothing to compete with either.

### The Fast of Ash and Patience (Greatcold, days 358-360)

A short, Antidiscordian-born counterpart to the Great Fast of Alignment, closing the year rather than opening onto its greatest feast. Strictest in the Patriarchate of the North (total abstention, a renewed ash-mark, near-silence) and steadily gentler moving south and west, it ends not the evening before the new year but at dawn of day 1 -- making the breaking of the fast the very first act of the Vernal Equinox span, immediately followed by that span's own week of building festivity toward the equinox on day 8.
