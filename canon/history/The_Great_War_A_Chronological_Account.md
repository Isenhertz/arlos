# The Great War: A Chronological Account

## The Undeclared War (1541-1561)

Long before the Empereur's herald delivered the formal declaration of war to a dumbstruck Parliament in 1561, the Great War was already being fought -- fought under company colors rather than national ones, in the archipelagoes and atolls of the Rim of Fire, by men who could be disowned the moment their actions grew inconvenient. The Lavienese Companies Royal and the Commonwealth's United Chartered Companies, chartered as mercantile monopolies rather than arms of either crown, kept their own fleets and their own soldiery for their own ventures, and it was these private forces, not the standing navies of Lavion or the Guard of the Common Weal, that first traded raids on rival factories and anchorages, first sank each other's convoys, first buried men in unmarked graves on foreign coral. Both empires disclaimed responsibility with a straight face for years, and both meant it exactly as much as such disclaimers are ever meant. This was a war of plausible deniability precisely because neither side could yet afford the alternative, and it went on long enough, and grew bloody enough in its quiet way, that by the time it could no longer be denied, an entire generation of company officers had already learned to fight this war before their crowns ever declared it.

## The First Reckoning (1561-1562)

When the deniability finally broke in 1561, Lavion's superiority in the opening campaigns was neither surprising nor, in its own eyes, particularly worth remarking upon. The Empire's core army fielded alumen breath-arms and aetheric-treated pliant armor against a Commonwealth patchwork of uneven quality, mobile arcannon batteries against arrangements that varied from Principality to Principality, and above it all a sky no rotostat nor recon craft yet contested in any serious way. For the officer corps, this first phase confirmed everything it had always believed about itself: that discipline, lineage, and academy training would tell in the field the way they had always told, and that the war would be short.

It very nearly was. In less than a single campaigning season, a string of the Commonwealth's own eastern princedoms -- those nearest the border and least able to muster a defense worth the name before Lavion's core army was already through them -- fell or surrendered outright, their levies scattered before any coordinated Commonwealth response could be organized at all. Winter halted the advance more than any Commonwealth resistance did, and by the time campaigning resumed, more than one prince who had ruled in his own right the previous spring now governed, if he governed at all, as an occupied city's civil authority under Imperial garrison. It was this run of quick, humiliating losses, more than any single defeat in the field, that forced the Commonwealth's hand -- proof, if any were still needed, that the old patchwork of separately-mustered princely levies could not simply be reinforced or better led into holding against Lavion's core; it would have to be replaced by something else entirely, and quickly, or there would be no eastern Principalities left to save.

The one note of friction, and it should not be understated, ran through the Empire's own ranks rather than across the front. Auxiliary levies drawn from the protectorates -- Eastern Gyre littoral conscripts, translupine cavalry raised from the very Korsovan stock Roburic's own campaigns had broken a lifetime before -- fought under Lavienese colors with brass-and-steel arms a full generation behind the core issue, and were regarded by the aristocratic officer corps with precisely the contempt such an arrangement invites: useful, expendable, and beneath real notice except as a body count to be spent sparingly. The labor-brassbacks employed behind the lines fared little better in officers' estimation, though for a different reason. An officer corps that measured its worth in personal glory earned in the field had no intention of sharing that glory with a machine, and so the brassback's considerable talents were confined to hauling shot, clearing rubble, and servicing the aeronaval ground crews -- a waste of the technology's evident potential that no one in a position to change it had any interest in changing, since changing it would have meant admitting a walking automaton could do a nobleman's job.

## An Uninvited Ally (1561-1562)

Vel Kiriya's entry into the war was announced as solidarity with the Commonwealth's cause and understood by everyone with eyes to see as nothing of the kind. The two powers shared an enemy and little else -- the Commonwealth fighting for its trade routes and its sovereignty, Vel Kiriya for Lu-Pto's iron sands and a pride that had never fully recovered from the earlier war over that same stretch of coast. Commonwealth diplomats who had hoped for a genuine second front found instead a partner whose armies moved on their own schedule, toward their own objectives, coordinating only so far as coordination happened to serve Velkirian aims as well. It was, from the first, a coalition of common grievance rather than common cause, and both sides knew it even as neither said so aloud.

What Vel Kiriya brought to that uneasy partnership, however, reshaped the whole character of the eastern front. A population base larger than Lavion's own let Vel Kiriya field hard pliant armor at genuine parity -- pliant craft having never been gated behind the aetheric kilns that bottleneck Lavion's alumen -- in numbers no auxiliary-padded Lavienese army could match, on supply lines Vel Kiriya could sustain far more readily than Lavion could answer by any means but its single aeronaval corridor across the eastern Lupines. Time itself, on this front, was already beginning to belong to the other side.

## The Lance Reborn (1562-1566)

In the west, the Commonwealth's early reverses -- an entire belt of eastern princedoms lost or occupied within that first punishing season -- forced an invention rather than a doctrine, and it is worth tracing how one grew from the other. The first response, mustered in haste through the winter of 1561 and into the following spring's campaigns, was simple attrition management: pair a rifleman with a labor-pattern brassback repurposed for close support, extra hands to carry, to dig, to take up a gap in the line a shrinking company could no longer fill on its own. What emerged from that expedient over the several campaign seasons that followed, as princely armorers and guildsmen refined it, was something closer in spirit to an old knight's lance than to any conventional infantry file -- a "rigged" soldier, wearing the pneumatic-tendon-and-hard-pliant exoskeleton the ranks had already taken to calling dumb brass, at the center of a small working unit built around him rather than beside him. The one fixed rule was a floor rather than a ceiling: at least one proper brassback fighting wingman to every rigged soldier, the two operating as a single unit rather than as interchangeable files in a larger line. Beyond that floor, a lance's actual size was simply a question of what the Principality mustering it could afford to field -- a wealthy trading city might send a rigged soldier out with two or three brassbacks in support, a poorer one could barely guarantee the one, and a company's fighting strength on any given day of the war was as much a matter of its home city's coffers as of the ground it held. Where Lavion's doctrine insisted on keeping man and machine in separate, hierarchically ordered spheres, the Commonwealth's necessity dissolved that separation into a genuine tactical partnership, and did so precisely because it could never afford the manpower to do otherwise.

It was this pairing, multiplied across a hundred uneven Principality contingents by 1564 or so, that finally let a numerically inferior Commonwealth army hold ground against Lavion's qualitatively superior one where the old princely levies had broken -- not by matching the Empire soldier for soldier, but by making every pairing of soldier and machine worth more than the sum of the two, and, in more than one recovered eastern princedom, by making the ground costly enough to hold that Lavion's own occupying garrisons found themselves stretched thinner than the year before had ever required. It was the same necessity, working itself out over these same years of shared front lines, that began to dissolve the old borders between one Principality's levy and the next. Lances raised in different cities, mustered under different princely banners and to different standards of equipage, found themselves holding the same stretch of line long enough that cooperation stopped being an arrangement and became a habit; companies that had begun the war as seasonal levies, sent home once a campaign ended, increasingly did not go home at all, reconstituting themselves the following season under officers who had learned each other's signals and each other's weaknesses rather than starting over with strangers. More than one such combined force, cutting across the old princely musters entirely, took to calling itself -- first as a boast, then as something closer to an oath -- auxiliaries to the Guard of the Common Weal, borrowing the one name in the whole Commonwealth that had never belonged to a single prince, and by 1566 the borrowing had outgrown its origin as a rallying cry: these were standing armies now in every practical sense, whatever their muster rolls still called them.

## The Sky Contested from Below (1563-1567)

The western front's air war followed its own halting path from necessity to innovation. The Commonwealth's rotostats began the war as reconnaissance craft pure and simple, a compact answer to an army that could never afford to be surprised by Lavion's concentrated core. But an army built around the rigged-and-wingman pairing had every reason to want fire support that could arrive exactly where a threatened line was about to break, and neither Lavion's patient, far-loitering aerocitadels nor its pipe-jet aeronavalliers, built for dogfighting and vertical assault, were suited to that role at all. Necessity did what doctrine would not: engineers shrank the gas envelope further, let rotor and engine carry more of the burden, armored the gondola in the same hard pliant doing service on the ground below, and by slow, costly degrees turned a recon craft into something that could loiter low over a contested field and fight for it. The cost was paid first in crews who had no doctrine to protect them from ground fire at gunship altitudes, and only later in the tactics -- suppressive coordination with the troops below, evasive low-level flying -- that made the role survivable. Lavion never bested this innovation outright, and did not need to: the rotostat gunship's true achievement was never air superiority, which Lavion's pipe-jet arm retained throughout the western war, but the simple fact that Commonwealth ground troops finally had someone overhead answering when they called.

Lavion's own answer to this development was improvisation rather than invention, and it showed. Lacking any industrial tradition built around rotostats, the Empire pressed civilian aeronaves and up-armed merchant flying boats into gunship service instead -- craft never built to carry combat loads or absorb ground fire, crewed by conscripted merchant aeronauts the proper aeronaval corps regarded with the same faint contempt the ground officers reserved for a labor-brassback. It solved the immediate tactical problem badly rather than not at all, and left the western front's skies, for the first time in the war, genuinely contested rather than simply owned.

## The Eastern Sky and Its Western Echo (1563-1567)

The eastern front, by contrast, had never known a sky Lavion could take for granted. Vel Kiriya's flying boats, armed for defense on principled refusal of pipe-jet technology, met Lavienese aeronavalliers in something closer to a running gunship-and-fighter contest than any clean contest of superiority, and where the airframes alone might have left Lavion narrowly ahead, invoked wind and fog turned against the Empire's reconnaissance and interdiction tipped the balance further still. It was, in more than one veteran's later telling, the strangest inversion of the whole war: that the front where Lavion's aeronaval doctrine was most tested was also the one where its enemy fielded no rotostat, no exoskeleton, nothing resembling the Commonwealth's mechanized answer at all -- and needed none, because the weather itself had been recruited instead.

It did not escape Lavion's own high command that its eastern reverses were, in their way, teaching the western front a lesson before the western front had learned it for itself. The same pressure that drove the Commonwealth toward rotostat gunships pushed Lavion, on the western side of the same war, toward the same improvised pressganging of civilian flying boats it might otherwise have been slower to attempt -- an unacknowledged debt to an enemy the Empire's own propaganda was, at the very same moment, dismissing as backward for its refusal of proper engines.

## The Widening Circle of Invocation (1562-1568)

What began, in the war's first eastern engagements, as invoked ice and water set directly against Lavion's incendiary doctrine did not remain confined to those two elements for long. As the front hardened and the stakes of any single engagement grew, Velkirian shrine-wardens and Judges extended the same grammar of address to Stone and Soil in support of field fortification and sapping, to Air in the weather-work already turned against Lavion's aeronaval corps, and, more cautiously and more rarely, even to Fire itself -- a use no shrine-warden undertook without real hesitation, given what that particular address had cost an empire once before. Every invocation of this kind was undertaken under close clerical supervision, and every one of them, however lawfully framed, however carefully distinguished in Velkirian doctrine from the coercive bloodcraft of the Sorcerer-Kings or the compulsion that doomed Reme, drew the same quiet, unresolved question from more than a few of the Judges themselves: at what scale does a lawful address, repeated across a whole army and a whole war, begin to resemble the very overreach their own scripture warns against? No formal ruling settled the matter before the war's midpoint, and it is not clear that one ever fully will; the doubt travelled with the warhost as constantly as the invocations themselves, neither dispelled nor permitted to halt the war effort it accompanied.

## The Resistance of Oar and Axe (1561- )

Varngard had never been fully pacified, whatever Lavion's own maps insisted since Roburic II's day, and the Great War gave its old resistance a new and unlooked-for weapon. The mountain and highland custom of gliding was, by this point, old enough to feel entirely native to Varngard and to Svenu alike -- a pastime that had spread passively basin-wide during the Aetheric Age, requiring nothing beyond a thermal, an elevated jumping point, and the kind of youthful boredom no century or country has ever lacked, so thoroughly diffused by the time of the war that scarcely anyone flying a highland glider knew or cared that the sport traced back, generations upon generations earlier, to tribal youths riding the thermals of Lu-Pto's basalt desert alongside the sprites of Air and Fire that played there. It was this same lineage, appropriated far more recently and far more deliberately after Roburic II's own conquest of that desert, that had given Lavion its aeronavalliers in the first place -- so that a Varngardi partisan taking to the air against an Imperial pipe-jet trooper was, without knowing it, meeting a stolen inheritance with the unstolen original.

What made the highland resistance more than a nuisance, however, was not the gliding alone but what the Svenish call samasiðr, Bonding -- an elemental relation that travels with a person rather than staying behind at any hearth or threshold, inherited by Varngard from its Svenu-descended stock in the narrower, person-to-person form the custom takes south of the Gulf. Most who carry such a bond carry only a moling, a sprite-tier acquaintance won through patient courtesy, no sturdier than any wild sprite an invoker might court elsewhere -- and no more able to survive a pipe jet's intake than any sprite would be. An adaling is another matter. Won outright by some deed a courtier itself judges worthy, and shaped, when it answers a summons, into a beast proper to its own element, an adaling belongs to a different order of being entirely, and it was quickly discovered, at real and uncertain cost, that an Air-adaling could grapple a Lavienese aeronavallier and survive an engagement that would have annihilated any lesser sprite outright. What that survival actually cost the elemental in question -- whether the bond simply held, strained but intact, or whether something less visible was worn away each time -- was a question no partisan or fyrstinna could yet answer with any confidence; no one before this war had ever had reason to learn what a bonded courtier risks against a machine built to consume its own kin.

The tactic itself was blunt rather than clever: not sabotage of the jet mechanism, but the same close, direct grapple a bonded pair would bring to any fight, beast against soldier, fought at an altitude and a speed no partisan alone could have survived. Equipment for it was correspondingly humble at the low end and precious at the high: simple wood-and-cloth sport gliders, built or repaired by anyone who still remembered the old highland craft, formed the resistance's everyday mainstay, while a smaller number of sturdier, Commonwealth-supplied military-pattern craft -- smuggled in as one quiet thread of the Commonwealth's own war against Lavion -- were reserved for whichever partisans had proven, by deed and by favor both, worth the greater investment. It was not lost on Varngard's own storytellers that the same measure of a person's exceptional character applied to both hierarchies at once: a partisan trusted with a Commonwealth glider was, more often than not, already remembered as one who had gathered more than a single adaling in her own name, and Varngard tells the two facts as one story rather than two.

## The Armies at the Turn of the War (1567-1568)

By 1567 and into 1568, some seven years into the fighting, each belligerent's army had settled into a shape that owed as much to necessity and culture as to any doctrine written before the first shot was fired in 1561.

Lavion's core remained what it had always been: a small, aristocratic officer corps leading alumen-armed, pliant-armored regulars, its mobile arcannon batteries the finest in the Circle Sea by a wide margin, drawing on a tradition of integrated gun-and-infantry maneuver refined since the War of Three Kings itself. This core still won essentially every engagement it could be brought to in full strength, and still could not be everywhere at once. Auxiliary levies, unreliable and openly resented by the officers they served beside, padded its numbers on both fronts; labor-brassbacks hauled and dug and never fought, by aristocratic insistence rather than any real technical limit. Overhead, pipe-jet aeronavalliers and aerocitadels retained uncontested mastery in the west and a hard-fought, still-narrow edge in the east. At sea, a navy stretched across home defense, the western war, and the standing dread of the Rush depended, more than any Lavienese admiral cared to say aloud, on a Balboan fleet that had lent its assets and officers faithfully for seven years of war and four centuries of peace before it -- reliable in every way the Three Kings' settlement had ever asked of it, and not yet asked what it would do should the asking ever cost Balboa its own home.

The Commonwealth had, by now, largely stopped resembling the uncoordinated patchwork it began the war as. Lances of rigged soldiers and their brassback wingmen, once mustered separately by a hundred uneven Principalities, increasingly held the line together as de facto standing armies, still varying in equipment and polish from one contingent to the next but no longer strangers to one another the way they had been in the war's first seasons. Guns were few, and no Principality pretended otherwise, but what artillery the Commonwealth fielded was excellent, concentrated less in mobile batteries than in fixed defensive installations, particularly along the coastline, where it did not need to be anywhere but where it already stood. Rotostat gunships, matured well past their reconnaissance origins, now flew as the Commonwealth's real substitute for a mobile gun arm, arriving over a threatened line the way a battery could never arrive in time. The Guard of the Common Weal itself remained largely fixed in place, watching over Santra's exposed approach on the Western Gyre, its name borrowed by the field armies as an aspiration more than an accurate description of who actually commanded them.

Vel Kiriya, by contrast, fought a war of mass and depth rather than of maneuver. Its infantry, armed with solid if unspectacular brass-and-steel breath-arms and hard pliant at genuine parity with Lavion's own baseline, simply outnumbered whatever Lavion could bring against it, sustained by supply lines Lavion could not match outside its single aeronaval corridor. Arcannon here were plentiful rather than precise, massed in depth to grind down an assault rather than maneuvered to break one, a fit for a military culture already inclined toward defense-in-depth over quick decision. What gave this front its truly distinctive character, though, was the quiet, clerically supervised work of Stone- and Soil-invocation alongside the guns -- ground made to swallow a footing, a slope made to give way at the worst possible moment, softening an advance the way a preparatory bombardment might elsewhere, without a single shell fired to do it. Overhead, defensively armed flying boats and invoked wind and fog kept Lavion's aeronaval corps honestly contested in a way no other front managed, and at sea, the Rush remained what it had always been for this theater: the one route by which Vel Kiriya could reach faster and hit harder than Lavion could ever answer by anything but air.

## The Hinge of History (1568-1569)

### The Balboan Question

No question hanging over the Great War was more pressing than this one, though it had never been a question of Balboa's good faith. For four centuries the Principality had honored the Three Kings' settlement exactly as written -- naval assets and officers lent to the Crown's ongoing use, mercenary companies free to contract as they always had, the old oath never once tested because no circumstance had ever asked more of it than it plainly gave. What Vel Kiriya's push toward the Ertran coast raised, for the first time since the settlement was made, was not a doubt about Balboa's word but a question the settlement itself had simply never had to answer: what happens to a fealty measured out in ships and officers once the giving of them starts to cost the giver its own home.

The dependency the question threatened to expose was total, and Lavion's own strategists had understood this for a long time without ever having to say so aloud. Balboa's fleet and officers, granted for the ongoing use of the Crown by the War of Three Kings' settlement, constituted the greater part of the naval strength that flew Lavion's colors across the Western Gyre -- not a supplement to Lavion's own navy but its effective core in those waters, four centuries of steady, faithfully honored practice quietly load-bearing in a way few in Lavion's own admiralty had ever had cause to examine closely. Balboa's position astride the Rush compounded this dependency rather than merely sitting alongside it: that swift current was the check that let Lavion govern who moved through it at all, and by extension who could reach the Western Gyre and, past it, the Eastern Gyre beyond. So long as Balboan hulls held that current in Lavion's interest, the door Vel Kiriya might use to strike swiftly at Lavion's holdings remained a door Lavion itself still controlled the key to. Should that arrangement ever narrow to Balboa's interest alone, the loss would not stop at the west -- every Imperial holding east of the Rush, the Eastern Gyre littoral protectorates and the aeronaval corridor's own anchorage among them, depended at bottom on a current Balboa alone commanded passage through.

What came of the reckoning was not betrayal but escalation, each step defensible within the letter of four centuries of settled practice even as its spirit was asked to bear more than it ever had before. The Principe's first move was the least dramatic and the most easily justified: a drawing-in of Balboan hulls from the wider Gyre patrols to concentrate instead on securing the Rush itself, a redeployment that served Lavion's own interest nearly as well as Balboa's, since the current remained exactly as vital a check against Vel Kiriya in Balboan hands as in anyone else's. Only when the threat to the home city and the land sustaining it continued to mount did the Principe take a step no Balboan ruler had ever taken before: recalling every company and mercenary asset still under contract abroad, and declaring, for the first time in the Principality's long history of careful ambiguity, that Balboa would raise arms against any threat to its own homeland -- and only to its own homeland -- until that threat had passed. Whether any vow to Lavion was broken in the declaring of it remains a matter historiographers of the two realms have never agreed on since -- Balboan chroniclers insist the old oath only ever bound the Principality never to fight against home, and said nothing of what else Balboa might or might not do besides, while more than one Lavienese account of the affair reads the same silence as a betrayal dressed in the letter of the law.

### A Question Resolved

But the practical effect was the one Lavion had quietly dreaded for four centuries without ever having occasion to name it: within a single season, the eastern war lost the naval backing that had let it be fought at that distance from home at all. Lu-Pto became impossible to hold, and what Imperial forces did not surrender fell back on the Lu-An littoral instead, a position sustained thereafter by a direct air-bridge over the Lupines, by the mountain passes, and by reinforcement drawn south through Korsova, the very translupine stock Roburic's own conquests had broken now the corridor keeping Lavion's eastern war alive at all.

Vel Kiriya, for its part, read the moment correctly and did not overreach. Its war goals largely secured, it reduced its commitment of military and economic aid to the Coalition rather than pressing the advantage further, content to let a Commonwealth it had never truly fought alongside face the consequences alone. Enough pressure remained on the Lu-An littoral to keep the front from ever going quiet -- pinning Lavion's attention and its resources there rather than releasing them westward -- but the wholesale conquest that might once have been within reach was left, deliberately, undone. Lavion, for its own part, found itself unable to retreat further even had it wished to: abandoning Lu-An outright would have meant surrendering the iron sands wholesale, a military and economic loss the Empire was not prepared to accept on top of everything else the front had already cost it, and so the fighting there ground on, neither side willing to end it and neither able to finish it.

### The Fight for Home and Hearth

In the west, the Commonwealth found itself standing increasingly alone, Vel Kiriya's support thinned to little more than a name on the Coalition's rolls. What filled the gap came from an unlikely quarter: Svenish adventurers and war profiteers, men and women whose own homeland regarded their trade with real unease -- a bonded courtier's favor sold abroad for Commonwealth coin sits uneasily against a culture that judges standing visibly bought as standing already spoiled, and more than one such adventurer found a harder welcome at the next All-Thing than any hero's welcome the Commonwealth was prepared to offer in its place. Their aid was disproportionate to their numbers regardless: adaling-borne aerial support alongside the Commonwealth's own rotostat gunships, and skipling-eased passage lending a handful of Commonwealth hulls a sailing edge no guild yard could match, kept the western war from tipping as far as its numbers alone might have suggested it should.

What followed was a bloody grind rather than a decision. The Principalities were fighting for home and hearth now in a way the war's opening seasons had never quite demanded of them, and it showed in a resolve Lavion's own strategists had not accounted for at the outset. Lavion's war machine, meanwhile, was burning through more than bodies. Rising restriction at home -- rationing, curbed liberties, a wartime censorship that grew heavier with each season the eastern front refused to resolve -- fed a dissent that had nowhere formal to go but found a voice regardless in the Commons, that rowdy lower house born of concessions wrung from the peerage after the widespread rebellion of 1277. The Commons could sponsor no bill of its own; that privilege belonged to a sitting peer or a member of the Privy Council alone. But a credible faction of dissenters, rocking a chamber that had stood unified behind imperial ambition since the war's first declaration, needed no formal power to make itself heard, and needed only one sympathetic patron among the peerage -- and Lavion's own officer corps, after two fronts' worth of doctrine tested past its comfort and found wanting, was not short of men who might, for reasons of conviction or of opportunity alike, prove willing to be that patron.

## A War Ended in Sin

### The Desolation of Santra (1572)

By the war's eleventh year, Lavion's strategists had convinced themselves of a single, decisive stroke that might yet end a conflict grinding on past all early confidence. Santra was chosen with care, and for reasons that seemed, from Virell, entirely sound: it was the seat of the Commonwealth's Parliament and the standing Synod of the Concordian Church alike, the one place in the whole princely confederation that could plausibly be called its head. A strike sufficient to end that head, the reasoning went, would end the war along with it -- a decapitation in the oldest and most brutal sense, aimed at leadership rather than at land or at line. It was, in hindsight, the single greatest misjudgment of the war, because Santra's true worth to the Commonwealth had never been what it commanded. It was what it stood for -- and no bomb ever built, however terrible, has ever succeeded in killing a symbol by killing a city.

What Lavion's suppressed lineage of natural philosophers delivered, when wartime desperation finally overrode whatever restraint had once kept their earlier work half-hidden, was a weapon beyond anything the pipe jet had prepared the world to imagine. For one unbearable moment above Santra, all six elements slipped whatever bond holds them each to the others' lawful check, and answered only to their own most extreme and unchecked expression at once: the city burned and froze together, churned and calcified together, rotted and broke apart in the same instant, each element's violence meeting no opposing element left whole enough to hold it back. The greater part of the Guard of the Common Weal, garrisoned there against the very exposure that had always made Santra Lavion's to threaten, was lost in that single moment, along with the Parliament it answered to and the Synod that had guided Concordian restraint through every crisis before this one.

The wound Lavion opened did not close. Where Reme's own cataclysm receded, over centuries, into a half-legendary founding trauma that every faith came to argue over rather than remember, Santra's remained raw and present and witnessed: a lasting aetheric singularity where the six elements have never fully resettled into their ordinary reciprocal peace, fire and ice and rot and stone still answering, diminished but unceasing, to whatever was untethered in them that day. No scholar has yet had the luxury of turning it into myth. Refugees who watched it happen, and the soldiers who watched their own Guard die in it, are still alive to say plainly what they saw.

It was, in every particular that mattered, exactly the failure Lavion's own strategists least expected. A war effort built on picking off separate princely levies until none were left standing found itself facing, in the aftermath, no separate princely levies to pick off at all. The old rallying cry of auxiliaries to the Guard of the Common Weal, already worn half-true by years of shared front lines, needed no further borrowing to become entirely true the day the Guard itself was mostly destroyed defending the one place that had always mattered more than any battlefield. What replaced every fractured appeal to province, to prince, to charter, or to coin was a single cry that needed no explanation wherever it was spoken: For Santra. Lavion had aimed to sever a head and remove a symbol. It had, instead, given the entire Commonwealth the one thing eleven years of grinding, uneven, half-coordinated war had never quite managed to forge on its own -- a single, undivided cause, and a debt that would not be considered paid until it was answered in full.

### The Eastern Rout (1572-1573)

Vel Kiriya's return to the war was not the cold recalculation of an advantage regained. It was, in the telling of its own Judges and shrine-wardens, a duty too plain to refuse -- proof delivered, in the worst way imaginable, of exactly what they had feared Lavion's whole abstraction of the elements would eventually produce. What followed was not a resumption of the old measured pressure that had once been content to pin Lavion at Lu-An and no further, but a full and righteous commitment the Lu-An littoral's thin, air-bridge-sustained garrison had no capacity to withstand. The corridor across the eastern Lupines, built to carry reinforcement east, became instead the only route by which anything at all could be carried west, and even that thinned by the season as Vel Kiriya's advance made the passes themselves a battleground rather than a rear area. Lu-Pto, Lu-An, and the whole of the Eastern Gyre littoral that Roburic II had once won at such cost passed from Imperial hands within a year of the Desolation, and Vel Kiriya's own vanguard, by 1573, stood astride the Lupine passes themselves, looking down at last on the western Circle Sea from ground no Velkirian army had held in living memory. It did not press further. Behind that new line, Vel Kiriya's administration of what it had won followed the same old pattern it had always favored over outright occupation, in the littoral as along the upper Mer and in Riv-î-Tep before it: new satrapies bound to Paxhar Vel by tribute and by rule through local hands rather than by garrison, a conquest that looked, within a season, less like an army of occupation than like the Eastern Gyre simply changing which distant court it answered to.

### The Advance for Santra (1572-1574)

In the west, a Commonwealth united by grief rather than by charter found itself, for the first time in the war, with an army large enough and coordinated enough to advance rather than merely hold. The lances and rotostat gunships built by a decade of necessity did not need to be reinvented for the offensive; they needed only a single object clear enough to aim them at, and the recovery of the eastern princedoms lost in the war's opening season -- named, mourned, and now sought back one by one -- supplied exactly that. What remained of the old Guard of the Common Weal, no longer divided between the field and a Santra that no longer needed defending, joined the advance rather than watching over an empty duty, and Lavion's aristocratic officer corps, still resisting to the last the lessons its own western front had been trying to teach it for a decade, found itself outmaneuvered by an enemy it had never stopped underestimating even while losing ground to it season after season.

### The Breaking of the Rose Throne (1573-1574)

At home, the reckoning came for the war machine before it came for the war itself, and it came from below as much as from above. The Commons had waited a decade for a cause too undeniable to hesitate over, and by the time report of the Desolation -- and, worse, the unhealed thing left in its place -- reached Virell in full, the waiting was already over everywhere else. Garrison towns and free cities along the western marches, closest to the advancing Coalition and least willing to bleed further for a war machine that had just incinerated Santra, rose against their own Imperial garrisons town by town through the winter of 1573, some expelling their governors outright and demanding a new settlement without yet declaring for anyone, a few simply ceasing to obey. Paltos alone went further than any city dared, its citizens and militia contingent throwing out the Imperial garrison in a single uprising and petitioning the Commonwealth for outright accession before the war had even formally ended -- a free city remembering, after a thousand years under Remean charter and Lavienese crown alike, the older republican instinct that had once made it worth besieging in its own right. Virell itself did not escape what the periphery had started. Disorder in the capital ran alongside the political crisis rather than following it, crowds in the streets answering the same news from the borders that was, at the very same hour, tearing the war machine apart within the Peerage and the Privy Council both.

What followed was not a trial in any formal sense, but it did not need to be: the aristocratic officer faction that had refused, for a full decade, to let a brassback or a pressganged aeronaut share its glory now found itself unable to escape sole ownership of its one true innovation, and the suppressed lineage of natural philosophers behind both the pipe jet and the Desolation alike was proscribed this time in earnest, its remaining practitioners scattered, disgraced, or worse, in a purge far more thorough than anything that had followed the pipe jet's own quieter unease. Empereur Edramar II, who had inherited momentum rather than peace and spent his whole reign adding to it, could not survive a capital in disorder and an empire coming apart at its own edges. His abdication, when it came, was less a decision than an acknowledgment of a throne he had already, in every practical sense, lost.

What replaced him was neither a restoration nor a republic, but something closer to a bargain struck in the middle of a collapse still underway. A collateral branch of House de Lavion -- distant enough from the war's authorship to be acceptable to a Commons no longer willing to be quiet, close enough to the old line to satisfy a Peerage unwilling to see the dynasty itself extinguished -- was raised to the throne, but only on terms no Lavienese sovereign had ever before accepted: a settlement, drafted in the same disordered months as the abdication itself and remembered ever after as the Compact of Virell, that gave the Commons' Chamber the right to sponsor its own legislation for the first time in the institution's history, and bound the Crown's power to declare war to the joint consent of Peerage and Commons alike. Lavion kept its rose and its throne. It did not keep the thing that throne had meant since Roburic II first wore a crown the Church had refused to bless.

### The Peace of Balboa (1574)

That the peace was signed at Balboa at all was its own kind of verdict. No city in the Circle Sea had done less fighting and shaped more of the war's outcome, and none was left, by 1574, with cleaner hands or greater standing among all three exhausted belligerents to host the terms that ended it. The Commonwealth recovered every princedom lost in the war's first punishing season, with reparation enough from a contracted, chastened Lavion to rebuild what could be rebuilt of them, and saw Paltos's own unprecedented wartime accession -- achieved by the city's own citizens and militia before any treaty existed to bless it -- formally recognized rather than reopened. More than recognized, in fact: the lands of the Duchy of Rasselune on the northern coast, Paltos's own hinterland, passed to the Commonwealth outright as a further territorial gain, and were raised together with the city into the newly acceded temporal Principality of Paltos -- the first Lavienese soil in the Commonwealth's history to become Commonwealth soil in truth, and a Coalition foothold sitting, for the first time in living memory, on the very edge of the Varsa basin that had always been Lavion's own unassailable heartland. Alongside it, the western marches of the Lupines themselves passed from Imperial to Commonwealth hands, closing the war on the ground the same way it had opened: with Lavion's own territory changing hands rather than only its neighbors'.

Vel Kiriya's gains were confirmed in the fashion the highland kingdom had always preferred to outright annexation. Its border was fixed at the Lupine passes themselves rather than pressed any further west, but the passes were claimed outright rather than merely held, and behind that line Lu-Pto and the Eastern Gyre littoral were formally recognized as satrapies of Paxhar Vel -- tributary, locally governed, and folded into the same pattern of rule through hands rather than garrisons that had always marked Vel Kiriya's holdings along the Mer. The peace also dissolved what the war had already made hollow: the Imperial protectorates Roburic II had won beyond the western Circle Sea were released outright, and Varngard and Korsova, the one worn down by a decade of partisan resistance that had never once fully surrendered the highland sky, the other never more than an occupied springboard for Lavion's own eastern wars, returned to independence for the first time since the Imperial Turn.

And Balboa, which had armed only in its own defense and broken no oath any court would test, saw its old, deliberately ambiguous Crown Vassal fealty rewritten at last into something no longer ambiguous at all: a genuine, formally recognized autonomy, extending in practice across the Ertran peninsula that Balboa's own harbor had always anchored and that Lavion's own writ, with the peace, now ceased to reach at all. The four centuries of careful hedging had, in the end, been cashed in for the one thing the Principality had never before asked for outright, because it had never before needed to.

### The World the Rose Left Behind

Lavion that emerged from the Peace of Balboa was not the empire Roburic II had built nor the one Edramar II had inherited. Its eastern holdings gone to Velkirian satrapy, its western marches and the whole of Rasselune ceded outright to a Commonwealth that now held ground within sight of the Varsa itself, its old protectorates beyond the western Circle Sea released to stand again as Varngard and Korsova rather than as conquered provinces, its sway over the Ertran peninsula ended by an autonomous Balboa's own quiet expansion into the vacancy, the Empire that Roburic II had raised from a single kingdom into a continent-spanning power was, within a decade, a kingdom again in nearly every sense but name. Its aristocratic officer corps publicly and permanently discredited, its own capital scarred by a winter of disorder it had never expected to suffer at home, the House de Lavion survived only in a collateral line, and only on terms the Compact of Virell had rewritten into permanence: a Commons that could sponsor its own laws for the first time in its existence, and a Crown that could no longer declare war without it. The Commonwealth that had entered the war a patchwork of uneven, separately-mustered princedoms left it something closer to a single people in fact if not yet fully in charter, bound by a cause -- For Santra -- that would outlive every soldier who had first spoken it in anger, and holding, in the reborn Principality of Paltos, the first piece of ground it had ever taken from Lavion rather than merely held against it. Vel Kiriya stood, for the first time in its long history, with a real and permanent presence at the Lupine passes themselves, its numbers and its clean conscience both vindicated in the eyes of its own clergy and laity alike, whatever quiet unease still lingered among the Judges who had watched invocation's scale grow past anything their own scripture had ever quite blessed. Varngard's own long, unyielding partisan war -- fought by wood-and-cloth gliders and bonded courtiers against a garrison that had never once fully pacified the highlands -- ended not in surrender but in the independence it had never stopped fighting for. And Balboa, which had spent four centuries mastering the art of never choosing, emerged the one power in the whole Circle Sea that had chosen nothing and lost nothing, its new formal autonomy, and its quiet new reach across the whole of Ertra, the plainest proof yet that the shrewdest wager in Circulean history was not yet finished paying out.

What remained at Santra itself -- the wound the war never closed, and never will -- is a matter for another telling.
