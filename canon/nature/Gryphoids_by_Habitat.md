# Gryphoids of the Circle Sea: A Survey by Habitat

What follows is a working survey of the small and middling gryphoids most familiar to city-dwellers, farmers, rivermen, and sailors of the Circle Sea, arranged by the niche each occupies rather than by formal lineage, which remains disputed among naturalists. As a rule the wings, beak, and general proportions of a gryphoid dictate the niche it fills and the scale at which it lives; a small number of lineages, noted where they occur, reach a size well beyond what such proportions alone would suggest. Of these, only the Tallowback owes its bulk to generations of selective breeding, and it alone has lost the capacity for flight as a result. The others are naturally occurring giants among their kind, and the aetheric lattice within their bones keeps every one of them a fully capable flier despite the size they have grown to.

## Eave and Gutter Dwellers

These are the gryphoids of roof-ridge, midden, and market square, small and bold around people, quick to exploit whatever a town discards.

The **Gutterjack** is a small, sooty, sharp-eyed forager, low-slung and lean, with long hooked claws made for scrabbling through refuse and a wiry, quick-turning body that lets it dart from underfoot the instant it's disturbed. It nests in eaves and roosts in loose, quarrelsome bands, descending on midden and market stall alike with equal opportunism.

The **Nick o' Silver** is somewhat larger, striking in black, white, and grey, with unusually deft, almost hand-like digits that let it work open latches, baskets, and pockets with real precision. A low, ringed tail trails behind a lithe, dark-masked frame built for raiding after dusk, and it is the gryphoid most often blamed, fairly or not, for missing trinkets.

The **Tower Rook** is a heavy-beaked, glossy black creature that favours high ledges and bell-towers, its build rangy and russet beneath the dark plumage, suited to covering ground at a steady trot between roosts as much as to soaring between them. Solitary or paired rather than flocking, it is credited with an unsettling memory for faces.

The **Market Tumbler** is the humblest and most numerous of the eave-dwellers, plump and ash-grey, soft-coated and twitchy-nosed, its whole compact body built for squeezing through the narrowest gaps in wall and stall alike wherever grain has spilled. It breeds prolifically and is tolerated more than admired.

The **Quay Skulk** haunts harbours and fish markets, a heavy-bodied, raucous, musk-scented forager unafraid to stand its ground against rivals or curious dockhands. It takes fish guts, refuse, or unguarded catch with equal enthusiasm, announcing itself well before it is seen.

## Garden, Hedge, and Canopy Dwellers

These smaller gryphoids fill the orchards, hedgerows, and woodland edges, mostly gentle in habit and prized as much for song and colour as for any practical use.

The **Courtyard Sparr** is the smallest and plainest of the hedge-dwellers, brown-flecked and quick, equally at ease foraging on bare ground or scurrying up bark, nesting under eaves and in thicket alike.

The **Sweep** is a slender, forked-tailed aerialist, lean and weasel-quick, spending most of daylight weaving low over open ground near barns and gables in pursuit of insects on the wing.

The **Humsip** is a garden curiosity small enough to be mistaken at a glance for a large insect, hovering at flowering vines on a blur of wings with a needle-beak lowered to the nectar; its round, cheek-pouched little body is built for ground-bound foraging and hoarding between bouts of flight rather than for any sustained travel.

The **Red-Throat** is a friendly, russet-breasted dooryard forager, tame enough to take crumbs from a hand, quick and small, denning overnight in a shallow burrow near whatever garden it has claimed.

The **Lamp-Mothlet** is a dusk-flying, mottled-grey hunter with enormous eyes suited to twilight, its whole soft-furred, wide-eyed frame built for a nocturnal life spent snatching moths and midges from the air before denning by day in a hollow branch.

The **Chiselbark** is a strong-clawed climber with a chisel-beak for hammering at bark in search of grubs, its bushy-tailed body equally at home ascending a trunk head-first or descending it in reverse.

The **Saptap** is a close cousin in habit, less a hammerer than a prober, twisting its head oddly to work at bark crevices; its lean, sinuous body gives it an unusually fluid climbing gait among the gryphoids.

The **Millpick** is smaller still, a hyperactive forager that travels in noisy little bands through orchards and hedgerows, its whole tiny frame built for constant, restless movement between twig and leaf-cluster rather than for any single specialised task.

The **Painted Cantor** is valued above all for its voice, a modestly plumed songster, slim and softly furred, kept and bred in aviaries across the Commonwealth for its evening performances.

The **Larderjack** is small but disproportionately vicious, a hook-billed hunter of large insects, nestlings, and the odd small vertebrate, with a nimble, long-limbed, quick-climbing agility that lets it work branch and thorn alike in pursuit of prey. It has an unsettling habit of impaling more than it can eat on thorns and barbs to save for later, and hedgerows in its territory are sometimes found decorated with such larders, a sight most farmhands learn to recognise and leave well alone.

## Woodland Floor and Game Fowl

Where the Farmstead fowl are kept and bred, these gryphoids remain wild, cryptically coloured ground-dwellers of forest floor and scrub, reluctant fliers that favour a short, explosive burst of wingbeats over sustained flight when startled.

The **Brackenfowl** is a richly patterned, ground-nesting forager of forest understorey and hedge-bottom, its plumage broken into mottled browns and coppers that all but vanish against leaf-litter, mounted on a small, slender, deer-like build suited to picking a careful path through dense cover. It bursts upward in a startling, noisy clatter of wings when flushed, covering only a short distance before dropping back into cover, and is prized by hunters more for the challenge of the flush than for any real difficulty in the shot that follows.

The **Coppertail** is a sleeker, longer-legged wild fowl of the Merhal's forest fringes, glossy and burnished about the head, with a quick, sure-footed gait built for outrunning trouble on the ground rather than taking to the air. It is the wild forebear of the Yardheeler, from which countless generations of domestication have bred the fowl's original sharpness and reserve, and something of its speed, out; wild Coppertail flocks persist in the Merhal region proper, considerably warier and lighter-bodied than their farmyard descendants, and occasional cross-breeding at the edge of settled land is a recognised, if quietly discouraged, source of hardier stock.

## Farmstead and Field Fowl

Kept and bred rather than wild, these gryphoids anchor the barnyard economy of the Circle Sea.

The **Yardheeler** is the standard dooryard fowl, plump and short-winged, its capacity for real flight bred out of it generations ago in favour of a soft-furred, thoroughly docile frame suited to rapid breeding in close confinement. It is the most economically important gryphoid in the Circle Sea, kept everywhere for meat and egg alike.

The **Tallowback** is the great exception among the farmstead gryphoids: bred across generations for size and bulk, it has grown to the scale of a proper stock animal, a heavy-necked, honking, long-bodied creature far too weighty to lift itself off the ground under its own power. Farmers value it precisely for this bulk, raising it for meat, fat, and down, and a mature Tallowback goad is a familiar, waddling, formidable sight in Circle Sea farmyards, quite capable of seeing off anything smaller than a dog.

## River, Marsh, and Wetland Dwellers

These gryphoids favour reed-bed, mudflat, and slow water, built for wading, dabbling, or diving as the terrain demands.

The **Canalet** is a slim, curve-billed wader common to canal-banks and rice-paddies, an accomplished swimmer that probes mud for grubs and shellfish and dens in bankside burrows when not in the water.

The **Paddletail** is a broad-billed dabbler at home on ponds and slow rivers, its flattened bill sifting shallow water for weed and larvae; its industrious, timber-gnawing habits make it equally comfortable ashore or afloat, and the species is credited, or blamed, for a good deal of minor flood-management along mill-races.

The **Mudmantle** is a small, quick-legged tidal-flat forager that works the waterline in tight-packed flocks, its build surprisingly stout and burrow-loving for so slight a creature; juveniles in particular look almost comically top-heavy until their wings mature fully.

The **Reed-Stalker** is a tall, patient wader, long-legged and delicately built from beak to tail, standing motionless in shallows for the killing strike at fish or frog, and is one of the more elegant sights along a quiet river-bend at dawn.

The **Tidepicker** is a slender, long-legged wader of tidal flats and lagoon-edges, working the tideline for small invertebrates on dainty, stilt-thin legs, and startles at the slightest disturbance.

The **Jeweldart** is a small, vividly coloured hunter that stakes out a single favoured perch above still or slow water, watching for fish or large aquatic insects before dropping in a near-vertical strike and returning to the same branch to feed. Loose folds of skin between its limbs let it glide silently between perches along a stretch of bank without ever needing to climb high or fly far, and a Jeweldart's chosen perch, once claimed, is defended fiercely against rivals for as long as the fishing there stays good.

## Coastal and Open-Water Dwellers

Sailors and coast-folk know these gryphoids well, some as omens, most as simple fixtures of harbour and headland.

The **Ringneck** is a long-necked diving hunter, sleek and web-limbed throughout, pursuing fish underwater with real speed before surfacing to bask with wings spread to dry.

The **Spraywing** is a slim, forked-tailed plunge-diver of harbour and open shallows, agile both in the air and beneath the surface, as likely to finish a hunt swimming as diving from above.

The **Gyre Runner** is among the great wanderers of the open Circle Sea and beyond, huge-winged and heavy-bodied, blunt-muzzled and powerfully built for swimming as much as for gliding, capable of crossing open water for days without landing and hauling out on remote rookeries for weeks at sea. Old sailors hold that sighting one far from land foretells a change in weather, for good or ill.

The **Shoalharrier** is a small, tube-nosed, compact seabird equally content skimming wave-troughs or foraging the tideline, and is considered a reliable sign of fish shoaling nearby.

The **Reef Pennant** is an aerial pirate of the reef and shoal, hook-billed and long-winged, harrying other gryphoids mid-air until they drop their catch rather than hunting fish directly itself, though it will work a tidepool when the air offers nothing.

The **Fourwing** has lost the power of flight entirely, its whole thick-coated, streamlined body compressed into a single efficient shape for a life spent mostly in cold water, its short, dense forelimbs serving only as paddles. It is confined to the coldest southern coasts and offshore rookeries.

The **Cliffscream** nests in vast, noisy colonies on sheer sea-cliffs, stout and brightly-billed, as sure-footed on bare rock as it is sure in the water when plunge-diving for small fish; colony sites are jealously guarded fishing rights along several stretches of coast.

The **Giant Fishtail**, also known by its Ertran name the **Pescoda**, is the great exception of the open water, a naturally massive, hook-taloned fishing-hawk of a creature that dwarfs any ordinary aerial gryphoid. Its hindquarters alone among the gryphoids has given itself over entirely to a life in the water: the hind legs are lost altogether, replaced by a broad, powerful, horizontally-set fluke in the manner of a great sea-beast, driving the animal through open water with a strength no true hind-limbed swimmer could match. Its bones nonetheless carry the same aetheric lattice found across the great gryphoids, and despite its bulk and its wholly fish-like rear half it remains a fully capable flier, launching from the surface to patrol open water from above before folding wings back and diving talons-first onto prey. It takes tuna and marlin as staple fare, and is bold enough to prey on seals, walruses, and dolphins besides; a nesting pair will sometimes hunt in coordinated tandem, harrying and exhausting even a small whale between them until it can be brought down. It is endemic to the Ertran Peninsula and serves as the heraldic beast of Balboa, though its size and hunting habits have made it a readily recognised sight, and a properly feared one, across the wider Circle Sea.

## Grassland and Open-Country Dwellers

Away from water, these gryphoids range the plains, steppe, and cultivated flatlands of the Circle Sea's interior.

The **Sun-Pennant** is a showy, long-tailed, long-legged ground-dweller kept ornamentally as often as it is found wild, trailing an extravagant plumed display in courtship and capable of a swift sprint across open ground when the display proves more hazard than help.

The **Windrunner** has abandoned flight for outright speed, tall and long-necked, its forewings reduced to organs of balance and display rather than lift; its long-legged, endurance-built frame can outrun nearly anything else on the open steppe, and herders prize the species' wariness as an early warning of approaching predators.

The **Theronga** is the great exception of open country, a naturally towering creature matching the largest plains browsers in scale, its long neck lifted to a height no ordinary gryphoid could reach. The same aetheric lattice that lets its greater cousins take wing keeps a Theronga capable of flight despite its bulk, though it spends most of its time grounded, and its long beak and neck serve two quite different purposes there. Much of its day is passed browsing treetop foliage no other ground animal can reach, but it is no pure grazer: it stalks small and middling prey across open ground with a slow, deliberate, almost mechanical gait, closing the distance in patient silence before the head darts down in a single swift strike, snatching prey too quick or too wary for most other ground hunters to approach. A mature Theronga's silhouette against open grassland is unmistakable from a considerable distance, whether browsing the canopy or stalking low across the plain.

## Apex Aerial Predators

The largest and most formidable gryphoids, these dominate the skies above mountain, steppe, and forest alike, and several appear disproportionately often in heraldry and folklore.

The **Shepherd's Kite** is a swift, agile hunter of open pasture and hedgerow, lean and quick from head to tail, taking small prey in stooping dives, and is sometimes kept, hooded and jessed, by those who can afford the training.

The **Demigryph** is built purely for speed, the fastest of the gryphoids in level pursuit, lightly built yet deep-chested and long-legged, bred for explosive acceleration across open plains and scrubland where nothing outruns it in a straight chase.

The **Bonehook** is a broad-winged, bald-headed scavenger that soars for hours over open country on the scent of carrion, descending in noisy, squabbling numbers once a carcass is found; its powerful, bone-crushing jaws finish what a lesser scavenger could not manage, and few gryphoids are as thorough at clearing a kill site.

The **Utar** favours dense forest and broken ground over open sky, hunting as much on foot as on the wing in coordinated packs, its build suited to short, controlled bursts of flight between cover and to long, relentless pursuit on the ground, with a killing strike delivered only once the chase is run down. Lone Utar are considerably less dangerous than a hunting band.

The **Royal Griffin** is the largest and most storied of all gryphoids, golden-hued and hook-beaked, its whole frame naturally scaled to match the greatest plains predators, so that it dwarfs every other creature of its kind. The aetheric lattice within its bones keeps it a fully capable flier despite this size, though it favours high thermals and mountain ranges where the air itself does more of the work, and on the ground its sheer size and power give it a dominance no purely aerial hunter could match. It is this rare combination of majesty and dominance, on wing and on land alike, that has made it the enduring symbol of sovereignty across Circle Sea heraldry.

The **Frostmane** is the Bonehook's cold-country cousin, ranging the high, forested ranges of the north, huge and dark-plumed, naturally massive on the scale of the largest northern beasts and, like its fellow giants, kept fully capable of flight by the lattice strength of its bones. It is a patient, powerful hunter of large prey rather than a scavenger, and one of the few gryphoids capable of bringing down quarry many times what its size would first suggest.

## Nocturnal Hunters

Distinct from the twilight insectivores of hedge and garden, this gryphoid hunts full dark for warm-blooded prey, silent on the wing and forbidding in aspect.

The **Nightmane** is a broad-headed, densely-plumed hunter of forest and scrubland, taking rodents, roosting gryphoids, and other small prey in utter silence on the wing. Thick tufts of feather rise from its brow and sweep back along either cheek in a striking, mane-like fringe, giving it a face that reads as bearded or sideburned at a glance despite being feathered through and through. It hunts by sound as much as sight, turning its whole head with unnerving precision toward the faintest rustle below, and its call, a low, carrying note repeated at long intervals, is widely held across the Circle Sea's rural districts to be an ill omen when heard too close to a house.
