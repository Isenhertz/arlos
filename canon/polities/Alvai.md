## Definition and Character

The Alvai are a long-lived, non-human people found across the Circle Sea basin and beyond, each of their major Kin intimately bound to one of the great Animal Spirits. The bond is at once sacred and irreversible: a Kin's animalistic features -- furred limbs, tufted ears, slit or dish-black eyes, claws proper to their patron -- are not mere biology, but a living inheritance from the Spirit that claimed them.

In general terms the Alvai run tall and lithe, though the spectrum across Kin is wide: a Starkin adult might reasonably be measured at a foot and a half-pole tall and be deemed average, whereas the tallest of Sunkin warriors who ever lived, Majira the Sundered, reportedly stood a full pole tall at rest, towering over all her contemporaries like a solitary mountain over the lowlands.

Common to all the Kin (as they call themselves) are animalistic features, differing by patron, but all are furred from the waist down. Body hair generally tends to come in tufts along spine or forearms -- pale, blond, and silvery tones in Moonkin, coppery gold and reds in Sunkin, dark blues and blacks as well as greys in Starkin -- and their hair can in some become a mane marrying the shoulders to the head; facial hair, too, is common. Claws and feet differ by patron: the Starkin bear sharp digitigrade talons with a raised heelspike, the Sunkin, also digitigrade, have padded feline hindpaws, and the Kin of the Moon instead have fully planted, though furred and visibly clawed, feet.

A trait shared by all Kin is the nature of their voice, described as both sonorous and ethereal -- a fact owed to their naturally occurring bitonality, akin to what in humankind is approximated in techniques like throat singing. Alvai speak with two independent tones at once, which features heavily in the dialects of their native language, Ngerhemäir, whose syllables carry a high and a low tone that differentiate a word's meaning. In human tongues that lack such tones, the effect can alternately be enchanting or haunting, depending entirely on which tone the Alvai chooses to give their words.

## The Moonkin

"Moonkin" is a cognomen of the Alvai associated with Lumal-Nâri the Wolf Mother, said by them to be the patron of white Aural -- Lur's Mirror, the moon that sits resplendent in the night next to patient green Belana, the Old Ambler, and the ember pinprick of the Reckoner, Langea. Like all Alvai, the Kin of Lumal-Nâri are marked by their furred abdomen and legs, though in a stark difference from other Circulean Kin the Moonkin have fully planted, though clawed, feet. This has enabled them to use sandals and soft shoes, which in turn contributed to their assimilation into the many societies of mankind after the Scattering, the diaspora following the destruction of their ancestral homeland in the Wolves' Teeth Mountains through a series of Lurist crusades during the Age of Enlightenment.

Before the Scattering, the Moonkin of the Wolves' Teeth lived as highland confederates, each holding a cirque or a string of hanging valleys in the Lupine ranges. Oath and feud defined their politics; guest-right, wergild, and ring gifts held them together. Their planted gait and furred legs made them sure on scree and ice. Arms were fashioned of bronze and seasoned wood: long knives, hook spears, round shields faced in rawhide, and javelins flung from the hand or with thong-loops. War was taken in the old manner of wolves -- prowl, feint, and break -- and the howl-horn and the drum called kin across the ridges. Thus lived the Kin of the Moon for ages untold in the prehistory of the world.

When the western outposts of ancient Reme pushed south toward the high country after their subjugation of the Merhal and their expansion into the lands adjoining the Western Gyre, they found not a virgin frontier to be surveyed, but opposition that struck by night. Early charges against Shaped legion lines were ruinous for the Moonkin, who soon learned to decline the open field. Under warqueen Baddhiga, raised from the warrior caste by council and deed, the clans turned to a fighting grammar of ambuscade and attrition, cutting roads, harrying foragers, burning wagons and bridges, and striking camps at moonrise only to vanish before dawn. Baddhiga's rule and leadership enabled the Moonkin to push back against an enemy that had bested all human opposition, such that after decades of bruising marches and skirmish loss, the Remeans abandoned their plans for the highland passes and, remembering their thalassocratic ideals, fortified the coastline further north, paying for passage rather than bleeding for it.

The Moonkin reputation was stamped in those campaigns and endured thereafter, a wild strength, honed against empire, that carried their name into the lowlands and kept their homeland free until the crusades of a later age unmade it.

The Scattering itself belongs to the long shadow of the Theocracy's crusades into the Wolves' Teeth, when Lurist hosts drove the highland bands from hearth and necropole. In later liturgy these campaigns were framed as purifications; in Moonkin memory they remain a winter without end.

Urban life altered Moonkin ways more than conquest ever could. The clans were broken on the frontier, but bands of kinship re-cohered in towns as houses of work and fellowship, taking shape around a master craft or a scholar's stipend rather than a chieftain's fire. Longevity leaned the Moonkin toward the slow excellences -- fine joinery, gold- and silverwork, conservation of manuscripts, the patient arts of dye and inlay -- where decades accrue great skill as surely as they do modest wealth. In riverine capitals and port cities, nascent guilds offered a new choreography to replace the old; oaths to ward, master, and work-bench softened the memory of oaths to hill and hearth and rewarded steadiness and quiet skill, the very currency the Alvai carry in abundance when the world allows it.

Their household grammar follows the needs of diaspora. Children are often fostered out across allied houses to spread risk; apprenticeships run long and are paid back with a share of future fees rather than coin alone; widows and the orphaned are seldom left to stand alone, but folded in as a matter of course. The old highland habit of night watches survives as a civic courtesy in their communities -- a quiet circuit of doors that wins them small credit with wardens.

Relations with Lurist polities have been uneven, and memory makes them more so. In the strict heartlands, a Moonkin body still reads too easily as an old affront, especially where sermons recall the first centuries in a key of zeal. Yet the modern temper of the Third Hierophancy prefers ordered towns to triumphant persecutions, and where charter, guild, and parish pull in one direction, coexistence becomes the dull habit of a decent city. In the Concordian West this civic mood was long ago made doctrine, and there a Moonkin's good standing is seldom subject to polemic. Even so, suspicion resurges with every scandal, and it will be the visible Alvai who are made to answer first for the faults of their wilder kin.

Many Moonkin keep an old Ngerhemäir name for family rite and a plain name for the world. The ear tufts that betray them to any practiced eye are groomed as carefully as their beards. Tailoring de-emphasizes the furred abdomen without pretending it does not exist. This desire to avoid giving offense means that across the centuries, only a few names rise from the footnotes -- Moonkin secretaries who outlived patrons and preserved a polity, treasurers who taught a court the virtue of exactness. For every such figure there are hundreds for whom the record is merely a rent roll, a clerical record of birth, a guild mark on a lintel. This, more than any single law, defines the Moonkin condition after the Scattering: to live within other peoples' institutions without surrendering the few old habits that they carried with them into the world.

### The Svartalfr

Some Moonkin, however, never left the old cold. The Svartalfr, the black-haired kin of Lumal-Nâri living in the far southern Lupines of Svenu, kept holding on to ancestral ways even as the world to windward forgot them. Trade brings them down with bone and horn inlays, plaits, smoked meat, and the rare and skillfully wrought axe or blade, for they learned the coveted secrets of steel before the lowlanders did; but ordinarily they can only be found well beyond the last plowed field or pasture. To a city-born Moonkin they are kin and strangers both, as opaque and enviable in their self-possession as any cloistered order. Though they share a common language and ancient rites with their kin, many Svartalfr no longer identify as part of the broader Moonkin diaspora, seeing themselves instead as keepers of a purer memory untouched by the compromises of exile. Of their traditions little is known outside their snowy keeps, but travellers from Svenu, where they are highly respected, speak of the custom of the "axe-brides," female warriors foreswearing family and clan to focus their long lives on the mastery of the whirling war-hatchets or cleaving pole-axes.

## The Starkin

The Alvai that call themselves the Kin of the Stars are a people of Brother Bat, whom they name Ghâl-Vekhu. Since time immemorial they dwell in An-Suur, where heat, shade, and water trade places by the hour -- jungles that steam at noon, mangrove coasts whose tides help fish climb trunks like monkeys. Long before the Prophet's moment of clarity bound the quarrelsome Anish cities into a single theocracy, the Starkin shared this land with them and kept to the deep green the Anish shunned. They are the shortest and lithest of the Kin: eighty-five fingers can be called average height; a Sunkin beside them might stand a head and a half taller. Their feet are raptorial, with the weight carried on fore-toes and a raised heelspike for grip on bark and root; their ears are large and mobile; and their eyes are black-glossed dishes that drink the night. Where a Moonkin's planted sole was meant for scree and sleet and now takes to cobble and stair, Starkin talons are for bough and buttress-root, and their poise is the quiet of hunters by habit and intent.

A Starkin's presence is a study in economy. The stance is forward on the toes; the heelspike barely kisses the earth until it is needed. The ears, ever moving, work much like hands do in the speech of a Balboan or a Fiuriscian, such that whole sentences can be changed by the ears tilting just so; in conversation with outsiders they go still so as not to give offense, if they aren't used to hold a second conversation with nearby Kin the outsider is not privy to. Fur is dark, sparse on the upper body and thicker along the abdomen and thighs; arm tufts are slight, and the hands are deft rather than heavy. Night-eyes show a dome of black that catches every glint of lamp-light; in bright noon many wear shade veils or brimmed hats that let them look without squinting. The whole posture reads as quiet intent, and the wise learn to treat that quiet not as humility but as readiness.

In the old rivers and along the long coasts, Starkin bands moved with the seasons and the fruiting of trees, fishing creeks in the rains and trading bush-meat and resin at the dry-season fringes. Their record-keeping was, and remains, the charge of memory-keepers who recite their epics whole. The coming of early Lurist zeal -- first the consolidation under the Prophet and the Judges, then the Theocracy's expansion -- pressed them further under the canopy, and the Kin followed the same grammar that old enemies had taught the Moonkin deep in the south: prowl, feint, and break, and vanish into green shadow to strike anew from another angle.

What the Theocracy called pacification, the Starkin remember as the Green War, a long uncounted season of raids, razed clearings, and disappeared patrols. From that fight rose Ejitwe the Thronebreaker, prince and partisan, who gathered exiles and oath-bands and led them not to conquest but to unmaking: the sack of An-Mirda in 336 AR, forty days of siege followed by flame and flight. For the faith, especially the popular imagination, he is the face of vile heathenry victorious; for his people he is a liberator and a caution both -- the last Batkin to take righteous vengeance openly and prevail, and proof that even a just victory can still leave a people without a heartland. When warrior bands name a fierce youth "child of Ejitwe" because they hear that old fire in the voice, the elders caution that the same flame that cleanses old blood feuds will also feed on new shoots: invoke his fire when you must, but remember that the outside world sees not the liberator, only the conflagration.

Starkin speech is sparse; ears that swivel, however, make for a quick and vivid sign-language of posture, finger-taps, and ear-tilts, such that a camp can pass a day in a hush. But at night the same band will find song in a call-and-response that binds memory to meter so a single keeper's recitation can braid the names of travels, kin bands, feuds, and treaties without a chisel to hand. Their houses are where the food is, lean-tos and canopy-tents, easily hidden and as easily abandoned. A band's anchor is not timber but the circuit of favored shallows and fruit stands, a web of kin oaths and guest-rights with neighboring bands. Starkin life is more caravan than village, and yet thick with belonging.

Most misunderstood of their rites is the Dreamsong, a feast for Brother Bat. Offerings of blood stand at its center -- beast most often, and by consent on rare occasions the blood of kin -- infused with the fruits and shrooms that wake the mind to color and pattern. The band drinks until the pulse of drum and voice lifts the camp into a single listening. What follows is a communal trance in which visions rise and repeat across many minds at once, a memory shared rather than proclaimed. Outsiders, hearing only of blood and sleep, made of it dark cannibalism; the Starkin hold it as a pledge of care, a way to carry the elders' seeing into the bodies of the young so they might remember as one.

The Theocracy is gone, but the Third Hierophancy, for all its modern restraint, still keeps its old monastery enclaves across An-Suur, and those, in turn, keep old and zealous habits. The Starkin have not forgotten. They refuse proselytization, and while bands that came out to the fringes mostly keep their knives sheathed and sell their knowledge as wardens and guides, others never came out at all. Smugglers and relic-hunters speak in low voices of the Deep Clans -- the bands that stayed in the jungle after the Theocratic collapse and still hold the old vendettas, reading the outer world as infection to be cauterized, not bargained with.

In recent decades, a younger generation has tested the tree line. Some Starkin live now at the jungle's edge in market hamlets and river-towns; others hire as pilots and wardens for scholars who can be taught to listen to the green. It is a cautious commerce, and even those that trade readily with any that would call them heathens are not pacified -- only at rest. A wrong word, a step too far, and the forest will close around the offender as surely as water closes around a stone.

The Starkin have centuries of cause to mistrust the Light's emissaries, and the living memories to know better than to be reconciled by sermons. That they continue to succeed with this refusal is not for chance but for structure: Starkin bands are small, kin-stitched, and perpetually in motion; their law lives in memory-keepers and in oaths that travel with them. Food, water, and medicine are inside the canopy, and the canopy itself is a fortress and a shelter. A missionary cannot simply enter a part of the jungle as one enters a village; he must be received to be heard. The bands simply choose not to receive any. In such a society, proselytization has no lever: promises of courts, poorhouses, and written surety mean little where justice is settled by elders who can recite three generations of debt and guest-right from memory, and where hunger is solved by feet and ear and net rather than by alms.

## The Sunkin

The Lion-Blood Alvai of Lu-An are the Kin of the Sun Lion Rasha-Tarô, a proud and perilous people, and among the most storied warrior bands of Arlos. Native to the sun-blasted and gold-grassed savannahs of Lu-An -- the Lonely Plains, in their own kenning -- they are one of the few Alvai peoples never brought under heel by the Remeans, the Theocracy of Eternal Dominion, or any later polities, not by open conquest nor quiet efforts of conversion. Unlike the scattered and assimilated Moonkin or the still jungle-woven Starkin of An-Suur, the Sunkin present themselves without apology. Their kinship with the Spirit of the Sun Lion, a beast of fire, pride, and glory, is read in leonine traits and in bearing alike: manes of ruddy gold or copper on men and women both, fierce amber eyes that do not dim with age, digitigrade legs and claws on hands and feet that come out at a moment's notice. The mark of their lineage is evident in their demeanor as much as their anatomy: regal, radiant, communal, and intense enough to feel like standing too long in direct sun; at once awe-inspiring, exhausting, impossible to ignore.

In their belief the Sun Lion is First Among Many of the Animal Spirits, and his children are avatars not as solitary champions but as prides. This semi-sacred bond-group -- equal parts family, household, and battlefield formation -- forms the basis of their society. Every Lion-Blood is born to a natal pride and is expected, at maturity, to enter or form an adult pride through oath, ordeal, and affinity. They share food, territory, offspring, craft, and risk; they deliberate collectively; they act as economic cooperatives; and honor, shame, and legal standing are the pride's before they attach to any single member.

Because the pride bond is central, Sunkin intimacy is unabashedly polyamorous in rite and practice. The forms vary from layered pairings to celibate warrior companies, generational kin blended with oath-companions. Childrearing is communal; biological parentage is secondary to the pride bond. Among these Alvai, romantic love is praised but not idealized in singularity -- passion burns best in a hearth, not a matchstick, as their proverb has it. These customs sit at sharp angles to Lurist orthodoxy, which reads monogamy as a social more, demands sacramental legitimation of children and traceable lineage, and treats polyamory as indulgence at odds with proper Right Appetites. For the Sunkin, Lurist monogamy is brittle, possessive, and unnatural -- a flame that burns for one thing alone is but a mere candle, and they are the sun.

At home, vast clan-holds ring the high savannah oases, and the lion-spirit that is their totem is honored at sunrise, midday, and sunset through offerings of oil and smoke, chants, and ritual combat to first blood. Gold is sacred as the sun made solid, and their heraldry is circular and radiant -- sun-disks crowned with a lion's face, sigils set as mane-flames or paw-suns, cloaks in burnt ochre, gold, and crimson. They do not follow Lur and speak of the Light as pale beside their blaze; the Hierophancy's cautions, to them, are the talk of shadow-dwellers that fear noon.

Many prides range outward from Lu-An. In Lavion, the satrapies of the Merhal, and even the Principalities of the Commonwealth, one meets their mercenary companies who hire only as intact prides and refuse to be broken into scattered retainers. They do not disdain coin, for gold is sacral, but they serve only those deemed worthy of the Lion's gaze -- not necessarily the richest, but the boldest, the most radiant in purpose. Weak-willed nobles can bid tenfold and be refused where a fierce rebel matron secures their claws for fame alone. To be guarded by a pride in the present age is therefore not a mere token of wealth; it advertises a will that is not ashamed to shine.

The intersection of pride and family does bring tensions, internal and external. A small enough birth-pride may dissolve when youths come of age; adults join or form functional prides according to temperament, skill, and desire rather than blood. Siblings commonly live in different prides; children are as readily fostered across oaths as across kin. Friction arises when blood and pride pull at odds, and such cases are a matter for the Dawnbinders, the elders and shamans. Ritual life hews to the embodied and the evident: "The Sun does not argue. It rises above."

The fiercest pressure, however, comes from outside, when authorities attempt to impose family forms for taxation, inheritance, or moral policing. Where prides settle in jurisdictions that will not recognize their bonds, they adapt by fiction and craft: pride-mates register as wards and retainers of a nominal couple; polyamorous households pass as extended kin with cousins and servants; extended pride-groups style themselves as chartered companies or partnerships to preserve legal personality.

A lion is not born alone; a lion belongs to the pride.

## The Fallen Kin

The reign of Emôn-Dju-Bâ-Mhâhat-Nmu the First-and-Only was marked by its brutal expansionist agenda. To fuel the First-and-Only's daily life-prolonging blood rituals, ever more sacrifices had to be procured and funnelled towards the monstrous stepped ziggurat at Riv-î-Tep, the heart of the realm.

To this end, he enslaved three Kins -- that of Night Jaguar, Iron Bear, and Grandfather Marrow -- to serve him as obedient tools filled with a cold, dark hatred for his enemies and disdain for their own lives. These unrelenting waves of pillaging marauders became the epitome of terror in the east.

To the Alvai, these bâlghubäerhe -- a phrase from Ngerhemäir that translates literally as the "lost-darkness-kin-souls" -- represent Kin whose patrons were forcibly twisted until the Spirits began to rejoice in atrocity, causing their Kin to follow in a feedback loop of cruelty. The Alvai phrase became the source of the word "bugbear" used in Virellic parlance to refer to something dangerous or monstrous hiding in the dark.

### Hatekin

Night Jaguar was one of the great hunting patrons of the jungle margin, bound up with ambush, righteous terror, and the thin line between guardian and devourer. In Alvai telling, Night Jaguar walked ahead of the firelight, teeth wet, watching the perimeter. It was the patron that kept things out as much as it brought death in -- frightening, yes, but not unnatural.

Its Kin were an Alvai people of the heavy forest and the broken river country in the north and east of Riv-î-Tep, and much like the western Batkin, they favoured short, brutal engagements, fought by scent and silence rather than ranks. In the old stories they cleansed bad places so that others could live within sight of the jungle. Physically they shared the Sunkin's feline traits, but turned to colors of shade and night.

Though the usurpation of the first Sorcerer-King by the second ended his reign of terror, the corruption of the Jaguar's kindred was irreversible. Loyal as they are to the dead spectre of their lord and master, their dispersed, disparate clans have become eponymous with acts of unflinching, bitter loathing towards the people of the Circle Sea, the "Westerlings" they were created to raid, torture, and abduct.

### Wrathkin

The Iron Bear was once a guardian of kin and hearth -- not literally made of metal, but emblematic of patient strength, endurance, and winter sleep, a patron of those who hold the line by standing on it and refusing to budge. In old Alvai stories Iron Bear is stubborn, slow to anger, and terrifying when properly provoked.

Its Kin were a highland and escarpment folk of Sarm, broad-built and lumbering, contrasting the more lithe Kin of the lowlands. Bearkin kept watch over their cliff villages, and served as caravan wardens along the Salt Road connecting the Merhal to An-Suur along the coast of Sarm. Their rage was famously rare and just as famously decisive.

Under the dominion of the First-and-Only, that strength was inverted into wrath unmoored from duty. Iron Bear was not broken quite as cleanly as Night Jaguar; instead, the rites of shaping and coercion leaned into one particular aspect of the patron: the moment when patience finally snaps. Compulsion looped satiation of rage to abject obedience; their wrath, once a simmering thing, now became their foremost trait.

Wrathkin were thus made of the same family of sins as the Hatekin. Where the latter were numerous and disciplined enough to make for excellent if sadistic armies, Wrathkin were singular and unorganized berserkers, flung at enemy lines to punch holes where normal infantry would break. They were also used in internal discipline: parading a berserker chain-gang through a restive vassal city just the once made people very eager to be seen paying their taxes on time.

### Slothkin

Bonehooks are the carrion eaters and bone-crackers that wheel above the battlefield. Grandfather Marrow, the Spirit representing them all, was a psychopomp to the Alvai, and presided over the honest work of breaking down what had fallen, sorting what goes to soil and what goes to story. In Alvai cosmology, he knew which souls need lifting and which bodies could be safely given to the worms.

Its Kin were an Alvai people of the Ptolean border deserts, living at the edge of settled land where graves are shallow and scavengers are busy. In prehistoric times they served as death-guides, embalmers, and bone-readers. Their rites ensured that corpses were properly dealt with and the dead were sent to where they needed to go.

The First-and-Only in his ego saw this knack for the dead and dying only in terms of what he might gain from it. Throughout his reign he fashioned the malignant infrastructure of his realm out of the living bodies of conquered peoples, turning them into horrors of resonant anatomy: bone-flutes, rib-chimes, blood-choirs, and worse, all keyed to specific harmonics to support, enhance, or otherwise further his grand works. Not trusting his subjects with the maintenance of his bloody designs, he bound the Spirit and twisted it until he no longer wished to guide souls but instead eagerly prolonged the flesh, dragging souls back and pinning them inside, stitching, splinting, keeping all those pitiable horrors that should have been allowed to die trapped in their own, mutilated bodies.

Its Kin, under that yoke, became the wardens and orderlies of the sorcerer-king's flesh-engines, a perverse inversion of psychopompy in which the people who once prevented abominations became essential to keeping abominations functional.
