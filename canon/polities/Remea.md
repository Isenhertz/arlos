# The Remean Empire

This entry gathers what can currently be reconstructed of the Remean Empire, the thalassocratic civilization destroyed in the year counted as 0 AR. No primary Remean text survives in unmediated form; the Hierophancy's suppression of Remean doctrine and political theory in the two centuries following the Fall was thorough enough that everything below is reconstruction -- drawn from hostile Lurist historiography read against its own bias, surviving foreign archives, comparative philology, and the physical record of Balboa, the one city understood to preserve genuine Remean building practice into the present day. Where the reconstruction is uncertain or contested, this is noted rather than smoothed over, since the uncertainty is itself part of what a lost civilization means in practice.

## Naming

The empire remembered in Lurist scripture and chronicle as "Reme" appears in no surviving source under that name, because no Remean source survives under any name. Inscriptional fragments recovered from Balboan foundations -- reused Remean spolia, older than the buildings built over them -- point to a proper form closer to *Remea*, the mother-city herself, with the empire understood as an extension of the city rather than a separate polity requiring its own name. "Reme" is very likely a Lurist mangling of this, whether an honest phonetic shortening by generations of crusaders and chroniclers who never heard the name spoken correctly, or a deliberate diminishment, clipping a proper name down to something that could be spat rather than intoned. Both processes would produce much the same result after four centuries of hostile retelling, and there is no way at present to tell which occurred.

## Governance: the Capitolinae and the Collegium

Lurist sources describe Remean governance only to condemn it, which makes them a poor source for institutional detail. What can be reconstructed from the discipline of the legions, scattered administrative fragments, and the layout of Balboa's oldest quarters points to two distinct governing bodies, kept deliberately separate from one another.

The Capitolinae were a magistral class functioning much as a senate: a public office exercising ordinary governance over law, tribute, fleets, and the ordering of the city's outward affairs. Whether the office was hereditary within fixed houses, filled by rotating election, or something else entirely is not recoverable from the current record. What is clear is that the Capitolinae governed the whole -- the polity as such -- and that a single Capitolina personally undersigned every charter issued in their name, whether in Remea itself or in a directly governed periphery polity. This makes charter authority personal rather than abstract: a Capitolina had a real, finite capacity for how many charters they could oversee at once, which is a plausible source of the political friction -- overcommitment, rivalry over valuable quotas, disputed succession when a Capitolina died mid-term -- that any large and long-lived magistracy would generate.

Separate from the Capitolinae, and not subordinate to them, stood the Collegium: the body of those trained in Shaping. Their charge was the citizen, not the polity -- the guidance of individuals toward the shared civic ideal, and the tuning of vocation to aptitude that let, for instance, a porter carry four filled amphorae on a single yoke without strain. The Collegium held no formal share of the Capitolinae's civic power, and this appears to reflect a considered principle rather than mere custom: that the shaping of a citizen and the shaping of the state should never rest in the same hands. Whether this separation was law, taboo, or simply prudence hardened by long practice cannot currently be determined, but it may explain why Reme's fall is remembered as a single catastrophic event rather than a slow institutional decline -- if the Collegium's ambitions and the Capitolinae's wars were genuinely separate enterprises, a magistracy in reasonably sound order could have been undone in a single afternoon by its own artisans of the flesh.

## The Somatic Art

The practice the Collegium administered is poorly served by calling it "magic," and only partially served by comparing it to the aetheric engineering of the present day, though the two are understood to share a common root. Shaping was, as far as current evidence allows, a wholly inward art. Where later hexcraft and aetheric engineering apply the same underlying principles outward -- to pressure vessels, resonant cavities, the Motility-relations of alumen -- Shaping worked only ever on the living human body.

Shaping appears to have rested on the same underlying triad now taught to any apprentice artificer -- structure, resonance, materiality -- but with the body standing in for all three terms at once: its structure the thing reordered, its own living resonance the pattern tuned, its flesh and bone the materiality worked upon. This is very likely why Shaping never generalized outward the way aetheric engineering eventually did. A resonant cavity in a wind-chest is indifferent to who built it or who uses it; a Shaping artifact was not. It functioned as a personal implement, keyed through some process of sustained attunement to a single bearer, and passing it to another hand required that hand to invest its own long discipline before the artifact would answer at all. A comb that reshaped and held a bearer's hair to their liking was not a styling tool in the modern sense but a small, continuously renewed act, its harmonic pattern re-struck with each use, and it would have done nothing at all, or something unpredictable, in an untrained stranger's hand.

The recorded range of effects runs from the cosmetic to the constitutive. At the lighter end: hair length, texture, and color held or altered at will; the smoothing of scars; the setting and knitting of broken bone faster than any splint could manage. At the more significant end: measurable enhancement of strength, endurance, coordination, and sensory acuity, and -- the effect hardest to credit and hardest to dismiss, given how consistently it recurs across independent hostile sources -- some form of shared cognitive timing among Shaped individuals acting together. This last is the likeliest explanation for the uncanny synchrony foreign chroniclers attributed to Remean legions on the march and in the line: not drill alone, however rigorous, but soldiers whose Shaping had tuned their reflexive timing toward a common pattern, so that a formation moved less like a group of trained individuals and more like a single body with many limbs.

Most consequential of all was the ritualized Shaping undergone at the threshold of adulthood: Harmonization, a working sustained over months or years that carried a citizen's inherited form toward ambigeny -- females Shaped toward the male and males toward the female -- until the two converged closely enough that no citizen who had completed the rite, and so stood properly Harmonized, retained a functionally pure female or male body within the settled core of the empire. This was not a single artifact's working but a whole curriculum, and it marked the passage into citizenship in something like the way any coming-of-age rite marks it elsewhere -- except that here the rite altered the body it initiated, permanently or nearly so, rather than merely declaring a change already assumed to have occurred.

### The Iconoclasm

Lurist reaction to this, once the Communion's early indifference curdled into hostility, was violent in a specifically targeted way rather than incidentally so. As the Theocracy of Eternal Dominion advanced in the wars that followed Judge Kharun's death, its soldiers and preachers are attested -- in fragments that survive precisely because they were kept as trophies or warnings -- as making a particular point of defacing Remean statuary and mosaic, with the gynoid ideal and any blurring of gendered form drawing the worst of the damage. Surviving sculpture from the period shows a consistent pattern of disfigurement concentrated on the face, chest, and hands of figures embodying the ideal, rather than the more random breakage ordinary looting or war damage would produce.

The theological reasoning behind this, reconstructed from Antidiscordian polemic of the following centuries, held that the Remean body -- elegant, feminine-leaning, functionally perfected -- was not an instance of harmony at all but its opposite: hubris presenting itself as concord. Orthodox and especially Antidiscordian doctrine of the age extended the Triad beyond the individual into an ontological order of the sexes and generations -- man to govern and act, woman to nurture and tend, the child to grow and obey -- each fixed irrevocably in place as part of a divinely apportioned structure. A people able to dissolve those categories at will, treating maleness and femaleness as a singularity from which all life sprang rather than a boundary to be kept, were understood not merely as strange but as assaulting the order the Light itself was held to have set in the world. Suppression, on this reading, was close to the point of the war rather than incidental to it.

The formal ban followed rather than waited for the war's end. Ecclesiastical decree under the young Theocracy prohibited the manufacture, possession, or use of any device made to alter the body's given form, and the Collegium's methods passed from foreign curiosity to forbidden art within a generation of the Fall. No verified practitioner is known today; surviving artifacts are, without exception, inert, of uncertain function, or dangerous to an untrained hand attempting to reawaken them.

The Somatic Art was almost certainly not the whole of Remean aetheric practice, whatever impression the sheer thoroughness of its suppression has since left. The empire that mastered the inward working of the human body to a degree no culture before or since has equalled in all likelihood also practiced a form of outward aetheric modulation that a modern artificer would find remarkably legible as ancestral to present-day hexcraft: a manipulation of the cosmos's sixfold elemental symmetry through the same underlying recognition that its vertices, and so the whole, answer to structure, resonance, and materiality. Almost nothing of this outward tradition survives even as fragment, and it left far less trace in hostile Lurist memory than Shaping did, very likely because it never provoked the specific theological alarm that a fluid, Shaped body did. But the triad at its root is the same one the Collegium turned inward, merely pointed the other way, and it is this shared grammar, rather than mere coincidence of form, that most plausibly accounts for the family resemblance between a citizen's personal focus-comb and a modern resonant cavity.

## The Legion at Its Height

The clearest surviving demonstration of Shaping's civic application, and the clearest outside description of a Remean legion in the field, comes from the account of Vizier Aset-na-Mer, servant of Thesun-Khetep, describing the Remean conquest of the Merhal a full millennium before the Fall. The account survives because it was preserved in the Merhal's own archive rather than in any Remean or Lurist one, and so escaped both the Sack and the later suppression. Nearly every detail recorded there as marvel or horror corresponds to a specific, traceable Shaping.

The record describes a host that read as entirely female, advancing in ranks so exact that "the wind itself kept time for them," never needing a shouted order. A reconstructed Shaping curriculum, pieced together from fragmentary instructional texts evidently written for the guidance of Collegium juniors, shows this was engineering rather than metaphor, applied in cumulative passes across a citizen's whole career. An early civic pass, laid down in a youth cohort before formal enlistment, tuned muscle fibre toward endurance, stabilized the joints, sharpened proprioception, and narrowed reaction time, alongside issuing a first personal focus-piece -- typically a wrist-cuff or gorget -- keyed to the cohort's shared cadence. This is very likely the mechanism behind the reported silent command: legionaries were not watching for a shout, because their nervous systems had been tuned from adolescence to anticipate the pulsed signals their focus-pieces carried, letting a whole cohort pivot as one on uneven ground because each soldier's sense of timing had been aligned to a common underlying pattern rather than merely drilled to react to the same order.

Formal enlistment triggered a deeper rewrite: the skeleton thickened to bear armour and pack weight, tendon and fascia realigned to store and release energy efficiently under load, and cognitive timing tuned specifically to formation drill, so that the body was already moving before a signal had fully registered. Later campaign-specific overlays -- a pattern recorded for service in the Merhal's floodplains adjusted sweat chemistry, gut flora, foot structure, and breathing rhythm to the hazards of heat, mud, and insects -- sat atop this base rather than replacing it, which is likely why a Remean soldier could be redeployed across very different terrain within a single career without years of reconditioning.

Two pieces of Remean equipment attested in the record -- an over-long spear comparable to what later ages call the sarissa, and a belly-drawn bow of unusual power -- remain essentially unchanged across the full breadth of the dateable Remean record, a millennium of continuous service with no meaningful evolution in either design. In most military traditions, a weapon this demanding of its user is abandoned within a generation or forces the surrounding tactical system to evolve around its limitations. Set beside the Shaping curriculum, the likely resolution is that the limitation such weapons ordinarily impose was never permitted to arise in the first place. Using an over-long spear in formation, rather than merely carrying it, requires a rank of soldiers able to maintain exact spacing, synchronized footing, and disciplined pivoting under the stress of an advancing line -- precisely the layer of cognitive timing and proprioceptive tuning built into every legionary at enlistment. Where an unshaped army must drill for years toward this coordination and can still fall short of it under pressure, the Remean legion issued its soldiers a nervous system already tuned to the requirement, so the weapon never needed to change because the failure mode it would otherwise have forced a redesign around -- a single mistimed step collapsing the formation -- had been engineered out of the soldier rather than the spear. The belly-bow answers the same puzzle from the other side: a draw of that power ordinarily demands either mechanical winding apparatus or an unrepeatable feat of individual strength, and either constraint would elsewhere have driven centuries of engineering toward reducing the draw or mechanizing the load. Reme needed neither, because a draw that struck an outside observer as uncanny was simply within the ordinary strength of a Shaped archer's tuned musculature. In both cases the true site of Remean military innovation was the body rather than the armoury, and a body, once correctly Shaped, does not need replacing every generation the way a design does.

The description of shields "faced in bronze, rubbed with gums against the humidity," and of armour that "shone with cast bronze muscle," points to a deliberate stratification of the legion's material culture. Rank and file wore laminate armour -- layered wood, hide, and packed linen bonded under Remean alchemical glues and resins into a single stiffened shell, a technology the empire's mastery of gums and solvents made viable centuries before comparable lamination appears anywhere else in the material record. Properly sealed against river damp and coastal salt alike, a laminate cuirass or shield-face could be produced in quantity, at a fraction of the weight of solid bronze, and repaired in the field by any competent artisan with the right resin rather than a bronzesmith's forge -- a significant logistical advantage for an army fighting as far from home waters as the Merhal. The bronze muscle cuirass, cast and chased to render an idealized anatomy in metal, belonged largely to officers, and is best read less as superior protection -- laminate construction appears to have offered comparable defense at far lower weight -- than as deliberate civic display: an outward image, cast in metal, of the very ideal the Shaping beneath it had already worked into the flesh.

## The Chartered Economy

Remean commerce did not resemble a free market in the sense later ages would recognize. The empire operated on a principle closer to command logistics than exchange: it determined what it needed, where, and when, and paid or compelled the relevant parties to see that it arrived on schedule, formalized through charters personally undersigned by a Capitolina. This preference for chartered conveyance over open trade follows naturally from the empire's basic shape -- a synoecism of merged core cities surrounded by a looser periphery of colonies, allies, and tributaries cannot rely on the informal trust networks a single sovereign state might extend to its own provinces, so guaranteed and scheduled throughput had to be formalized as a binding instrument rather than assumed as custom.

The conquest of the Merhal illustrates the underlying logic well: the floodplain functioned as Reme's breadbasket, and its acquisition secured a scheduled grain supply that voluntary trade with an independent Merhal could not have guaranteed. The black volcanic sands of Ertra and the coasts of Lu-An and Lu-Pto across the Eastern Gyre held a comparable position for iron, and control of that supply was very likely among Reme's earliest and most consequential conquests, since a synoecium built around Shaped citizen-soldiers and precision metalwork could not risk its iron supply to an unreliable outside partner.

Exports ran in the opposite direction along the same charters, and were not purely material. Alongside worked bronze, brass, iron, and the clear resins used to imitate gemstones, Reme exported Shaping and alchemical knowledge, its political theory (understood by outsiders as heavily biased toward a Shaped citizenry, since it largely was), the imposition of order against local or outside potentates, and -- in a category as consequential as any of these -- infrastructure and expertise. Much as a comparable land empire in our own history is remembered for its roads, the Remeans are understood to have built ports, dams, and lighthouses throughout their periphery, and it was very likely this reciprocal investment, more than raw coercion, that kept a distant holding like the Merhal pacified for the better part of a thousand years: an empire that only extracted grain and iron without returning anything of comparable value would be expected to generate rebellion faster than compliance, and the record instead shows the Merhal remaining a Remean holding up to the era of the Long War.

Currency followed the same administrative logic as everything else. Reme's system of account was sexagesimal, denominating charters as binding instruments -- closer to a bill of lading or promissory ledger-entry than to coin meant to circulate anonymously in a marketplace -- with the physical record most likely closer to an early paper-currency equivalent than to minted metal. This explains both the thinness of the surviving coin record, since a chartered economy has little need to mint for daily anonymous exchange, and the unusual completeness of the economic erasure that followed the Fall: the ledgers were the empire's real monetary infrastructure, and ledgers burn far more easily than a hoard of coins. The sexagesimal system itself proved to be Reme's most durable legacy by far. It survives, filtered and partly disguised, in the dozenal notation used throughout the Lurist creed today -- a system later chroniclers describe as "cleft from the sexagesimal order of the Empire yet compatible enough to be easily adopted," since twelve divides sixty cleanly enough that the young Theocracy could keep the arithmetic that made commerce function while discarding the number's Remean origin. In this respect Remean numeracy has outlived Remea far more thoroughly than any single building or artifact, in much the way older systems of counting time and angle have outlived the civilizations that first fixed them.

## Harmonization: Citizenship for Adults

Citizenship by birth entailed the standard childhood Shaping curriculum, undergone while the body was still developing and its bone, tendon, and nervous system still plastic enough to carry a full and lasting Harmonization toward ambigeny. Citizenship could also be granted to adults, most often as an incentive extended to tributary or provincial laborers well outside the empire's settled core, but the underlying difficulty of working on a body that has already finished growing made this a genuinely fraught process rather than a simple upgrade, and the empire seems to have treated it as such.

The standard adult track, administered not by the metropolitan colleges of Remea but by field shapers stationed directly in periphery territories, typically extended no more than a single working, or at most a small handful, to any one candidate -- a modest investment set against the years of sustained, iterative attention a childhood Harmonization received. The result was usually a real and valued improvement in health, function, and appearance, but rarely full ambigeny: most naturalized adult citizens remained visibly, permanently distinguishable from the native-born, carrying legal citizenship without ever being fully Harmonized the way someone Shaped from childhood was. This visible incompleteness functioned as a durable social marker in its own right, roughly analogous to an accent that legal status alone cannot erase, and it appears to have been an accepted and unremarkable feature of the system rather than a failure of it.

Full Harmonization in an adult was achievable, but only as an exceptional case, reserved for a candidate judged worth a Shaper's sustained personal attention over a period of years rather than a single session -- a defector of unusual value, a client ruler being fully absorbed as a reward for loyalty, or an individual the Collegium itself took a particular interest in. Because this level of investment could only plausibly be authorized and funded through the same chartering mechanism that governed the rest of the economy, a full adult conversion was in effect a Capitolina's charter as much as a Shaper's working, and would have been legible to everyone around the candidate as a conspicuous, expensive act of patronage before the process had even finished.

The reason most conversions stopped short of this level was not merely economic. The risk to the candidate was real on three separate fronts at once. Medically, an adult body's habitual processes -- reproductive function above all, since this was among the very first systems addressed in a childhood curriculum and consequently among the hardest to safely redirect once fully set by adulthood -- carried a genuine chance of complication or lasting harm when re-channeled this late. Psychologically, identity is bound up closely enough with one's own body that even a successful, medically clean partial conversion could be disorienting or destabilizing, a cost the Collegium appears to have expected and managed rather than treated as a rare complication. And economically, a full and clean induction demanded the kind of sustained, personal, years-long attention from a trained Shaper that was, of every resource the empire commanded, the scarcest, which is very likely the deciding constraint on how many conversions could ever be authorized at the higher standard, regardless of how many candidates might otherwise have wanted or deserved one.

## The Long War

The Communion of the Enlightened's early spread was not, in its first century, a war with Reme. The Prophet's revelation finds Reme already ancient, a thalassocratic power of perhaps a millennium's standing, settled deep into its habits, its coasts, and its indifference to hinterlands and wandering ascetics alike. The early Judges preached freely along trade routes that were, more often than not, Remean routes, and for a century or more the empire appears to have regarded the Communion as beneath serious notice -- one more coastal cult among the many its cosmopolitan ports had always tolerated.

That indifference ends with the death of Judge Saint Illut-Kharun of the Salt Road, under a tribunal convened by Remean provincial officials. Why a scribe-turned-pilgrim, preaching mutual aid among scattered congregations, drew the attention of magistrates who had ignored the Prophet himself is not recorded in any surviving source. The most plausible reading is that Kharun's organizing of scattered congregations into something resembling a body with cross-border loyalties looked, to a provincial administrator, uncomfortably like the beginnings of a rival civic order within Remean territory -- precisely the kind of thing a synoecism held together by compact rather than conquest could not afford to tolerate at its edges.

Whatever the precise provocation, the martyrdom converts a diffuse devotional movement into a persecuted and radicalizing one, and the following century and a half sees the conflict escalate steadily from suppression to open war. Judge Nallume the Binder leads besieging fleets against the Remean provincial capital of Paltos; Judge Mazuren the Patient wears down Remean outposts in the west through long attrition. By the time Saint Virellian compiles the Book of Judges and the faithful elect Maerel the Undimmed as First Hierophant, Reme has spent a century losing provinces and outposts to an enemy that has, in the same period, gone from scattered preachers to a standing crusading order with a permanent hierarch at its head. The empire that once ignored the Communion as beneath notice now faces, for the first time, a unified war machine it cannot simply outlast.

## The Fall

The First Crusade is remembered in every surviving Lurist source as an unambiguous triumph, the empire's climactic destruction at the hands of Phlogistophor read ever after as the Light's own verdict on Remean arrogance. This is very probably true in outcome and mistaken in mechanism, for a reason the Lurist record itself never noticed: no crusading fleet was present to see it happen.

The likeliest reconstruction, built from current-pattern studies of the Western Gyre and the otherwise unexplained fact that the First Crusade made landfall far south of Remea's home waters, runs as follows. Facing a war it had been losing for a century and could no longer outlast, the Collegium reached for an instrument that had never been tried: not the guidance of an already-compliant, already-Shaped citizenry, but the binding of a Noble Element itself, Phlogistophor, as a weapon against the approaching crusading host, as a last resort against a specific and immediate military threat rather than an act of unprompted ambition. It was a reasonable extrapolation of a mastered art, and also a category error. Centuries of Shaping had been practiced on flesh already made harmonious, willing, and tuned; an element has no such prior inclination toward accord. It was never leashed at all. The binding failed at the moment of attempt, and whatever was released was sufficient to annihilate Remea outright and, in the same event, scramble the currents of the Western Gyre badly enough that the crusading fleet -- already en route, expecting a siege -- was carried far south of its intended landfall and never saw the city it believed itself sent to destroy.

Two consequences follow from this. First, the Theocracy's founding martial myth is very probably wrong about its own victory: the First Crusade did not defeat Reme in any battle it actually fought, and its chroniclers recorded an eyewitness triumph they did not, in fact, witness. Second, Remea's death was not a siege with survivors streaming into the record as refugees and daughter colonies bearing testimony -- it was a single catastrophic event with no crusading witness and, so far as the fragmentary record shows, remarkably few Remean witnesses who lived to describe it either. This is the likely seed of Remea's peculiar historiographic silence: not only that the Hierophancy erased what it found afterward, but that there was, from the very first year, unusually little left to erase.

## What Survived, and Where

The formal suppression of Remean memory was a Hierophantic project of the following two centuries, and it appears to have been selective rather than total. Doctrine and political memory were the deliberate targets: texts touching Shaping, the Capitolinae, or Remean civic theory were suppressed root and branch, and no primary text on any of these subjects is known to survive. Practical knowledge fared better, chiefly because the guilds that inherited it had no reason to ask after its provenance. The retort-distillation of zinc -- the process underlying what metallurgical guilds today simply call true brass, its actual Remean origin long since stripped from the name -- survived in exactly this fashion, passed hand to hand through provincial workshops the Sack never reached, indifferent to the fact that the hands which first refined it belonged to a Collegium whose name a smelter had no cause to know.

The clearest surviving witness is not textual but civic. Balboa, a staging harbor for the Remean fleet's operations into the Western Gyre at the time of the Fall, lay far enough down the gyre-current chain to be spared both the crusading fleet -- misdirected south by the same catastrophe -- and, by the same distance, the worst of whatever the catastrophe did to Remea's own coastline and waters. Balboa inherited Remean building practice without inheriting a corpse to build over: the packed, vertical use of space between shore and the Ertrese foothills, descended from Remean insular building, still shapes how the city occupies its ground, and a handful of its old public baths remain in functionally unaltered use, regarded by Balboans today as simply Balboan rather than as a relic of anything older. This is very likely why Balboa has never been subjected to the scrutiny leveled at any site more legible as a ruin: a living city going about its ordinary business does not read as a shrine to a suppressed empire, even when its baths and its close-packed streets are exactly that.

## The Present State of the Question

Investigation into Reme's history currently enjoys a cautious toleration from the Autocephalous Church of Ertra, under whose license the relevant archaeological work is presently conducted -- a toleration those doing the work are careful not to test. What can currently be published tends toward the technical and comparative: metallurgy, hydraulic engineering, the philology of place-names, the reading of Balboan foundations as a layered record. What cannot yet be published, and may not be publishable for some time, is any reconstruction of Remean religious or civic thought that would require restoring, even provisionally, the doctrine the Sack was expressly designed to erase. Progress on the Remean question therefore proceeds largely by inference from silence, by reading hostile sources against their evident bias, and by the patient work of asking what a smelter's furnace or a harbor's foundations can reveal that no surviving book is presently permitted to say outright.
