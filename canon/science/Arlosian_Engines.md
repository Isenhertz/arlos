# On the Engines of Arlos

## The Shape of the Problem

The history of the Arlosian engine is the history of a civilization denied the two easy roads. Elsewhere one might imagine industry raised on the detonation of prepared mixtures, or on great boilers of driven vapor; on Arlos both roads are closed, and closed by the world itself rather than by any failure of wit. An explosive mixture is, to the sprites of Fire, an invitation impossible to decline, and no charge of that kind has ever been kept long enough or safely enough to serve as a tool. Vapor is little better: the sprites of Water and Air that play prettily in the plume above a kettle become genuine hazards at any scale worth an engineer's attention, and a boiler large enough to drive a mill is a boiler large enough to gather company. Religious sentiment, which reads the deliberate raising of great vapors as a provocation verging on discourtesy, finished what practical hazard began, and steam was confined for good to the kitchen and the bath-house.

What remained was heat itself, and the oldest observation of the smithy and the organ-loft alike: that heat set against a contained column of air will, under the right geometry, begin to sing. The whole lineage of Arlosian engine-craft descends from that single fact -- that a temperature held across a tube can be made to sustain a standing tone, and that a standing tone in contained air is work, if only one can teach it to push.

## The Bellowing Tubes

The first machines to do so at civic scale were anything but musical. The open-tube charging stations of the late Age of Corsairs -- the "bellowing tubes" of period complaint -- were crude arrangements of fire-throat and resonant bore, roaring day and night to drive the compressors that fed a city's pneumatic mains. They were inefficient, deafening, and indispensable. Their waste heat, vaulted and channeled, warmed whole districts; their voice, carrying over rooftops in all weathers, became the acknowledged sound of a town that had joined the age of contained air. A mature Concordian city of the early thirteenth century ran its forges, workshops, hearth-bellows, and tube-mail from mains those tubes kept charged, and the portable brass bottle, rechargeable at public points, carried the same power into the street.

It is worth insisting on what the bellowing tube was not. It was not yet an engine in the later sense, for it drove nothing directly; it made pressure, and pressure did the work elsewhere. The division of labor that would mark the whole Aetheric Age -- the burner and the bore at the district's heart, the clean expansion motor at the point of use -- was already present here in embryo, awaiting only the theory and the materials to perfect it.

## From Alchemy to the Choir

Both arrived within a generation of one another. Around 1225 AR a scholar of the Grand Academy of the Broken Isles demonstrated the compression-expansion work cycle in full: mechanical work and a cold outlet drawn from compressed air expanded through a pliant-membrane engine, a boiler-less prime mover that slotted directly into the existing mains and marked the true inflection from crude open-tube practice toward disciplined aetheric engineering. Some fifteen years later the Law of Harmonic Conversion was set down -- that heat may always be turned into organised resonant or pneumatic motion, but never wholly, its yield bounded by the ratio of temperatures at a device's hot and cold ends -- and engine-craft acquired the limit that has governed it ever since, and the incentive that follows from any limit honestly stated: if the ratio bounds the yield, then the hot throat must burn hotter, the cold end run colder, and the geometry between them waste nothing.

Answering that incentive remade the machine. The alchemical pliants -- membranes and seals of graded compliance, tuned in stiffness as a string is tuned in tension -- allowed bores to be coupled, staged, and packed together without the leakage and rattle that had made the old tubes so profligate. Aetheric modulation, applied with a light hand at the resonant joints, let a working tone be held stable against the drift of load and weather. The result, maturing through the first half of the fourteenth century, was the stacked engine: banks of tuned pipes and tubes sounding together in deliberate harmony, each rank reinforcing the next. The name arrived before the theory was even finished. No one who stood in a hall of such machines, their ranked voices rising and falling in ordered chord as the load shifted, needed to be told why they were called choir engines.

The formalisation of self-harmonising resonance design in the mid-fourteenth century completed the type, and thermoacoustic choirs became, in the chronicler's phrase, the heartbeat of industry. Ironically, the perfection of the form soon quieted it: closed-vessel variants, their working gas sealed for good behind pliant and brass and their tone confined with it, displaced the open choirs in most heavy service before the century was out, being more efficient, more compact, and blessedly less loud. The name outlived the sound. Every sealed engine of the modern age, humming behind its lagging with scarcely a murmur, is still a choir engine, and the muted chord it carries within it can still be heard by anyone who lays a hand on the casing.

## The Fuel of the Choir

A choir engine is indifferent in principle to what heats its throat, and the great stationary engines of the mains burned whatever the district's fuel-yard held, charcoal above all. But the engine's spread beyond the district heart was decided by a different substance. Turpentine -- distilled in quantity from the resin-trades of the great forests, with Varngard's woods foremost among the sources -- proved the near-perfect engine fuel: liquid, dense in heat, clean in a properly made burner, poured and stored and carried as no solid fuel can be. Around it grew a family of refined fuels and resin-oils, graded and blended for burner and season. The formal registers still call them spirits -- spirits of turpentine, rectified burning-spirits -- but common speech declined to let one word carry drink, the manifold spirits of the living world, and burner-fuel besides, and clipped the last of these to sprit. The ledger and the alchemical treatise keep the long form; the wharf, the fuel-yard, and the engine room have said sprit for generations, untroubled by the spar that shares the name, and every sailor knows without thinking which sprit is meant by where the word is spoken. The consequence deserves emphasis: the choir engine remained bound, like every furnace of Arlos, to the arithmetic of the woodlot, for its fuel renews at a tree's pace and no burning stone or earth-oil has ever been found to break the bond. The engine did not free industry from the forest. It made the forest's produce portable, and portability was revolution enough.

The same trades supplied the engine's subtler appetite. Varnite, the fossil resin of the Gulf shore, worked for centuries as mere ornament, was found by Aetheric Age artificers to hold resonant properties of direct worth to choir-engine and resonance-craft work, and passed in a generation from jewelry to strategic material.

## To the Water

The stationary choir engine took the place a heavier history might have given to great vapor-engines, and for a time that was place enough: mills, foundries, mains, and the public power of cities. But an engine burning a liquid fuel and carrying no boiler wants, by its whole nature, to travel. Through the late fourteenth and early fifteenth centuries the type shrank to fit the hull. Motor launches came first, harbor craft whose sealed engines and bottled fuel made them tireless where oars and sail were not; the paddlewheeler followed, and became the riverine and maritime standard of the Circle Sea, the wheel's steady beat mated to the engine's steady chord on every great river and along every coasting route. Overhead, the same engines drove the pontoon airships -- the aeronaves -- that linked the major ports, the logic of the air following that of the sea.

## The Small Engine and the Imperial Turn

The last transformation was one of scale, and it was bought with alchemy and aetherics as much as with honest engineering. Pliants of ever finer grade, resonant elements cut from varnite and its refined kin, modulation applied not at the joints of a great machine but through the whole small body of a miniature one: by the closing decades of the fifteenth century these together yielded choir engines of human scale and below, sealed hearts no larger than a strongbox, then a loaf, then a fist. The aetheric furnace's gift of alumen arrived in the same years to lighten bottle and frame alike, and the two developments completed one another.

What the small engine enabled marks the turn of the age. The pneumatic prosthetics that had entered workshops and wards from the 1450s, fed until then from bottles and public points, could now carry their own power, and the powered limb passed from clinical curiosity to working fact. And the same fist-sized heart, set in a frame of brass and braided pliant and governed by the new fluidic logic, made possible the human-scale pneumatic constructs -- aethermatons in the academy's language, brassbacks in everyone else's -- that entered docks, yards, and policing in the early Imperial Age. Every brassback that hauls, digs, and stands its silent watch does so around a choir engine, and the faint chord under its chest-plate is the same tone, four centuries refined, that once bellowed over the rooftops of the corsair towns.

## A Necessary Postscript

One further descendant of the engine-craft of this age cannot go unmentioned, though its full account belongs elsewhere. The pipe jet, the reaction engine of Lavion's aeronavalliers, shares the choir engine's ancestry in contained air and burning spirit but none of its settled conscience: what its intake does to the small elemental presences of the open sky has been documented, suppressed, and disputed in equal measure, and the question sits under the Imperial age's aeronautics like a fault line under a city. It is treated in its proper place; it is named here only so that the record of the engine's triumphs is not mistaken for the whole of the record.
