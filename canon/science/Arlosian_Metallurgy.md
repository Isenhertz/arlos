# On the Metals of Arlos

## The Shape of the Problem

Arlosian metallurgy is best understood as a long negotiation with two scarcities at once: a scarcity of iron in the earth, and a scarcity of fire hot enough and cheap enough to master what iron there is. Neither constraint alone would have set the Circulean world on the path it took. Together they produced a civilization whose engineering matured to the very threshold of modernity on copper, tin, and zinc -- an age of bronze that never ended, but instead deepened, refined itself, and finally transfigured.

The scarcity of ore is plain: workable iron occurs in quantity in only a handful of places known to Circulean commerce, such as the black volcanic sands of Ertra's coasts and where they land across the Eastern Gyre on the shores of Lu-An and Lu-Pto, and the bog iron of Varngard's marsh country, drawn from the soil itself and renewed by the slow chemistry of its waters. Everything else is incident and exception -- a mountain lode here, a streak of ore there, enough to make a man rich but not sufficient to found an industry upon. The consequences run through the whole political history of the Circle Sea. Reme's early conquest of the Eastern Gyre sands, Varngard's centuries of favorable trade, and Vel Kiriya's war aims in the late Imperial Age are all, at root, the same fact wearing different centuries' clothes. Indeed much of the earliest history of alchemy and elemental bargaining alike is filled with people seeking ways to find, transmute, or otherwise gain acces to that metal.

The scarcity of fire, however, is a subtler matter. The furnaces of Arlos burn what its forests give: charcoal above all, supplemented by turpentine, pine resin, pitch, and the other combustible saps and gums of the timber trade. These are excellent fuels -- clean, controllable, and well understood -- but they are fuel sources that renew themselves only at a tree's pace, and so bind every furnace to a forest. Therefore a great smelting district always also needs a great woodland under management, and the size of a realm's metal industry is bounded not by its ore but by its acreage of exploitable woods. No fuel has ever been found in Arlos that breaks this bond; the earth of the Circulean world yields no burning stone or pitch-like oil, and having no reason to imagine such a thing, its inhabitants have never marked these absences. What they have remarked on, at length and for centuries, is the arithmetic of the woodlot, which sits beneath every treatise on smelting like a foundation stone.

Between them, these two scarcities fixed iron's place in Arlosian life. Iron and steel could be made, and were, from antiquity onward; they could simply never be made cheaply or in quantity. Iron was the metal of edges, springs, and instruments -- of things whose function no other material could serve -- and it was priced accordingly. It was never, at any point in history, a metal to waste.

## The Long Mastery of Bronze

What iron could not be, bronze became. The copper and tin trades are older than reliable memory, and the alloy they yield rewards exactly the virtues Arlosian metallurgy was forced to cultivate: it melts at temperatures a charcoal furnace reaches without strain, casts cleanly and repeatably, resists the salt air of a maritime civilization far better than iron ever could, and forgives reworking, for a flawed casting returns to the crucible and is poured again, wasting fuel but never metal.

The Remean Empire raised this mastery to its first summit. Remean foundries cast bronze with a precision and at a scale that the succeeding Dark Ages could only marvel at, and the surviving material record -- fittings, statuary, the fixtures of drowned harborworks -- shows an empire that thought in bronze as naturally as later ages would think in brass. Yet even Reme, commanding the iron sands of two coasts, stratified its use of metal with visible care. The rank and file of its legions wore laminate, not metal; bronze armor was the mark of officers, and its purpose was display as much as protection. Iron went only where nothing else would serve, or where the show of it would make the most difference.

Alongside bronze, Reme worked a second alloy whose full secret it carried into its grave. Brass of a common sort -- copper cemented with zinc earths -- was known widely and made everywhere. But the retort-distillation of zinc, the delicate art of driving the metal over as vapor and condensing it pure, was a Remean process, and it was lost at the Fall with so much else. Its recovery in the second century AR from the vaguely transmitted knowledge of scattered provincial workshops indifferent to its provenance, stands among the most consequential technical events of the Age of Enlightenment: purified zinc yielded true brass, an alloy of even and dependable grade, and it is on true brass that everything distinctive in later Circulean engineering was built.

## The Civilization of the Drawn Tube

The importance of true brass to the modern Circulean world is difficult to overstate, because the modern Circulean world is, at its mechanical heart, a civilization of contained air. From the organ-builders' wind-chests that fathered the first breath-arms among the corsairs, through the graded pneumatic mains of the cities of the Aetheric Age, to the choir engines whose thermoacoustic pulse became the heartbeat of industry, every stage of that development rests on the same material foundation: an alloy that can be cast true, bored fine, drawn into seamless tube, and trusted to hold pressure through years of service in damp sea air. Bronze is too dear in tin and too coarse in the boring for such work at scale; iron rusts from within and cannot be spared besides. True brass alone answered, and the tube-drawers' and instrument-borers' guilds that grew up around it became the custodians of their civilization's central technology.

This is the proper sense in which Arloscan be said to have carried its bronze age into industrial times. The phrase misleads if taken to mean stagnation, as the metallurgy of the Aetheric and Imperial Ages was not primitive; it was copper-alloy engineering of extraordinary maturity, solving with brass, bronze, and cunning the problems other conceivable histories might have solved with cheap steel, and solving many of them -- pressure vessels, precision bores, corrosion-proof fittings -- rather better than steel might have.

## Steel at the Margins

That steel, meanwhile, held its narrow and honored ground, more elevated still for its scarcity. The bog iron of Varngard, crossing the Southern Gulf as it has for centuries, comes into the hands of the svartalfr of Svenu -- the lone Moonkin enclave never scattered in the diaspora, whose long lives have made them smiths without rival -- and leaves them as a steel with no equal anywhere in Arlos. That steel is a blade-metal and an instrument-metal: springs for the finest mechanisms, edges for those who can pay, the small irreplaceable hearts of devices otherwise built entirely in brass. Circulean merchants credit Svenu with the finest blades in the world, and only those who deal directly in the ore know how much of that fame was mined in a Varngardi marsh.

Once unlocked from the secrets of its making, steel elsewhere served the same marginal, precious functions from lesser sources at lesser quality, the pattern everywhere the same: iron and steel as the seasoning of Arlosian engineering, its substance borne in brass and bronze.

## Alumen and the Aetheric Furnace

The first genuinely new metal in nearly two millennia arrived in the fifteenth century AR, when aetheric furnaces first yielded alumen. The process belongs to the mature phase of aetheric engineering: not combustion but a sustained working driven through a molten bath, winning from certain common earths a metal no fire alone had ever loosened from them. Alumen is light beyond any prior experience of metal, and its first great consequence was military -- a compressed-air bottle light enough to carry several without straining a soldier's back, completing the breath-arm's long passage from duelling curiosity to infantry weapon. But it also saw much use in the nascent aeronaval industries, where every ounce of weight spared meant less load on the gas envelope and fewer loadbearing aetheric calibrations of Attraction.

However, alumen's second great consequence was political. The aetheric kilns that win alumen are costly, few, and demanding of the rarest calibration expertise, and Lavion's early lead in building them hardened into an effective stranglehold. Through the Imperial Age and into the Great War, alumen at scale meant Lavienese alumen, and every rival power's war planning bent around that fact. The Commonwealth and Vel Kiriya fought the war's opening years with barely any alumen to spare for the men in the trenches, and it was precisely this bottleneck, as much as any property of the metal itself, that turned Commonwealth attention toward a stubborn earth its smelting literature had been complaining about for two hundred years.

## Piatrium

The smelters of the black sands had always known their ore carried a second burden. A fraction of the sand resisted every furnace, fouled the bloom, thickened the slag, and made Eastern Gyre iron dearer than its grade alone explained; the treatises of the Aetheric Age's scientific flowering named it an "uniron", characterized it as slag, a metal-bearing earth that no art then known could reduce. There the matter rested, a common scholarly fact of no practical consequence, for decades.

Practical consequence arrived by way of the Rim of Fire. The tropical waters there had taught the United Chartered Companies, at ruinous cost in rotted fittings and eaten hulls, to prize salt-resistance above nearly every other material virtue, and during the two decades of undeclared war Company money began quietly funneling into inquiries into the light earth whose refined specks and laboratory grains shrugged off brine like only brass would. The outbreak of formal hostilities in 1561 first paused that inquiry, then transformed it: under the war administration of Parliament, specialists drawn from every relevant guild were convened in Barvella, a municipal principality whose long history of armory work -- it stands on record as the finest source of arcannon outside the Academy yards -- made it the natural seat for work demanding furnace discipline, pressure-craft, and discretion in equal measure. The Barvella Project ranged across the whole field of war-material inquiry, though most of its lines of work belong to other accounts than this one.

Its metallurgical line succeeded in 1571. The Barvella process, sibling to the alumen furnace but of a distinct lineage, drives a sustained aetheric working through a bath of molten salts in which the stubborn earth itself is seated, stripping it of its burden directly and yielding the metal in a porous sponge rather than a melt. The sponge is consolidated by heat and the hammer, in sealed vessels and under jealous exclusion of open air, for the hot metal ruins itself on contact with the atmosphere -- a forge-discipline wholly new in its particulars and yet, in its reliance on the hammer over the crucible, oddly ancestral in feel. The metal it yields is pale, light, uncannily strong, and indifferent to salt, rust, and rot alike; and the guildsmen who first worked it, needing a name for a substance whose obstinacy in the furnace and constancy once won seemed to speak for themselves, called it piatrium, after the Noble Element of Stone.

Word of the breakthrough is understood to have reached Santra in the months before the Desolation, and to have burned with the city. What survived was Barvella itself: the charter, the guilds, and the process. In the fevered patriotism of the war's final years, piatrium went to the field not by orderly procurement but by the improvised channels of individual armories supplying the lances they knew -- experimental exosuits, bottle sets, rotostrat frames, no two batches to a common pattern, in numbers too small to weigh on any battle. The war ended before the metal could matter to it, but its qualities made it matter all the more in the peace that followed.

The Project passed into the Barvella Consortium, a standing joint venture owned by its member guilds in common -- a legal novelty in its own right, since the process belongs to a body that exists only because its members can no longer untangle their contributions to it. Alloyed with copper into piatrite bronze, and with tin after older recipes' fashion, piatrium placed into Circulean hands, for the first time, a metal beyond bronze and brass, strong as fine steel at a fraction of the weight and yet proof against the sea that remains, at present as in prehistory, the focal point of civilisation.
