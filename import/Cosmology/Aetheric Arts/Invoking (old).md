Invoking, in Arlosian usage, is the deliberate calling for aid to the [[Noble Element|Noble Elements]] and their manifold expressions by name, sign, or sacrifice. Where aetheric engineers speak of modulating relations between Fire and Air in pipes and lattices, invokers instead speak to Fire and Air as persons: sprites in the walls, courts in the deep, and the Noble Six whose moods shape weather and landscape. It is an art of speech, posture, and offering rather than of levers and calculus, and at its heart lies the old conviction, so often proven true, that the world listens.

In the broadest sense, the muttered everyday plea to a sputtering candle not to go out and the chanted hymn to Phlogistophor with offerings fresh on the altar are part of the same spectrum. At the gentlest end, there is the give and take practiced since time immemorial, the gentle coaxing of sprites that arise wherever elements are already in flux: inviting emberlings to bask on the hearth, letting dewlasses and furrow‑folk settle in the fields, distracting rimekin from the door by drawing on the windows, beseeching cobolts to stop haunting the mines. Folk traditions and fairytales are both ripe with wise words about how to appease, assuage, coax, or chide such beings, and of the petty disasters that follow when they are slighted. At the harsher end lies the high coercion of legend, people aspiring to compel and command the elemental courts, if not the Noble Six themselves. Between these poles lies most of human practice, a thousand small courtesies observed between hearth, hedge, and harbour.
## Early animism and the household rites

Before the rise of Lurism, and far beyond its reach even now, the Circulean world was thick with animist cults for whom the elements were the first society. In such traditions, the Noble Six are not diagrams on an academic hexagon, but persons and kin. [[Svenu]] preserves this most clearly: there the sea is Sjarmodr, the Sea‑Mother; the storm is Styrisysdr, Sister Storm; the cold is Daudisamma, Granny Ice‑Death; the hearth is Arnvordr, the Watchful Hearthkeeper; Soil is Sprotafadr, Sprout‑Father; and the trace of Stone in iron is Jarnibrodr, Iron Brother. Shrines in that land are less temples than conversations with a landscape that answers, raised where the elements speak loudest -- at a cliff over the surf, in ringed stones that catch the wind, in a cave carved into everlasting ice. Their keepers arbitrate between village and nature, and their quiet authority is rarely challenged even by chieftains.

Elsewhere along the Circle Sea the names and masks differ, but the grammar of address is much the same. Rivers are mothers of cities and fathers of kings; volcanoes are the furnaces of a god's heart; the sea is both bounty‑giver and soul‑keeper. Fields are treated as altars under open sky, ploughshares bathed with milk and ash, and the first cut taken in prayer with a heel of last winter's bread. Children grow up dropping the first slice of the first loaf onto clean soil as if paying rent to an unseen landlord. The expectation is simple: show respect, keep your bargains, and the elements will keep theirs.

At this scale, invoking is less a specialised craft than a habit of life. The right words, the right token, the right restraint at the right time -- these are taken as common sense. To leave kitchen scraps for worms instead of burning them, to lay green pegs in a hedge without quite saying why, to snuff a candle with wetted fingers instead of blowing directly on the flame: each small courtesy is  invocation in action.

## High Antiquity and the sorcerer‑kings

The Rivian Epoch took these habits of address and scaled them to monstrous proportions. In the Mer‑Hal lowlands a succession of sorcerer‑kings discovered that the same resonance that stirs sprites from weather and hearth can, when amplified by choir and stone, be turned into statecraft. Their temple‑cities were engineered as vast resonance machines, marrying hydrology, acoustics, and coerced bodies. Choir pits, mile‑deep shafts lined with copper‑plated bone, and terraced step‑fields pulsed to harmonic cadence turned labour and landscape alike into instruments of rule.

Emon‑Dju‑Ba‑Mahat‑Nmu First‑and‑Only stands at the head of this tradition. Later fragments speak of his rites as horrors of song and sacrilege: lungs cut from legion of victims and cured as bladders to bottle their breath, ribs planed into flutes that sang in perpetual agony, obsidian and marble ziggurats running wet with blood. He broke and bound the spirit of the Night Jaguar and twisted its Kin into the Hatekin, living examples of how far invocation could be pushed toward outright deformation of body and soul.

Even today, a thousand cautionary tales are told about him around the Circle Sea, speaking of how the Wind herself was caged by him in a cacophonic harmony of breath, bone, and mirror. This is invocation turned into terror: a direct assault on the pillars of creation, the attempt to suborn and enslave the very nature of an element. How it worked is lost to memory, but how it ended is well-preserved in mortal consciousness, for the one he sought to keep imprisoned would in later ages be known by names such as "Chain‑Breaker" and "the Unfettered Gale".

Later sorcerer‑kings are smaller in scope but similar in method. Anh‑Met‑Ren Who‑Walks‑in‑Fire harnesses rivers of vitrified lava and kilns the size of districts, binding Fire to brick and glass as his mark on the land. Set‑Ukhet‑Ra Feeds‑the‑Serpents builds canals whose moving silt‑baskets are living coils, yoking Water and Soil together through serpentine intermediaries that answer only to his sigils. Ba‑Dju‑Nemu Sings‑to‑the‑Ribs‑of‑Earth hollows amphitheatres into bedrock and tunes them to the deep hum of Stone in the hope of wringing better metals from the earth. All these works are forms of invocation, in that they address elemental natures with crafted resonance and sacrifice, seeking specific favours in return. All of them cross, if not obliterate, the line into compulsion.

But even in this era, the old household courtesies continue among the populace. Fields are still blessed, rivers are still praised. The way in which the few yoke the practice to their pursuit of power is not emblematic of an irrevocable shift of how mortals interact with the world, but it outlines the gravest insults in stark lines: a lesson remembered by later ages as a tool of terror.

## Reme, the Scourge of Fire, and the Lurist turn

The rise of Reme in the wake of the failing Lesser Kings can almost be read as a rejection of invoking as statecraft. Shaping, the heptessential craft that comes to define the Empire, favours inward change over elemental negotiation, crafting bodies and tools as their primary means of power. Yet even the Remeans, at the end, turn to what the sorcerer‑kings had first dreamed: to bind a Noble Element as a beast to their yoke. The catastrophe that follows -- the Scourge of Reme, when Fire buries the city under ash and obsidian instead of marching in its defense -- becomes the hinge upon which later historiography turns.

To early Orthodox preachers, the fall of Reme is the archetype of hubris punished. The last rulers of the Empire overreached, seeking to compel what can only be addressed, and were annihilated. At the same time, the young Theocracy of Eternal Dominion is quick to claim the event as its own victory, casting Phlogistophor as an avenging minister of the Light rather than as a lord provoked to rage by both sides. In homily after homily the lesson is sharpened: to force the elements is sin; to invoke them at all is suspect.

As the Theocracy consolidates power, old elemental cults are driven underground or rebranded as parables. Dedicated household shrines dwindle; sites dedicated to river‑keepers and wood‑wardens are suddenly suspect; the Noble Six are recast as lawful but strictly subordinate ministers of the All. Invoking is officially proscribed whenever it smells of bargaining with powers outside the Light. In the crusader age, such rites are denounced as heathen superstition at best and compact with cacophony at worst.

Yet practice proves harder to purge than doctrine. In ports and farmsteads the older habits persist. Sailors departing into the Circle Sea still pour a libation to Maregina, or tie a blue cord under the rail and whisper a plea to the Lady when the churn of the Ring beats at the hull. Farmers in the Mer‑Hal and beyond still drop bread heels along field edges and keep richly tended compost heaps as if they were small, busy shrines to Kuapakalipa, Lord of Loam. No good parish priest rails too loudly against these customs if he wishes to keep his flock: he frowns at the wilder names, tolerates the tame ones, and frames what he cannot eradicate as thanksgiving to the Light for the lawful operations of its ministers.

In learned circles, a vocabulary emerges to manage the discomfort. People speak of acknowledging the Noble Elements, not of worshipping them; rites are said to honour the order of creation, not to buy favour. The distinction between appeasing and compelling becomes central: petition, if modest and framed under the creed, may be tolerated; any hint of command is condemned by reference to Reme and to the sorcerer‑kings before it.

## Kharunite panentheism and invoking as prayer

The Eastern Rite, hardened in the highlands that will become Vel Kiriya, takes a different reading. Shaped by Saint Illut‑Kharun's early efforts to gather scattered congregations into one communion, and informed by strong pre‑Lurist animist traditions, Kiriyan theology insists that the Noble Six and their courts are necessarily of the Light, potent concentrations of the sixfold harmony, not rivals to the source that generates that harmony. Venerating elemental beings is an acceptable way of venerating the Light in All rather than worshipping a false ultimate power, and Kharunite tradition sees no harm in petitioning a powerful neighbour to help in the struggles of the wider community. So the distinction between venerating a saint and addressing an elemental is blurred: a single seaside shrine in the Eastern Rite may bear the creed‑stela of Lur, a niche with a sailor-saint's effigy, and a basin into which libations to Maregina are poured. To a visitor fresh from Virell this looks like confusion if not blasphemy. To a local, it is a single act of address to the Light in its many modes.

And Kiriyan Judges and shrine‑wardens are not merely indulgent of folk practice. They are themselves proficient not just in the canon, but in the lore and grammar of invocations, and many can, when pressed, conjure and bind sprites or even more powerful elementals into orderly service. For Virellites their work sits uncomfortably close to the rites of the ancients, yet Kharunites see a form sharply distinguished from those by intent and frame: the invoker in this tradition bids the elements to join a common labour for the sake of harmony, rather than forcing tools under a single will. When Antidiscordian zealots in the west accuse them of backsliding into heathen superstition, Kiriyan preachers reply that if the Light is truly All, then to refuse to acknowledge its operations in the Noble Six is to deny the divine.

This panentheist reading makes invoking a species of prayer. A Kharunite fisherman may address Maregina before crossing a bar exactly as he calls on Saint Kharun when attempting an honest compromise; a mountain warden may speak to Morwynt, Queen of Tempests, about an oncoming storm and then end the rite with the Verses. The words differ by addressee, but the posture is the same: a mortal voice seeking harmony with a greater pattern.

## Western compromise and civic appeasement

In the autocephalous Church of the West in Stranu a more legalistic accommodation emerges. Concordian theologians remain firmly Lurist in doctrine, but treat relations with the elements more as mutable balances to be prudently managed rather than as fixed diagrams to be feared or adored. Rites of appeasement are therefore permitted under clerical guidance, codified in charters and concords, and woven into civic observance without quite the layer of unease that colours Orthodox practice.

In the Concordian context, invoking is a part of official ceremony. Harbour guilds maintain rites before dangerous passages in which priests bless beacons and hulls, beseeching the crew to Right Action under Lur while beseeching Maregina for her cooperation. Western synods still firmly reject Kharunite arguments that invoking is simply another form of prayer, wary of collapsing the distinction between Creator and creation, yet their streets and stairwells are full of plaques and practices that treat the Noble Six as partners in the maintenance of a good community.

In all of this, the sanction persists: one may ask, one may thank, one may seek to soothe. One may not command. When structural invocation is required -- the alignment of great dams, the tuning of civic compressoriums, the fire-shielding of hospitals and archives -- it is entrusted to engineers and clerics working together, couching their invocative acts in the neutral language of modulation and safety.

## Invokers and elemental patronage

By the 15th century AR, overt invocation has retreated from the centre of public power. Aetheric engineering, from its early hexcrafting roots to thermoacoustic choir engines, has become the respectable means of engaging the elements. Scalarism and the Fractalist paradigm describe materials in vectors and triads rather than in terms of gods and courts. Learned discourse now speaks of resonance patterns and regenerator cycles. Yet this change of language has not erased the older habits; it has layered new explanations atop them.

Even in this era the difference between invoking and aetheric work is less sharp than textbooks suggest. An engineer bringing a new regenerator online will softly appease Fire out of childhood habit, even while she checks gauges and calculates fuel flow. A Kiriyan judge, setting a pen to a verdict that will realign water rights, may pause to acknowledge the river whose behaviour binds the law. A Svenu captain, staring out at a grey, hard sea, will ask the Sea‑Mother to keep her temper before consulting the tide tables.

At the same time, there are modern invokers that inhabit the seams between orthodoxy and craft. Some are rural charm‑keepers and hedge‑priests who still keep the right songs for dew and frost, tending shrines to sprites at wells and crossroads. Others are Kiriyan shrine‑wardens whose training makes them valuable adjuncts to engineers, mediating between the lawful grammar of invocations and the needs of civic works. A few work under Church warrant as exorcists or purifiers, using antiphonal chants and deliberate dissonance to unvoice lingering corruptions in places scarred by earlier excess.

Alongside these recognisable roles there remains a more personal practice that could be characterized as elemental patronage. Whereas in most societies around the Circle Sea the old animist ways contended with all six courts as necessary and the Rivian blood-rites simply coerced them, the marginalized invokers of later eras mostly address a single element, as best fits their character. This is the domain of pyromancers, windsingers, wavecallers, woodwoses, frostspeakers, stonewalkers, and any myriad of other names for what is in essence a tradition of dedicated single-element invoking. In their own accounts, such people speak less of mastery than of acquaintance: a stoneshaper who has "Piatros' ear" and feels in his bones when a wall is unsound; a windrider who claims that Morwynt "knows her name" and has so far bent every gale around her boat; a woodwright who keeps an orchard so thick with life that people whisper of Kuapakalipa pausing to listen when he sings to the trees. In scripts of censure these practitioners are readily called warlocks and witches for consorting with the elementals in this manner, accused of confusing the bannermen for the liege and of seeking private advantage where only common harmony ought to be sought. All the same, both dry chronicles and fanciful fairytales continue to name people who possess some manner of connection to an element, for weal or woe, and neither can decide whether they chose their patron, or were chosen instead.

## Continuities and cautions

From animist shrines at ice‑cut cliffs to blood‑driven resonance machines, from Kiriyan basins where offerings to Maregina share space with saints' relics to quiet bread crusts laid beside hedges, invoking persists as an enduring practice of interfacing with elemental reality. What changes are the frames within which it is judged. Sorcerer‑kings make invocation the spine of their tyranny and teach later ages to fear its excess. The arrogance of Reme turns a single attempted binding into a continent‑wide warning. Lurism seeks to subordinate invoking to a higher harmony, sometimes brutally, sometimes with patient accommodation. The Kiriyan Rite rehabilitates it as a form of prayer; the Concordian West domesticates it into civic ritual; modern engineers tuck it into the interstices of their craft.

But throughout all these shifts, three facts endure, whether or not theology agrees. First, the elements are real, and so are their moods; to ignore them entirely is folly. Second, respect is safer than presumption: a lock of hair left on a stump, a loaf shared with the soil, a blue cord tied at the rail cost nothing more than the admission of mortal feebleness, and may still serve to lessen a hard blow. Third, any art that forgets that its addressees possess their own will drifts toward the fate of the First‑and‑Only, who caged the wind for a time and was in the end undone by it.
