## Definition and Character

"The Merhal" names the confluence and floodplain of two great rivers, the Mer and the Hal, in the northeast of the Circle Sea basin, and "Riv-î-Tep" names the same region by an older habit, using its long-vanished capital from the epoch of the Sorcerer-Kings as a metonym for the land it once ruled. The region is the only one in the basin fertile enough to support double harvests without calling upon the element of Soil, a fact that has made it a civilizational hotbed since the earliest riverine polities formed at the confluence, the seat of the Sorcerer-Kings through the long Rivian Epoch, and Reme's breadbasket for the whole duration of the empire that conquered it. As the center of Circulean civilization shifted west under Orthodox Lurism, the Merhal reverted to local leadership, only for [[Vel Kiriya]], sited north of the floodplains along the upper Mer, to draw many of its nominally independent cities into tribute and outright satrapy in the centuries since.

## Geography and Hydrology

The Merhal occupies the northeast shoulder of the Circle Sea basin, the hottest and most equatorial reach of settled Circulean geography. Two rivers bound it on three sides and give it its name and its wealth alike. The Hal rises in [[Sarm]]'s highland monsoon belt and descends the western escarpment -- the old Dvergr frontier -- in a single incised channel, running east across the region's interior before bending south to meet the sea, and for most of its length it stands as the region's western wall as much as its river. The Mer, gathering a far greater volume out of many tributaries across the humid highland arc on Vel Kiriya's southern slopes and the broken forest country beyond, arrives in a long arc from the north and curves first south, then west towards the estuary, forming the region's southern and eastern boundary and physically separating the Merhal from the margins of northern [[Lu-Pto]]. Only to the north, where the escarpment and the Mer's arc pull apart from each other, does the region lack a river for a wall, and governed land simply thins out into unmarked frontier.

This gives the Merhal the shape of a wedge narrowest and richest where the two rivers meet at the shared Mer-Hal Estuary in the south, and grower ever wider and rougher toward the north, until the land opens into country no city-temple ever fully held. The alluvial core near the confluence is the Merhal people usually mean, the dense, intensively irrigated floodplain of the old Rivian city-temples; the northern reaches are a looser, thinner thing.

### Three Zones

Only where the two rivers run close enough to reach the same ground does double-flooding occur, and by the wedge's own shape that is the confluence alone. The region is accordingly best read in three distinct hydrological zones rather than as one uniform floodplain. The alluvial core, narrow and rich at the wedge's southern point, is the only ground where both the Hal's flood and the Mer's reach the same fields, and it is this overlap, not simple proximity to water, that made it the seat of the Sorcerer-Kings and remains the seat of the Patriarchate of the Merhal today; the Thes cluster belongs here. Upriver along the Hal to the west, cities answer to a single, disciplined flood and read as a clean basin-irrigation culture in their own right, Op-ê-Aru chief among them. Upriver along the Mer to the north and east, cities answer instead to a single broad, unpredictable flood out of a much larger catchment, and read as a levee-and-drainage culture shaped by one unruly river rather than by the reconciliation of two; Tla-ê-Amha belongs here. A city's zone, more than its era or its founding king, is very likely the single best predictor of how its walls, its fields, and its law were built.

The two rivers could not behave more differently. The Hal's catchment is modest but climatically simple, so its flood arrives once a year on a calendar exact enough to set rites by, and outside its immediate floodplain the land falls away quickly into semi-arid steppe and true desert. The Mer's catchment is vast and climatically various, so its flood is not a single clean pulse but a long, uneven swelling spread across a wider season, arriving through a braided, anastomosing tangle of distributaries that shift, silt shut, and reopen within a human lifetime rather than holding one course the way the Hal does. The two systems' wet seasons overlap rather than run truly separate, but the Hal's smaller catchment answers almost at once while the Mer's sheer scale, and the distance its water must travel to reach the confluence, delays and spreads its own flood out behind the Hal's, so the confluence country floods twice in most years rather than once: the Hal's punctual pulse first, then a recession and a planting, then the Mer's slower flood working through the lower channels some time later. It is this staggered double inundation, not any working of the element of Soil, that lets the alluvial core double-crop where every other floodplain in the basin, including the Merhal's own upper reaches along each river, manages one harvest at most.

The instability of the Mer's lower channels is not a minor detail. A city built to the Mer's mouth under one Sorcerer-King can find the river gone from beneath it within a few generations, and channel migration, more than any war of record, very likely explains why the Rivian era's centers of power moved as often as they did. Riv-î-Tep itself once stood in the alluvial core, near where the confluence lies today, before it was torn from the ground and raised into the sky; by now, whatever ground it once stood on has long since been lost to the Mer's own wandering, with nothing left to mark where the old capital's roots once were.

## The Flood Calendar

The Merhal keeps Kharunite reckoning, as befits a region so thoroughly threaded through with Eastern Rite practice, and its working year is properly told in Passes and Phases rather than in Spans. What follows describes the alluvial core specifically, where both floods reach the same ground; the upper Hal and upper Mer answer to their own single rivers on their own schedules, a consequence still to be worked out.

The wet season builds toward the Estival Solstice, which falls in Phase 4 of Pass 3 and marks the true climatic peak of the year across the whole hemisphere, however mild its seasonal swing reads this close to the equator. Sarm's monsoon is the shorter-lived and more continental of the two systems feeding the Merhal, and the Hal's catchment small enough that travel time barely enters into it, so the Hal's flood answers first and sharply: building through Phase 2 of Pass 3, cresting in Phase 3, and already in recession by the time the solstice itself arrives. The solstice therefore carries a double meaning at the confluence, standing as both the astronomical high point of the year and the turning point of the first flood, and the month and a half that follows, running through the close of Pass 3 and into the first Phase of Pass 4, is given over to the water's withdrawal and the first planting.

The Mer answers far more slowly. Its own feeding system, the wetter and longer-lived monsoon of the Vel Kiriya highland arc, is already well advanced by the time the Hal has finished receding, but the sheer scale of the Mer's catchment, and the distance its water must travel to reach the confluence at all, turns what began as rain into a long, uneven swell rather than a pulse. It builds across the second Phase of Pass 4, holds through the third and fourth, and only crests in the first Phase of Pass 5, some sixty days after it first began to rise -- a flood better described as arriving than as striking. The second recession follows immediately, opening in Phase 2 of Pass 5 on the Autumnal Equinox itself, so that the confluence's two harvests are bracketed with a certain symmetry: the first flood turns on one pivot of the year, the second harvest begins on the next.

What remains, from the close of Pass 5 through the whole of Pass 6 and on into the early Phases of Pass 2, is dry season, roughly half the year and containing the Hiemal Solstice at its deepest, coldest point. New Fire, the Kharunite New Year, falls well inside this stretch rather than anywhere near flood or planting, which means the Merhal's farmers keep their calendar's New Year and their own working year's true beginning as two separate occasions entirely -- fitting enough for a rite whose reckoning has never centered itself on a fixed agricultural point the way western Lurist observance sometimes does, attaching instead to the Judges' own travel and to the Six Relations.

## The Sorcerer-Kings

No one now living can say when Emôn-Dju-Bâ-Mhâhat-Nmu first ruled. The civil count itself opens with his reign, and yet the reign's own beginning sits outside anything the count can reach, an absence at the root of the very calendar that measures everything after it. What is known is that he ruled long, that his power over the ordinary limits of a human life earned him the name First-and-Only, and that his court at Riv-î-Tep, standing then on solid ground in the heart of the alluvial confluence, drew tribute and terror in equal measure across the northeast, deep into Lu-Pto and [[Lu-An]], halting only where the Dvergr held the western escarpments against him. He bound Morwynt, the Noble Element of Air, and by the strength of her own captivity tore Riv-î-Tep from the ground it stood on, raising it bodily into the sky, an affront no captive element could be expected to forgive once freed. Three animal-spirit Kins of the [[Alvai]] were broken to his service in that same long reign, their patrons twisted past any hope of the guardianship they had once offered, and somewhere in the same span the city of Oro-î-Thes rose to a paramountcy in the Thes region that none of its neighbors would ever quite equal again, whatever indignities time went on to arrange for it.

It ended, at last, at the hand of his own lover and second-in-command. Set-Ahotep-Imekh Breaks-The-Crown needed no coalition to bring him down, whether goaded by the still-captive Morwynt or moved by ambitions of his own, and he cast the First-and-Only's body into the estuary when the deed was done, around 1650 BR. It was Morwynt herself, freed of her chains at last by that same death, who turned on the sky-city that had been both her prison and an insult to her own element, and ended it. No tradition now agrees on where it came down. Some say Lu-An swallowed it; some blame the Sifted Fields of Lu-Pto, that dry, shifting sea of unstable ground, on its impact; others hold that some vanished side-arm of the Mer received it and has long since silted over the wreck along with everything else it has ever carried. What no one disputes is that the ground Riv-î-Tep once stood on was left empty, and it is very likely for this reason, more than any other, that the name endured as a byword for the whole region rather than for any single surviving place. Nor did Morwynt ever find what she was truly hunting: her tormentor's body, cast into an estuary that has never given it back, left her without closure and, in a tale told and retold in the ages since, searches for him still in every storm, the Wild Hunt at her heels.

Set-Ahotep-Imekh reigned some two centuries in his turn, long enough to rule and be resented in the ordinary way of kings, before his own lover ended him exactly as he had ended his own. Fa-Hê-Rhet-Bâ-Estu She-With-Skin-of-Silver, the last to hold the High Kingship and styled king rather than queen in the old pharaonic manner regardless of her sex, ruled a further century, in part from Tla-ê-Amha on the eastern Mer, before her own fall came, around 1350 BR. It came differently than the two before it. Where a single hand had twice sufficed to end a High King, it took three conspirators acting together to end her, and the centuries that followed never recovered whatever had made one conspirator enough before. The bloodcrafted choirs and tuned chambers that let the High Kings work feats no one since has matched belong, by every indication, to that vanished unity; it is difficult for a careful reader of the record not to conclude that the triumvirate's violence broke something in the taking of the throne that the throne alone was never going to be able to give back.

What followed is properly called the rule of the Lesser Kings, and the name was earned rather than merely inherited. For three and a half centuries the Merhal belonged to no one throne but to many at once, city against city, each holding what its walls and its patron's favor could keep and no more. Yli-â-Thes, younger than ancient Oro-î-Thes but older than most, rose to prominence somewhere in this long middle stretch, only to lose its footing when the river that had made it shifted its banks elsewhere, the same restlessness that has unmade more than one Merhal capital before and since. Through all of it, up on the Hal where the escarpment's cascades finally level into the floodplain, the Cult of the Golden Bull at Op-ê-Aru answered to no king at all, its Assayer of Stone standing as the highest instance of law in a city old enough to remember when that arrangement had needed no defending.

The end, when it came, came from the last of the Lesser Kings rather than from Reme. Around 1050 BR, Thesun-Khetep, ruling from Omt-î-Thes, subdued the long-independent Cult of the Golden Bull at Op-ê-Aru -- the final attempt of any Lesser King at consolidating the Merhal. A decisive win over Thesun-Khetep at the walls of Omt-î-Thes around 1000 BR gave Reme the keys to the breadbasket that are the floodplains, and subjugation of even lesser opponents followed swiftly. Grain and tribute that once went to the courts of Sorcerer-Kings were loaded from then on onto the slow barges that float southwest with the Eastern Gyre's patient turn, to support and succor Reme. With this conquest, the synoecism of Ertran east-coast polities finalized its turn from confederation to factual empire.

## Under Reme

For the better part of a thousand years the Merhal remained a Remean holding, its grain moving under charter rather than open trade, its irrigation law and city-temple administration absorbed into the same chartered economy that bound the whole empire together.

## History After the Fall

### The Age of Enlightenment

The Fall struck the Merhal harder than it struck Reme's more distant western holdings, and the difference is one of timing rather than distance. The Long War had already pushed Reme back from its scattered western garrisons decades before the Fall itself, leaving those territories years, in places generations, to adjust to Reme's absence before it became permanent; the Merhal, still fully bound into the chartered economy on the day the Fall came, had no such runway. What followed was a genuine shock rather than a slow unwinding: scheduled grain quotas simply stopped, and the imperial authority that had once backed the region's irrigation law vanished with them, leaving whatever local administration survived, very likely the same scribal, city-temple lineages that had kept the old sexagesimal accounts since before the conquest, to reassert itself piecemeal across a floodplain with no single throne to answer to. Onto this disruption the young Theocracy of Eternal Dominion, eager to prove its reach, turned its crusading zeal early toward closer and more manageable ground: the suppression of the Merhal's own surviving elemental cults, the last living thread back to the Rivian inheritance. A region trying to rebuild without its imperial patron found its own religious continuity actively persecuted by the one western power in a position to help it, and none of the Commonwealth's own founding milestones touch the Merhal at all; they happen entirely without the region's notice.

### The Dark Ages

Where the west spent these centuries fracturing into rival claimants and Anti-Hierophants, the Merhal, already resentful of Theocratic suppression and never more than peripheral to An-Mirda's quarrels in the first place, had every reason to drift rather than to care. It was in these centuries that Judges of the Eastern Rite made their real advance along the upper Merhal and deep into Riv-î-Tep, offering a model of piety that treated the region's own elemental inheritance as continuous with devotion rather than opposed to it, precisely where the earlier Theocracy had offered only suppression. What followed was not merely tolerance but genuine recovery: the same centuries in which western scholarship was disgracing elemental invocation under a rising Antidiscordianist sentiment and burying Reme's own aetheric practice under damnatio memoriae were the centuries in which Kharunite scholarship in the Merhal quietly studied, preserved, and systematized what remained of the old Rivian invocation tradition, the grammar of structure, resonance, and materiality that the Sorcerer-Kings' own bloodcrafted choirs had once worked by. Cities that had known nothing but fragmentation since the Fall flourished for the first time since the Sorcerer-Kings themselves, and Vel Kiriya's tributary claim over the northern Merhal grew out of this religious foothold rather than out of conquest, cities that found Kharunite accommodation more livable than Orthodox suppression drifting into Paxhar Vel's orbit as a consequence of faith long before it was ever a matter of arms. Toward the era's close, as Genovrast de Lavion's settlement of the Varsa in 998 AR brought a more conciliatory Third Hierophancy into being, the western Church, for the first time in centuries able to speak with something like one voice, formally recognized a Patriarchate of the Merhal seated at Omt-î-Thes, an attempt to reclaim ecclesiastical ground the Kharunites had spent two centuries quietly winning.

### The Age of Corsairs

The corsair culture and blue-water daring reshaping the Western Gyre in this era have little to do with a floodplain economy built on caravan roads and river barges, and the Merhal's own development runs largely on a separate track through these centuries. What the era does bring is a first, partial answer to the Mer's oldest problem. As arcannons, torsion weapons, and crossbows spread basin-wide, armed riverboats begin to patrol the Mer's braided lower channels for the first time, not by mastering the river's shifting geography but by simply outgunning whatever had been using that maze as cover; river piracy is suppressed and convoys escorted where before only a specialist pilot's local knowledge offered any safety at all. It is a military answer rather than an economic one, but it is enough to hasten what has already begun: the patchwork of paired clergy and Kharunite houses along the Merhal solidifies into the settled arrangement it holds today, and Vel Kiriya's tributary influence hardens from religious sympathy into something closer to formal satrapy administration.

### The Aetheric Age

This is the era the Merhal's fortunes properly turn, and they turn twice over. Below the water, the basin-wide shrinking of choir engines to fit launches and riverboats gives the Mer the propulsion answer armed patrol boats never could: a paddlewheel needs no fixed channel and no reliable draft, only enough water in general, which is exactly what a braided, silting, avulsion-prone river can offer and a keeled sailing vessel never reliably could. The old asymmetry, the disciplined Hal as trunk route, the wild Mer as a wilderness corridor for specialists only, inverts within a generation. Above the canopy, the great pontoon liners that are this era's own signature wonder, already fully linking major ports across the basin, find early and eager service along the one route that has no port to speak of at all: the jungle gap between the Merhal's satrapies and Paxhar Vel. Simple roll-on roll-off freighters, a flatbed catamaran hull slung beneath an unglamorous gas envelope, cheap to build and forgiving of rough handling, prove far better suited to that crossing than any luxury liner, and while bulk grain continues to move by river, tribute, officials, and anything worth a premium begin moving by air instead, over a jungle that has suddenly stopped being a meaningful barrier to anyone who can afford the fare. Vel Kiriya's grip on the Merhal, already religious and increasingly administrative, becomes something closer to integration in truth rather than mere claim. In this same era, the Grand Academy of the Broken Isles's own celebrated breakthrough, the demonstration that formally opens disciplined aetheric engineering under the triad of structure, resonance, and materiality, arrives suspiciously well-formed for a discipline the west had spent five centuries actively disgracing rather than developing. The Merhal and Vel Kiriya both know what the Academy's work owes to Kharunite scholarship quietly kept alive through the Dark Ages; western histories credit the Academy's genius outright and say little of where the groundwork came from, and it remains a live, quiet sore point rather than a settled grievance, the kind of thing a Kharunite scholar might mention once, dryly, and let drop.

### The Imperial Age

The aerial corridor opened in the previous era matures rather than begins anew, the simple buoyant freighters of the pontoon-liner generation giving way to faster flying boats built on reduced internal gas bags, the same basic technology refined into something with real performance rather than pure lift. By the time Lavion's Roburic II turns his professionalized soldiery eastward, claiming protectorates along the Eastern Gyre littoral, the war this provokes is fought beyond the Merhal's own edge rather than through it, Vel Kiriya's loss of Lu-Pto's iron sands to Lavion the earlier conflict its own Judges and shrine-wardens still remembered when war came again. The Merhal itself is untouched by any of it, already a settled and deeply integrated satrapy frontier by this point, the southernmost extent of Vel Kiriya's holdings, its produce and tribute underwriting a war fought entirely beyond its own borders. When the Great War itself comes, the region's own fate scarcely changes hands through any of it; what changes, at the Peace of Balboa, is everything beyond it, Vel Kiriya's satrapy administration of Lu-Pto and the Eastern Gyre littoral confirmed at last, ground recovered rather than newly won.

## Cities of the Merhal

### The Cities of Thes

Three cities carry the Thes name, and between them they trace the region's whole political history in miniature.

**Oro-î-Thes** is the oldest of the three, raised in the Rivian Epoch under the First-and-Only or his immediate successors as what was very likely the region's paramount temple city in that age. Its long primacy did not survive the centuries that followed it, but the city itself did: Oro-î-Thes stands inhabited still, proud of a pedigree no rival can match and just diminished enough to feel the insult of that rivalry keenly. It is the region's old money, resentful in the particular way only a city that remembers being first can be.

**Yli-â-Thes** held the middle ground, both in age and in fortune. Younger than Oro-î-Thes but older than the city that would eventually eclipse it, Yli-â-Thes rose to regional prominence sometime in the long, fractured centuries of the Lesser Kings, only to lose its footing when the river shifted its banks away from the city's walls, the same restless channel migration that has unmade more than one Merhal capital before and since. Stripped of the water that had made it, its power passed elsewhere.

**Omt-î-Thes** inherited what the other two had lost. Its founder-king, Thesun-Khetep, last of the Lesser Kings, took his own epithet from the region's older name rather than the reverse, meaning the city that would go on to lose the war that built an empire was, by the Thes region's own reckoning, the newest of the three, not the oldest. That inversion has outlived the Rivian Epoch entirely: Omt-î-Thes stands today as the seat of the Patriarchate of the Merhal, the region's living center of religious authority, watched by two ancient neighbors who remember when the crown sat elsewhere.

### Op-ê-Aru

Where the Hal drops out of the escarpment's cascades and finally levels into the floodplain, the river becomes workable rather than merely spectacular, and it was there that Op-ê-Aru rose. For most of the Rivian Epoch and long into the fractured age of the Lesser Kings that followed it, no Sorcerer-King's writ ran here at all: the Cult of the Golden Bull held the city, and an Assayer of Stone stood as the highest instance of law, an elemental judiciary answering to no crown. That independence outlasted the First-and-Only, his successors, and the long chain of Lesser Kings after them, ending only around 1050 BR, when Thesun-Khetep subdued it in his final act of consolidation before Reme's own conquest swept away everything he had built.

### Tla-ê-Amha

A river-bound city on the eastern Mer, Tla-ê-Amha was once a capital of Fa-Hê-Rhet-Bâ-Estu She-With-Skin-of-Silver, third and last of the High Kings, who closed the line's grim opening pattern: Set-Ahotep-Imekh overthrew his lover the First-and-Only, and was in turn undone by his own lover, She-With-Skin-of-Silver, before she too fell to a triumvirate of usurpers and the age of the Lesser Kings began in earnest.

### Unplaced Cities

Nine further city-names are attested for the Merhal, each governing its own vicinity in the manner of the five above, none yet placed on the ground or in the timeline: Tla-î-Locs, Mor-ê-Deyu, Mor-ê-Eni, Sip-ê-Apah, Dras-â-Rat, Nym-ê-Geda, Um-ê-Epeh, Op-ê-Urei, and An-ê-Uteh.

## Naming Conventions

Merhal city-names follow a consistent three-part structure: a prefix, a lexicalized connecting vowel (î, ê, or â), and a suffix. The suffix names the city's region or locality -- three cities (Omt-î-Thes, Yli-â-Thes, Oro-î-Thes) share the suffix Thes, itself older than the sorcerer-king Thesun-Khetep, whose epithet derives from the region rather than the reverse. The connecting vowel is a fossilized preposition, "of," "by," or "upon," with its surface form conditioned by ordinary historical sound-change rather than by any live grammatical distinction today. The prefix very likely once signified the settlement's function -- temple city, fortification, market town, and so on -- but two and a half millennia of lexicalization and drift have planed that meaning flat, and it is deliberately left unglossed rather than assigned invented etymologies.
