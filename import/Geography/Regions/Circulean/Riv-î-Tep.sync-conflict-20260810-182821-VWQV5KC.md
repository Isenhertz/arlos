# Land of the Two Rivers, Cradle of Thrones

Riv-î-Tep lies in the north-eastern quarter of the continent of An-Suur, its territory defined by the convergence of the Mer and Hal rivers -- twin lifelines whose confluence has shaped not only the soil but the very soul of the region. Though much of its ancient grandeur is now lost to time, its climate, landform, and cycles of water and wind continue to define the lives of its people as much as the silent, humming bones of cities long buried.

Regarding naming, it is customary to speak of the meeting of the rivers in hyphenated form as the "Mer-Hal confluence", but the region between the two rivers as the "Merhal floodplains", or simply the Merhal. Riv-î-Tep, the region's common exonym, is a historical _pars pro toto_, harking back to the legendary seat of the First [[Sorceror-King]] of old that ruled the region from there.

## Geography and Hydrography

Riv-î-Tep lies upon one of the Circulean's broadest lowlands, a near-level expanse built by the long partnership of the Mer and Hal rivers, two unlike waters. The vast, fertile region between them is called the Merhal floodplains. Rich in loamy silt, this regions of An-Suur is one of the few capable of sustaining double harvests of barley, river-root, and flax. For that reason this area was once the heart of the realm of the Sorcerer-Kings and then the breadbasket of the [[Remean Empire|Remeans]] after them, and though it never regained its historic significance after the fall of the ancients, it remains heavily populated.

From the humid, far northern jungles the colossal Mer slides in vast, slow curves towards the sea, braided around wooded islands and anabranching belts until the channel commonly reaches six miles wide. From the eastern highlands a different river descends: the Hal, clear in the dry months, laden in the rains, shallow and disciplined within a corridor that can reach two miles in width by the time it meets the Mer. Their confluence rests upon a megafan of alluvium that they have together laid down for ages, the bed of a civilization measured in levees, oxbow lakes, relict channels, and marshy backswamps.

The Mer's gradient is so slight that the sea's hand is felt far inland. In the last reaches the tidal backwater slackens the current, channels widen, and suspended clays flocculate into brown clouds that drift toward the coast. The delta is not a single spearpoint into the Circulean but a migrating, many-mouthed fringe. The Great [[Circle Sea#The Gyres of the Circle Sea|Eastern Gyre]] and its coastal currents shear and sorts the river plume, pushing some mouths to stagnate and others to thrive, so towns and harbors must respect a coastline that redraws itself within a lifetime. Barrier bars, reed marshes, and brackish lagoons form a storm buffer that breathes with the seasons.

West of the fertile riverland, where the land slowly rises towards the highlands of [[Sarm]], the floodplains give way to dry, rugged escarpments that rise gradually to form wind-swept plateaus. Little grows here but thornvine and windgrass. Subterranean aquifers still follow old riverbeds, making deep wells viable but temperamental.

South of the flootplains, an open basin of sun-cracked earth and erratic fertility opens up, vast grasslands that mark the transitional region between the floodplains and the savannahs of [[Lu-Pto]]. It is mostly home to semi-nomadic shepherd communities, who maintain a guarded reverence for the ruins dotting the plain.

## The Two Rivers and Their Floods

The twin rivers keep different calendars. The Hal's flood follows the eastern rains and lingering snowmelt in the highlands, rising on a predictable pulse that temple stairs can count and farmers can trust. The Mer swells later under the northern monsoon, a wider and more capacious crest that can run for weeks without drama. In most years the two peaks arrive out of phase, a blessing that tames catastrophe at the confluence. Once or twice in a generation, the crests align and water rides over the natural levees in a sheet, carving new crevasse splays, drowning boundaries, and laying down the silt that makes recovery swift.

Sediments differ as much as the hydrographs. The Mer carries a prodigious suspended load of dark clays and silts scoured from jungle soils and far upland stones; these blanket the floodplain in nutrient-rich muds. The Hal lays down finer, quick-settling silt along its rims, building low, fertile levee crests and basin clays beyond. Cutbanks collapse on outer bends where the rivers bite their own work, so the people of Riv-î-Tep armor toes with stone and living reed mats and accept that avulsion is part of the world, not an error to be eliminated.

## Settlement and Urban Form

The Hal teaches line and ledger. Towns string along its levee spine like beads, terraces stepping down toward basin fields that flood and retreat in rhythm with the year. Streets follow the crown, house-fronts face the water with flood doors and sacrificial ground floors, and side lanes fall toward reed ponds and rice-like marsh crops. Because the Hal can be bridged with craft and will, even in high water, administration can walk or roll from town to town, and the fields can be measured, counted, and taxed. The river is a gentle highway here, defining the landscape but not dominating it. 

The Mer by contrast teaches reach and roadstead, because it not just defines the landscape -- it is the landscape. Vast enough from shore to shore to fill the horizon, its downriver cities look more like ocean-side communities than riverbound ones. Upriver towards Vel Kiriya, where the Mer cuts through the jungles of the north, its cities gather on islands and raised bars, larger districts climbing on piles and stilts, their plans radial from quays rather than axial along roads. Rope bridges lace inner canals; ferries knit neighborhoods; bell towers signal fog codes that mean more than any streetcrier. Authority, like all of civilisation here, rides in boats. Charts may bind better than charters, and the right to a moorage or a navigable back-channel is worth more than an inland deed.

## Economy and Navigation

Bulk and predictability flow down the Hal: grains, flax, papyrus-like reeds, basin fish from managed oxbows, and the tidy surpluses of fields whose water can be timed by stair marks. Reach and variety travel the Mer: hardwoods, gums, resins, and far upland ores gathered by an archipelago of island depots and carried on deep-keel freighters that make routine voyages toward Vel Kiriya. The Hal favors broad, shallow barges pushing against a tow-path atop the levee; the Mer is almost more maritime than riverine, allowing the ocean-going vessels of the Circulean to cut deep into the hinterlands.

Irrigation follows the land's logic. Headworks stand on levee crowns; canals run roughly parallel to the main rivers to feed lateral spillcuts into basin fields, with return drains to oxbows that serve as hatchery lagoons. In law and habit a portion of the plain remains a sacrificial avulsion belt -- land allowed to take the fury of a great flood so that levees elsewhere stand. Harbors in the lower country must accept siltation and migration. Some are dredged seasonally, some are abandoned without ceremony, their functions transferred down-coast where the mouth now runs.

## Law, Water Rights, and Civic Duty

Because rivers are sovereign, the Merhal writes law that bargains rather than commands. Along the Hal, property is surveyed anew after each crest from the measures on flood stairs, and rotational gates apportion water by schedule. Failure to keep one's levee span is a civic offense counted like dereliction of watch. Along the Mer, titles run to moorings, narrows, and shorefaces. When a channel avulses, customary rights may vanish and must be compensated by shares elsewhere, for the river has the older claim.

The common obligations of the floodplain are canal desilting, levee tamping, revetment repair, and avulsion-belt planting. These corvées are ranked like militia service and recorded with the same pride. Nilometers and flood pillars, some of them older than the polities that now maintain them, are public instruments whose readings can start sowing, reduce taxes in a lean year, or trigger debt remissions after a ruinous crest.

## Religion and Ritual

Old rites endure because they work in the most practical sense. On the Hal, processions climb and descend the flood stairs, and the year is proclaimed from measured rise in a language that everyone understands. On the Mer, offerings are made at confluences and narrows, pilots take blessings before sounding, and bell towers keep fog codes that are half liturgy and half lifesaving craft. The [[The Light|Lurist]] overlay here is a tug of war between the [[Church of the Light|Anish Rite]] and [[Eastern Rite|Kharunite]] predominance, but syncretism here is neighborly rather than doctrinal; the Mother of Rivers is venerated highly here for her two great children, the might of which simply cannot be ignored.

## History: From Sorcerer-Kings to Successor States

In the oldest strata of memory, the Sorcerer-Kings learned the same two lessons the land still teaches.  When the Remeans came, they standardized rather than overturned. The Hal welcomed survey, tax, and timetable, and Levee Roads carried legion logistics. The Mer was fortified at island nodes and reliable narrows, its pilot guilds folded into imperial transport. After Reme's eclipse brought the long shadows of obscurity, the plains fractured along their native grain. Temple-principalities hardened into bureaucratic sovereignties on the Hal; island leagues and ferry-lords styled themselves thalassarchs on the Mer; cities survived as chartered leagues that adjudicated water rights and managed the great depots where surplus met carriage.

Official clerical positions view the ruins of the Sorcerer-Kings with caution: not forbidden, but "unsound", particularly where lingering aetheric effects are verifiable. Archaeology of former temple sites or similar places of significance is discouraged. Modern scholars that view Riv-î-Tep as a treasure trove of proto-aetheric insight must often cloak their expeditions as hydrological surveys or social medicine campaigns to avoid theological resistance.

The spread of paddlewheelers made up-Mer traffic routine, enriching riverine ports and strengthening trade with far Vel Kiriya. Yet the map of power remains written in levees and channels, not in roads alone, and it is still updated every flood.

## Hazards and Long Change

Siltation shifts the delta mouths down-coast, burying some harbors in a decade and making others bloom from sand and reed. Poorly drained reclaimed basins salt in dry cycles and must be flushed in winter and broken of hardpan or returned to reeds. Storm surges and Circulean seiches push the salinity wedge inland in low-flow years, so intakes are guarded by locks, and freshwater springs at the delta margins become precious. Dust rides off adjacent drylands into lateral canals and is met with windbreak groves along the levee crowns. None of these are surprises. They are listed in the old manuals, and the solutions are humble: plant, dredge, flush, record, and remember.

## City-States

Though modern Riv-î-Tep is a mosaic of successor states that are tributaries or outright client states of the King of Kings in Paxhar Vel, the cities of the floodplains remember its founders with a fervor that is half devotion and half apology. In the Rivian Epoch the sorcerer‑kings wielded factual otherworldly power -- a mode of working the world that modern aetherics can describe only in metaphors and footnotes. Each city spent labor, blood, and material in its own signature grammar to feed a single towering ambition, so that levees, stairs, terraces, and caissons became parts of a person, not merely a place.

The grand designs of the ancients are now obscured by time and the rivers -- yet the undertakings were of such scale that fragments still shape ordinary life as cadastral walls, ferry piers, hatcheries, and salt pans. The oldest cities claim direct founding by decree of Emon‑Dju‑Bâ‑Mahat‑Nmu First‑and‑Only, long before Reme was so much as a smudge on any map, and take pride in looking back on a history of over three thousand years of continuous human habitation.
