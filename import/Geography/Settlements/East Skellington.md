East Skellington lies on the northern coast of [[Stranu]], on the southern shore of the [[Circle Sea#^113153|Suurian Bight]], nestled in the grimy shadow of the great industrial sprawl of Greater Lancratershire, itself a coastal [[Commonwealth]] conurbation that expanded unchecked during the aetheric industrial boom of the late 1400s and early 1500s AR.

It began as an overspill of the Northern Seaboard Port Authority -- dry docks, quarantine sheds, timber yards, slag pits, and bunkhouses thrown up on the mudflats east of Lancrater to service the Strait. The Authority liked the site because it was close to the cranes and outside the city's nuisance laws. They staked old hulls in the shallows as breakwaters and stripped them to ribs; locals called the row of bleached frames a "skeleton town", and the name stuck to the shoreline long before it stuck to people, eventually slurring to "Skellington" with the "East" added to differentiate it from the respectable western wards of Lancrater proper. No one lived there: You punched in, you punched out, you went home westside.

Then in 1479 AR the [[Circle Sea]] rose like a fist. Storm surges raked the western coasts raw -- Stranu's inlets drowned, north-western [[Lavion]]'s harbors shattered -- and a month later the Strait clogged with refugee scows and broken ships drifting under rag sails. The Authority took emergency contracts to clear channels and unload relief cargo; the sheds and bunkhouses became triage halls overnight. Families were told to wait in the lee of the Skellington "for a week, two at most" while the city found space. Weeks became winter. The Authority posted **NO HABITATION** signs to keep liability off its books, but every day the hiring clerks still walked the line with clipboards and scrip, because hands were needed and these were close and cheap.

When the contracts ran out, the notices hardened. The sheds went back to being "facilities", not shelter. The city fathers declared the mudflats unfit for residence and outside municipal responsibility; the Authority hired private men to scrape the shoreline clean. But labor beats law when cranes still need turning. Foremen began paying off the books -- you could not sleep here, but you could work twenty hours and lean against a post between shifts if no one saw. Scrip replaced coin, redeemable only at Authority stores. Families roofed the gaps between pilings with sailcloth and tin. Someone ran a tap line off a mains valve and no one important came to fix it. With the hiring line came the Tongs and their peace for sale, and with the Tongs came the first permanent ladders nailed into the ribs of the old hulls.

The place grew the way rot does -- quietly, then all at once. Salvage scows winched derelict hulls upright and spiked them into the flats as foundations for new tenements. Catwalks stitched building to building; stairwells doubled as gutters; the alleys developed ceilings. Authority crews conducted "clearances" when auditors toured, and Thieftaker [[aethermata]] walked down the lanes, but the day after a burn the frames were braced, the wire was re-stolen, and pots steamed again. Waste from the yards and aetheric tailings seeped into the ground; tapping a pipe meant [[The Six Elements#^eb262b|fulminance]] burns on a good day and a blue-white death on a bad one. By the time anyone in Lancrater thought of "East Skellington" as an actual thing, the borough had already been there a decade and would not be made to go away by decree.

Its relation to Lancrater settled into an arrangement both sides pretended not to notice. The city claimed jurisdiction ended at the Authority fence, which meant sanitation, drainage, and fire control did too. The respectable wards pointed at the **NO HABITATION** signs and washed their hands; their contractors sent runners by night to hire "deniable" crews when guild men struck or a dock collapsed and bodies needed finding in the slurry. The Authority set gate fees and lantern tariffs that skimmed wages back into its own coffers; the police took copper to look away; charities set out soup during elections and shuttered once the counting was done. When epidemics flared, the bridge to Skellington closed "for quarantine" and opened when the fever had burnt through the weakest. When cranes needed extra shifts before the harvest fleets, the bridge opened wide and the day rate dropped because there was always someone hungrier on the mud.

Every scandal in Lancrater had a Skellington fix. A warehouse fire that threatened the city line meant a "controlled clearance" on the flats the next week to prove resolve. A budget shortfall meant a new schedule of fines for "trespass" and "improper use of Authority fixtures", collected exclusively on the east side. A strike in the respectable yards meant a week of scab crews ferried from the ladders at dawn, paid in scrip and promised regular work that vanished as soon as the guild rolled over. Property men in the city bought Authority bonds with clean hands and spoke earnestly about civic order; the same men's factors leased "storage space" by the square pace inside East Skellington and collected rent on Fridays.

By the 1500s the borough and the city had learned each other. Lancrater advertised itself as shipshape and safe while exporting its risks to a zone it refused to recognize. East Skellington provided a standing army of workers who could be hired without names, dismissed without severance, and blamed without consequence. The Authority kept the wire humming and the Thieftakers oiled, and the city kept its conscience clear by insisting the signs were right there on the posts for anyone literate to read. The storm of 1479 had made refugees; the decades after made a labor pool. That is how a row of skeleton ships became a skeleton city, and how a legal fiction shaped a real one until both sides needed the lie to keep the cranes turning.

By 1550 AR, East Skellington was a human reef welded to the ribs of Greater Lancratershire -- a vertical maze of hovels and workshops stacked so tight the alleys had ceilings and the ceilings became floors. Sunlight is but a rumor in the lower rungs. The Strait's wind comes in salted and sour, then dies against a thicket of catwalks, laundry lines, and exhaust flues that weep black condensate even on clear days. The Northern Seaboard Port Authority still owns every rotting plank and rivet here, and its posted decree remains simple: **no habitation**. Which means everyone who lives here is an illegal squatter, and when everyone is illegal, everything they do is too.

From the water, the borough today looks like a cliff made of junk. Scows shoulder through slicks of oily runoff toward wharves that were condemned before most of the residents were born. Hulls too crooked to sail are winched upright and spiked into the mud to serve as new tenements. Shinbone alleys thread between them, paved in ash and fish scales. Above, aerial bridges stitch door to door across open voids. Below, crawlspaces hold the dens -- card tables, sootgem parlors, black kilns for curing boot-leather and cooking engram paste. Children learn to navigate by smell: brine and tar to the docklands; vinegar and brass polish to the lockshops; ozone and lavender oil to the gutter-surgeons who will wire a hand steady for a price.

Combustion is risky when everyone and everything is this closely packed deep in the warrens. Open flame calls hungry fire sprites, so the locals hang mesh cages filled with river salt and glass slag to keep the worst of the flickers out. Light comes from stolen lines, the cold hum of fulminance conduits peeled off NSPA trunks and fed through bone-handled knife switches. The glow is steady until a surge walks the wire and snaps across a wall like sheet lightning. It is common to see scorched fingertips, uncommon to see compensation. When a conduit burns out and sets off the breakers westside, the Thieftakers arrive well before the maintenance crew -- brass-jointed, lacquered black, faceplates etched with serials. They do not patrol so much as pursue, each carrying on its slated instructions a list of names and charges. Once a name is written, the thing will hunt until its directives are satisfied, its mainspring is broken, or the rip-stop pulled.

Order exists, of a kind. The Tongs sell it wholesale. Their brokers sit in levy-stalls under awnings of patched sailcloth, pricing everything that passes: sacks of bolt-heads pulled from shipwrecks, gutbrew in stone jars, illicit copies of resonance schemas engraved on bone slips. Dock-chiefs take percentages from every deal that crosses their ladder. On Feastdays, the poles along the main lane bloom with chalked sigils -- a truce, of sorts -- so labor gangs can negotiate without knives. Truces never last through the night. There is money to be made after dark when the Thieftakers turn inward to grind their lists and the NSPA foremen retreat to the safety of Westside, leaving the borough to settle its own accounts.

The Authority denies it, but whole assignments depend on East Skellington hands. Salvage quotas, refit schedules, the quiet disappearance of seized cargo -- all require workers who can be paid in dock scrip and dismissed without notice. Recruiters come with clipboards and cudgels. Contracts are short, penalties are long. Fail to make tally, and your barge berth goes to the next family on the stairs. Most wages return to the Authority in the form of gate fees, lantern tariffs, and fines for the unlicensed use of NSPA materiel. Rent is illegal here, formally speaking, so it is called a "storage fee" instead and collected weekly. The bank always wins.

Air tastes like metal filings. The rain leaves sheens of turpentine. When the wind shifts, soot rises from the slag yards and falls as a tender ash that looks almost like snow until it burns on the tongue. There are gardens of a sort -- buckets of saltwort and rag-beans strung on pulley lines up in the good air where the Thieftakers do not care to climb. Water is boiled in kettles under sprite screens, or drawn at night from the Authority mains with quarried taps and quiet prayers. If you can hear the sea you are close enough to smell it; if you can see it you are either rich or falling.

There are services. A woman called Greylace runs a clinic in the belly of a dead barge, sewing wounds with stiff thread. Payment is coin, or parts. A man named Knowin' Lurca maps the borough and sells his routes to outlanders with the warning that maps go stale here the way bread does. A choir of factory widows keeps a shrine to Redemptionist saints -- most residents consider that a cruel joke. The common prayer is more practical: _let the wire hold; let the latch catch; let the slate misspell my name_.

The Commonwealth's weak hand is everywhere. There are magistrates over the ridge and a council down the road, but the writ stops at the line where Authority guards demand their fee. Elections come and go with pamphlets promising drainage, paved stairs, new piers; the only things that arrive are inspectors and cranes. When reformers tour the alleys, the Thieftakers herd the worst of the visible misery into the underslots until the visitors leave. The next morning, the levy-stalls open, the chalked sigils are wiped off, and the same men bid on the same chains of labor with the same cold calculations in mind.

Hope exists, but not freely. A berth on a clean barge costs three months of wages and a favor you cannot refuse later. A daughter with clever hands might apprentice to a lockshop and learn to cut forged brassback keys cleanly enough to keep her pride, though far more squalid choices aren't rare. A son with quick feet might run messages along the wire-ways faster than the Thieftakers can triangulate his path, but the Tongs will be waiting for him instead. Every sliver of hope is pried from someone else -- a berth stolen, a favor called, a rival pushed off a ladder in the dark with the sea to eat the noise. The borough teaches its own ethics: debts are heavier than bodies; secrets weigh least of all.

By midnight, East Skellington glows like a drowned star, all cold light and dripping black between. The Strait breathes through tight seams. Somewhere, a slate scratches and fulminance arcs in a bluish blaze. Somewhere, a chalk mark appears under a lantern, promising truce until dawn. In the morning, the ash will fall again, and the Authority will count its cargo, and the borough will count its missing, and life will proceed along the only rule that ever mattered here: **survive, and pay**. Or as the local saying has it, muttered at stairheads and levy-stalls alike -- _If the Tongs don't get you, the Thieftakers will._
