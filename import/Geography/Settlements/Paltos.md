Paltos clings to the sea on a line of pale cliffs on Stranu's eastern coastline, a spill of warm-coloured stone and red roofs looking northeast across the [[Circle Sea]] as if it were terribly proud of itself, and not entirely without reason. The city sits where the Western Gyre's southbound rush brushes the [[Kingdom of Lavion|Lavienese]] coast, making it the northernmost port in the kingdom, and its harbor is deep enough that it can receive the big deep-sea hulls that arrive salt-streaked and weary from the [[Open Ocean]]. The city is built on a gentle slope in the otherwise steep chalk-white cliffs of the coast here, a sheltered basin tucked under the lee of the headland. Above it on the cliff's crown the White Basilica of the Everlasting Flame gathers the sun, its towers visible far out at sea, equal parts navigational mark and unsubtle reminder that [[Church of the Light|the Church]]'s reach does not end at the shoreline.

Behind the cliffs the land softens quickly, rolling into a fat, well-watered countryside that grows nearly anything the coastal climate can be bullied into yielding: olives, vines, hardy grains, truck-gardens, and an assortment of orchard fruit. The Paltans like to insist that their markets smell of crushed herbs and citrus peel even on bad days, and inconveniently for [[Virell|Virellian]] snobs they are often right. The Free City's charter seats it as the lone council-ruled enclave in the otherwise ducal lands of Rasselune, its guilds and wards electing councillors rather than bowing directly to some baron with an inherited chin. Proximity to the [[Commonwealth of Principalities|Commonwealth]] across the border has left fingerprints, inclining the citizens towards deliberative meetings, debate, and a habit of treating civic duty as a thing of pride. Lavienese courtiers therefore dismiss Paltos as rustic, almost provincial, and profess amazement that its books actually balance, its roads are patrolled, and its customs officials can count without moving their lips.

Inside the walls, the city is of a gentle sort of verticality. The lowest streets hug the harbour edge, crowded with the usual fragrant collection of fishmongers and quay-side cookshops, then roads and switchback lanes climb the slope southward along the cliff face before curling back north toward the upper city, where merchants and notaries keep their counting-houses in buildings that can actually catch a breeze. Above even that, on the plateau, the basilica and the council quarter share a broad, colonnaded plaza, with priests, advocates, and guildmasters orbiting one another in a permanent dance of precedence that could probably look dignified if it were not accompanied by so much shouting. On high holy days processions descend from the church to bless the harbour, bells answering bells between spire and ships; on ordinary days, the same steps carry cargo-clerks and dockside runners, because sanctity is all very well but someone has to move the crates. In sum, Paltos is exactly what Virell likes to pretend it has outgrown, a working city where the people are just independent enough to be annoying and just profitable enough that no one in the capital suggests changing that.
