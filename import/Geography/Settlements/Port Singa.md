Port Singa was the premiere entry point into the [[Rim of Fire]] during the [[Timeline of the Circle Sea#^c8057f|Age of Corsairs]], the port where [[Circle Sea|Circulean]] and Rimlander polities met, mixed, and mingled.

It sat on the inner edge of a broad, drowned shelf at the eastern bend of the Rim of Fire, where the deep blue of the [[Open Ocean]] heaves up into only a few gross paces of water and breaks into a maze of reefs and atolls. Trade winds from the [[Circle Sea#^113153|Suurian Gates]] blow in from due east here, then curl south and west along the volcanic arcs. At the shelf they shear, slow, and tangle. Currents climb from the depths in cool upwellings that fatten fish and clear the air, then slide away again along pale sand tongues between coral heads. From above, if anyone in the Age of Corsairs had the altitude to see it, Port Singa's archipelago looks like a central sickle of an island cupping a deep inner lagoon, and hundreds of lesser volcanic teeth and sand-keys scattered like chaff over that continental shelf.

A convoy that has slipped the Suurian Gates to brave the long blue desert must push west for days with nothing ahead but sky and swell. Then the water begins to pale in broad bands, soundings climb in a hurry, and scattered towers rise on the horizon: stone beacons on isolated coral heads, each topped with a lantern and a [[Gryphoids|gryph]] perch, each kept by a pilot clan that has been selling safe water since before there were Circulean charts. The deep channel threads in from the east and then shoulders along the southern face of the main island before bending into the lagoon. Every captain learns the same rule: you do not attempt the Singa shelf without a local pilot unless you are a fool, or in a hurry to meet [[Noble Element|Maregina]] personally.

The Singans themselves are a canoe folk who became something more. Their outer villages still run to stilted longhouses and woven walkways over green water, outrigger canoes tied up three deep under the floors, broad crab-claw sails stacked and drying, the older men with their hair in neat topknots. At the same time, the Singan clans famously developed their own bookkeeping early, which made them a good match for the Circuleans and their mercantile mindset. They weigh anchor dues against fish weirs, reef rights, and timber from the outer islands, levy tax in coin where it can be had and in kind where it cannot, and they have a long memory for foreign flags that try to anchor without paying.

The city on the shore began as a collection of stilt-villages gathered on the lee of the main island, where the lagoon is deepest: longhouses on piles, woven walkways over green water, tide-stairs worn smooth by three tides a day and more storms than anybody bothers to count. As Rim polities consolidated in what was the equivalent of the [[Timeline of the Circle Sea#^dbac83|Dark Ages]] along the Circle sea, warlords discovered that taxing harbors is easier than raiding them, and the Singans raised their first stone fort of coral block and basalt on the island's hill. As the largest island on its shelf, Old Singa actually had the timber to field a handful of petraries pointing down over the inner channel and the throat of the outer sea. By the time [[Kingdom of Lavion|Lavienese]] and [[Commonwealth of Principalities|Commonwealth]] vessels began to reach the Rim in regular numbers, the fort had grown into a citadel and the stilted bamboo sprawl had acquired a heart of burnt clay.

The main island now possesses three faces. Old Singa sits on the outer shore, looking eastward to the Open Ocean over the reef edge. Houses still stand on piles above the spray line, their walls painted in bright earths against mildew and salt. Shrine trees crowd the higher ground, hung with shells, knots and little wooden offerings to tide spirits and to Maregina, who foreigners learn is feared more than loved in a region where water is not merely conveyance but life. On this side of the island the canoes are still king: slim fishing craft and big voyaging hulls, double- and triple-hulled, crewed by lineages that can find submerged reefs by the colour of the water alone.

Inside the lagoon, the water is calmer and the arrival of foreign money led to the formation of distinct enclaves. To the north, a Lavienese quay of stone and brick bites into the shallows, hard-edged and regular. Their dockyards are all capstans, winches, and sweating gangs to drive them. Farther along, Commonwealth counting-houses form a tidy grid around a bare square where guild banners hang in the stale shade. To the south lie the whitewashed warehouses of the Keystone Isles, clustered around a courtyard full of barrels and quarrels and [[Humankind#^4b8a91|Ukili'atan]] pilots drinking on their steps, and farther still lies a [[Vel Kiriya|Velkirian]] compound with stepped gables and a little shrine to Saint Kharun.

All of it lies under the eyes and guns of the Singan citadel. The fortress wall walks the crest of the island in a broken pentagon, its bastions raking both lagoon and outer reef. Within, the Maraj's compound is a chimera of tile roofs copied from Balboa by way of some homesick clerk beside palm-thatch halls built in local style, all around a court that smells of fish, incense, and resins. The Maraj and his councillors do not pretend that Port Singa is the centre of the world, only that it is the one place in the Rim where everyone must pass.

Politically, Port Singa is a spider in the middle of a very complicated web. On paper, the Maraj is a vassal of a larger Rim thalassocracy that claims a scatter of island chains and outer reefs. In practice, the archipelago is its own realm, balanced between the hungry Circulean powers like a toothpick in a maw. Lavion wants a reliable victualling station for its corsair flotillas and the early Companies Royal; the Commonwealth guilds covet a port that is not controlled by their Lavienese rivals; [[Svenu|Svenish]] merchant-adventurers sees a place to sell amber, timber, and hard-won metal; [[Beniotti]] wants a hand on the purse of every convoy that makes the crossing, whether to or from the Circulean; Vel Kiriya wants the spices and alchemica ingredients neither its highlands nor the [[Riv-î-Tep|Merhal]] can grow. None of them can simply take it without provoking the others. So Port Singa lives in the margin: not conquered, not free, but necessary.

Before the Age of Corsairs, Singan wars were paddle and spear affairs, fought by canoe flotillas in the reef channels, with boarding hooks and clubs of bone set with obsidian shards. The Circuleans brought torsion petraries and arms, and later even more refined horrors: airguns charged by leaky hand pumps, and heavy arcannons whose aetheric guts were as temperamental as any sea. The locals had never seen such tools, but they were sailors and pragmatists. Within a generation air-charged punt guns and swivel pieces mounted the walls, Singan gunners knew how to walk arcannon fire along an incoming hull, and local smiths could strip and repair an airgun as if they had invented it themselves.

Dock work, though, is still the same as it was before any of these marvels: rope, yard, pole, and tide. Ships are warped around the lagoon using capstans driven by straining crews on handspikes. Cargo comes out in nets and barrels swung from long booms by men pacing in circles. Water is raised in buckets and poured into casks. Where there are wheels and windlasses, they are turned most often by arms and backs, sometimes by wind or water, and only rarely by draft beasts panting in the heat.

Port Singa's laws recognise how quickly such a place, exposed as it is to so many differing sentiments, can turn into a blaze of violence. Beyond the salt-stone boundary -- an agreed line that separates the quays on the inner side from the reef fort on the outer -- no gun is to be fired and no blade drawn without the Maraj's leave. When this rule is broken, punishment is rarely subtle. A crew that brawls outside a dockside tavern may wake to find its ship fined its entire cargo, its captain clapped in irons until the home country settles a ransom, or its national flag politely informed that it will not be recognised in harbor for a Span. Outside the salt-stone, law belongs to whoever has more guns at the moment, and letters of marque from half a dozen powers circulate like talismans.

The city's most notorious face, however, looks neither to reef nor to quay, but toward pleasure. It does not, moreover, exist strictly within Port Singa at all -- it rides the lagoon.

A sprawl of rafts and pontoons is chained to piles driven into the sand over a bank of reliable shallows. Walkways of lashed timber and woven cane link them in shifting streets. At low water, some of the piles stand dry and barnacled; at full tide, the whole quarter rises and falls gently together, a loose island of its own. Lanterns hang at the junctions, mirrored in the water so that by night the place looks like a floating city.

Locals call it the Lantern Shoals, or simply "the Shoals". Circulean sailors, fond of flattering their vices, whisper other, more romantic names and make literary comparisons when they think nobody from the Church is listening to deride them for it. Officially, however, everyone maintains the same fiction: the Maraj's magistrates point to the salt-stone line and the wording of their own edicts that say the city's sumptuary laws extend over stone and fixed pier only, not over such temporary floating structures (never mind that the Shoals have been a staple for over a century). [[The Light|Lurist]] chaplains, faced with this arrangement, decide that their flocks are technically stepping out of the port and into maritime waters, which is somehow easier to excuse in confessionals. Everyone gets to swear, hand on the _Book of Judges_, that nothing untoward happens in Port Singa itself.

The Shoals grew from the collision of three customs. Rimlanders like the Singans have many customs that place artful courtesans very highly in society, and such courtesan houses already offered something more elaborate than hurried dockside tumbles: singers, poets, companions trained in dance, music, and the slow art of conversation. Lurist merchants brought with them a church that frowns loudly on fornication but has learned to tolerate the practice of "port wives" -- temporary spouses by contract, with some veneer of respectability and ritual. Lastly simple seamen brought their own brutal pragmatism: silver on the barrelhead, no questions asked. In Port Singa these collided and entangled into a system that is neither brothel quarter nor domestic street.

Within the Shoals, arrangements are witnessed and logged, though not in any notebook a prelate could seize. For a voyage cycle or a season, a courtesan may be a contracted partner in the eyes of local custom and, with some creative theology, in those of more flexible confessors. The Shoals have their own guilds and internal courts away from the Maraj, and the courtesan houses possess staggering influence: Their highest-ranked members act as artists, hostesses, and political brokers as much as lovers, blending refinement with just enough respectability to keep the money coming and the sermons at bay. Performance pavilions host plays and masked dances where gender and station twist fluidly in ways that would make Lurist censors froth if the shows were not so lucratively hosted in their own countrymen's back rooms; teahouses and gaming barges line the quieter channels; bath-rafts steam under canvas roofs where deals are made in the hiss of poured water and the rattle of dice.

By contrast, the piers and alleys of Port Singa proper keep their more straightforward trade: quick transactions, few questions, fewer witnesses. Everyone understands the difference. To drag dockside habits onto the Shoals is to discover just how fast a man can be barred from every raft and every tavern in the lagoon.

Meanwhile, every foreign quarter on the quays has its own sound. The Lavienese in their measured cadence, an audible habit of a realm that loves order; the Commonwealth's quarter rings instead with the arguments of guilds trying to agree on tolls. Svenish longship crews in exile sing songs of boisterous bravado and grim melancholy alike. The Ukili'ata, when a fleet swings through, ignore most of this and anchor on the lagoon, sending only a few skiffs ashore for trade, as if the city were a slightly suspect caldera bound to erupt.

From the citadel, on a still night, the Lantern Shoals look harmless enough: a scatter of light over dark water, music and laughter carried thin on the wind. The Maraj's councillors pretend not to see the boat traffic that pulses back and forth after curfew, though they will happily tax it. The Circulean factors pretend not to recognise their own junior clerks in masks and borrowed silk. In the morning, the Shoals sit still once more, bobbing demurely outside the letter of the law, and Port Singa is free to call itself a respectable port in a wicked sea.

Culturally, the city is layered, in the most interesting way. The old Singan shrines are fed with flowers, shell bracelets, and cups of arak poured into the sea. Lurist chapels ring their bells for services whose timings have somehow arranged themselves to avoid the best tides. In festival season, Singan canoe regattas streak through the lagoon, paddlers chanting under banners of painted cloth, while foreign gunners let off ceremonial salutes from their ships, the thunder of arcannon shot rolling off the reef like a storm. In quieter moments, you can stand on the citadel's wall and look down over the whole thing: masts jammed like a forest into the lagoon, canoes weaving between them, smoke from cook-fires and kilns drifting over roofs of tile and thatch, and beyond that the hard blue scratch of the outer sea.

In the Age of Corsairs, Port Singa endured because it gave everyone something they could not easily get elsewhere: reef-savvy pilots for the Circuleans, coin and metal for the locals, a neutral ground for corsairs and chartered companies to eye one another without immediately cutting throats, and a floating quarter where even the most devout sailor could pretend, for a handful of nights, that Circulean expectations were lifted. It was not free, not safe, and not honest, but it was indispensable, which in that era counted for more than anything.
