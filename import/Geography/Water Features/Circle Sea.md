The Circle Sea or _Circuleana_ is the body of water that connects all of the most advanced nations, and as such is a hotbed of trading, piracy, and naval skirmishes. It lies centrally between three landmasses, and is generally characterized by warm and gentle waters.

## The Gyres of the Circle Sea

^06ff45

The Circle Sea is bound by two great, slow engines of water that turn the life of the region as surely as any crown or creed: the Western Gyre and the Eastern Gyre, the former leaning toward the [[Commonwealth of Principalities]] and the [[#The Suurian Gates|Suurian Gates]], the latter sweeping past the [[Ertra|Ertrese Peninsula]] toward [[Lu-An]] and [[Riv-î-Tep]]. Together they make calm fetches, predictable eddies, and a rhythm of slack and push that ports, pilots, and priests alike have learned to keep.

### Shape and rotation

Within the Circulean basin the Eastern Gyre is taken as counter-clockwise, its flow gathering the rich sediments of the Mer and Hal in the northeast before curving towards Ertra. The Western Gyre returns the favor, easing water back clockwise along the coast of Rimec-Suur toward the Gates and An-Suur in the north. The main navigable band of each Gyre -- the Rim -- runs wide off the coast as a stable lane averaging about 4 knots, strong enough to carry barges without sail and steady enough to schedule by. The Rim sits just seaward of the shelf, thickening where capes and islands pinch it and thinning across open reaches.

Closer inshore, counter gyres and back eddies give upstream relief at gentler speeds and greater variability; pilots use them for work against the set, but they are not the highway. Far offshore the water gathers into a tighter shear belt -- the Ring -- which turns hard inward toward the core. At its heart lies a true maelstrom of perpetual elemental upheaval and down draft chimney where speeds spike and convergence rules. Where the limbs draw near, the currents braid rather than clash, yielding lanes that feel like roads under the keel. Exchange with the Open Ocean is moderated by the Gates; outside, trade winds rule, while inside the patient churn of the Gyres sets the tempo. Seasonal drift exists, but the habit of the sea here is regularity rather than surprise.

The dimensions of the Western Gyre are best given as distances between coastlines: from Sarm in the north to Lavion in the south, the Gyre spans almost 1600 leagues at its widest. Between Ertra in the east and Santra in the west on the Circulean coast of Stranu, the distance is approximately 1800 leagues. In the Eastern Gyre, the distance between shorelines is almost a full dozen gross leagues (1728) between the coasts of Lu-An in the south and the western escarpments of the Merhal Floodplains in the north, while its east-west measures between Ertra and Lu-Pto run to a dozen gross leagues at the widest (from the Cape of Balboa eastward), and 1200 leagues in the narrowest span. The narrowest distance between coasts outside the strait of the Suurian Gates is the convergence of the two Gyres between Sarm and the Cape of Balboa, measuring roughly 580 leagues north to south. 

However, these are best guesses; concrete measurements are complicated by the fact that crossing the Ring or Eye makes any direct routes hazardous to the point of foolishness, and sea travel generally follows the Rim.

### The maelstrom model

The Rim is the broad, shelf hugging ocean current used by nearly all blue water traffic. It is charted, scheduled, and policed -- commerce and journey alike live here. In the Western Gyre, this band can run anywhere between 300 to 400 leagues in diameter from its shoreward onset to where it gives way to the shear zone, and averages about 6 knots of surface speed. In the smaller Eastern Gyre the band is narrower, but of the same speed.

Beyond the "mariner's friend" towards the center lies the Ring, the shear belt where the water begins to turn against you. It can be almost a gross leagues wide in some places though it tends to be narrower, and 12 knots are common within its core lanes, with cross set, standing lines, and confused chop. Only specialists dare linger here, and cutting the Ring to cross the Gyre is done sparingly. In fact, "courting Our Lady" is an idiom for cutting through the Ring dangerously close to the maelstroms, and many tales are spun about which daredevils ventured the closest.

The Maelstrom proper is in the very center, a site of elemental upheaval in the form of a down draft chimney. Though impossible to precisely map, estimates put the maelstroms in either Gyre at a diameter of at least a gross leagues, and as much as twice that in some places. Speeds here  exceed twenty knots with strong convergence and a chop that shreds hulls or simply swallows them wholesale in rogue waves. Mariners' yarns speak of hearing Maregina's siren song in the center; the water is a churning blue desert below.

Many coastal currents are stable enough to almost be considered gyres in miniature; most notable of these are the current flowing past the archipelago of the Keystone Isles (a counter-clockwise coastal gyre interfacing with the waters of the Suurian Bight and the Western Gyre), and the Broken Drift that slowly circles east of Balboa, having given rise to the Broken Isles.

### Shores and sediments

The Gyres are couriers of silt and wealth. The estuary of Mer and Hal sends fine mud and organics into the Eastern Gyre; An‑Suur adds fertile runoff from jungled slopes; Sarm contributes wind‑blown dust and flash‑flood wash. Along the east and southeast coasts the currents sort and lay down what they carry. The shores of Lu‑An and Lu‑Pto are rich in black iron sands, washed north and east from the shield volcanoes of Ertra and then fed along the Eastern limb. In a continent short on iron, these beaches are mines that move, and their gleaners watch the tides like accountants.

### Lanes of travel

Inside the Circle Sea, shallow hulls marry the riverine to the maritime, treating the quiet Rim as just another stream. Sail-less barges ride it at schedule, hauled in and sent out by harbor tugs; powered packets overtake and peel into inshore returns for upstream work. In the past, the oar ruled here, and the combined power of man and wind still defines many smaller vessels; in larger ships, patient paddlewheels have taken the place of oar banks and sails alike. Beyond the Suurian Gates, however, sails still rein supreme.

Aeronaves, too, follow the logic of the water below: their sky routes mirror the rim, rising over its limb and curving in step with the coast, more comfortable setting down on water than docking at a pylon. Pilgrimage circuits and convoy schedules alike are planned to the slack and the turn, and captains mark the ring on their charts as a place to avoid unless duty or desperation demands a crossing.

### Faith along the flow

Mariners across the basin speak easily of Maregina -- Our Lady of the Sea, Empress of Fathoms, Guardian of the Gyres. Her cult is the hedge against hubris and the plea for fair flow. Wayhouses and shrines stand at capes where the rim tightens, and named stretches mark on the map where the ring cleaves deeper into the gentle rim than it ought.

However, Lurist doctrine sets a hard line: the Noble Six are stewards, not gods, and bargaining for their favor is error. The Six are at best to be acknowledged as lawful ministers of the All, not courted as patrons. Overt invocations are fined even in the Harmonist harbors of Ertra; in the conservative North, the Ordinate of the Flame is much quicker with its censure. Only the the Eastern Rite is her worship acceptable, as the Light-in-All speaks through her.

Public practice under Orthodox eyes adapts such that rites are framed as appeasement and thanksgiving, never as quid-pro-quo, and in the Commonwealth these are performed with clerical guidance, priests that bless beacons and hulls with words that speak of order under the Light, not gifts begged from the deep.

Private custom is older and franker. A bosun might tie a blue cord under the rail as a ship turns into the shear zone and whisper, "May Our Lady hold Her breath." A master keeps a carved cameo of Maregina in his footlocker, asking each time for a safe return. Knots are counted on a line as a low prayer; a coin is cast to lee for each hand lost to the sea after disaster. None of this is paraded; all of it is known. The tension between Right Habit and Right Action often frays in a storm; after a hard passage many a captain will light a candle at a chapel, pay a tithe for the poor, and speak to the confessor of any rash vows made to safe their vessel from an untimely wet grave.

## Notable Currents and Regions
### The Suurian Gates

^113153

The Suurian Gates are a long strait about 500 leagues from its western throat to its inner opening. At the "Gate of the Continents" in the furthest west, Rimec-Suur and An-Suur draw to barely 15 leagues shore to shore; from there the water widens eastward until it opens toward the Western Gyre in an inner bight 100-150 leagues across.

Coastal currents translate between the churn of the Circulean Gyres and the Open Ocean's swell and trades that press in from the west, but the two seas do not brawl so much as bargain. On most days the local currents win and pour seaward through the throat; on others the Ocean breathes in and the surface flow slackens while a quiet underflow steals outward beneath. The Rim of the Western Gyre does not reach here, lying well to the east past the counter-current of the Keyhole Isles, and the terrors of the maelstroms are a distant, foreboding tale that seasoned mariners tell young deckhands.

Navigation is civilized, with many light towers and wayhouses on both shores; numbered beacons mark the recommended angles of entry and exit; signal hills relay bell codes clear across the water on fog days.

### The Keystone Isles

^e95967

Between the Western Gyre and the Suurian Bight lies a chain of low islands and channels called the Keystone Isles. Here a regional counter‑clockwise loop turns between basin and bight, gathering water off the Western Rim's outer shoulder and feeding it toward the coastal currents that thread the Suurian Gates. On fair days the loop's outer arc runs steady north and west toward the Gates; the inner arc creeps back south between islands and the coast before it rejoins the Western Gyre, closing the circuit.

For mariners the Keystones offer a friendly push of less than three knots that ferries coasters and packets into the Bight; the inner return carries even less knots and is thick with workable eddies between passes. Shoals and tide rips bloom where channels pinch, so pilots read the water and time the slacks as if threading a river delta.

The Archduchy of Beniotti sits astride this motion. Its ports look both ways -- outward to the Gates and inward to the Gyre -- and its watchtowers sit where the loop quickens. In the Age of Corsairs the Archduchy's cutters used the Keystones to launch, hide, and return on schedule; tolls, convoy services, and pilotage made its coffers fat, and it retains a singularly maritime culture in its independence.

### The Broken Drift

East of Balboa the coast opens into a broken, island‑strewn bight. Here runs the Broken Drift, a slow but steady coastal companion to the Eastern Gyre's southbound rush. Roughly 480 leagues across at full breadth, the Drift is churned on by the Gyre's current along the archipelago's outer margin, and then folds back in a lacework of inner returns through passes and sounds along the coast of the Ertran peninsula, feeding water toward Balboa.

The Drift, they say, is a patient mistress. Its main set rarely tops two knots, but it runs day in, day out, lifting coasters, schoolers, and packet tenders along the island chain. The islet mazes break swell and wind, so pilotage is a craft of bearings, beacons, and slack windows rather than brute power. The worst of the ocean's temper lies screened behind the outer reefs.

Geology made the map here, and the Drift keeps rewriting it. The volcanic east of Ertra -- most active just south of the Drift -- throws up domes and cones; ashfall and pumice rafts ride the slow current and sort into bars and reefs; rocky islets grow where lava met water and held. The result is the Broken Islands, an embayment of hundreds of stacks and sounds whose lanes owe as much to Water's calming hand as to Fire's ire from below.

### The Rush

Between Sarm and the Cape of Balboa the two Gyres lean the same way, both running north to south and shouldering water toward the Ertrese Peninsula. Where their Rims draw close the flow gathers into a long tongue of fast water locals call the Rush. Foam lines and weed rafts sketch its convergences; the ring lies well seaward, but its pull can be felt in the outer streaks when the weather freshens.

Sailors approach the Rush with care, for here the Rims and their steady four knots tighten, with short pulses reaching speeds usually only encountered in a Ring. Ships bound for Balboa ride the inboard fan and make for the cape; those outward bound from the east slant across the Rush at slack and then settle onto the Western Gyre's Rim. Coasters here work the counter‑currents along the peninsular shore, fishing eddies under points and waiting on the bells before they commit to a crossing.

Day by day, the cape is written and re-written by the hydrology of the region. The Rush delivers river silt from the Merhal, desert dust from Sarm, traces of iron sands and pumice off Ertra's own fires, and together these form bars and spits that Balboa's dredging guilds must tame to keep the Sea Gate clear. But the same convergences also draw bait and plankton, so cliff colonies and fishing grounds track the tongue's edge as it folds along the coast. On clear days a haze often hangs above the line where the two lanes knit, a visible seam where Maregina's two great cycles reveal Her steady influence.
