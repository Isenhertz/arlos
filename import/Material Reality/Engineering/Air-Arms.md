The development of Circulean air-arms progressed in close rank with advancements in [[Alchemica]], particularly sealants.

Their history, once the principle had been proven sound, began with hand-cranked multi-stroke frontloaders as exemplified by the "Cortesia", a noble's affectation first found in [[Principality of Balboa|Balboa]], early in the 11th century: a duelling pistol, produced like jewellery and treated like porcellain. Its body is ivory, pearl, hardwood, lavishly chased brass, with a polished bronze barrel liner and steel in the valve seat, the striker pin, and a return spring that together cost more than most peasants will see in their lifetime. A folding crank on the right side drives a miniature piston pump, needing upwards of a dozen turns to produce one decent shot, a lead ball wrapped in oiled cloth rammed down the muzzle. The lock is not flint or wheel but a "tap" that lifts a metering needle from its seat, letting a measured burst of air into the bore that is soon called the "breath" of the weapon. It shoots accurately enough at duelling distances of a dozen [[Measurements|paces]], with all the charming unreliability of something new and highly fashionable. Noblemen, fortunately, are never put off by that sort of limitation.

By the 12th century, air-arms are no longer a noble's toy but in active military service, such as the "Boatswain's Tap". Widespread among ship officers during the Age of Corsairs, it is still hand-cranked, but the mechanism is now simplified and more rugged: fewer fine teeth, broader bearing surfaces, and seal packings designed to be replaced in the field. The biggest change, however, is in the valve train. Instead of a delicate needle, the tap uses a stout poppet valve with a steel-faced seat, and a regulated orifice plate that meters the breath more consistently from shot to shot. It remains a single-shot muzzle-loader: one decisive shot to finish an entangled boarding fight, to signal without smoke, or to put down a mutinous man at ten [[Measurements|poles]]' length without waking the rest. It is also sturdy enough you can smack it against a door frame or a recalcitrant's head and still have it function -- an attribute the earlier Cortesia never cultivated.

As boarding actions became more professional than romantic, the weapon that flourished was not the pistol but the short, brutal "Decksweeper" built around a detachable bottle slung under the stock -- a bulbous brass or bronze flask wrapped in lacquered cordage for hoop strength, fitted with a fast-couple neck that a sailor could change one-handed.

It fired a paper shell of lead pellets, sometimes mixed with jagged grit to encourage rapid surrender. Unlike the muzzle-loaders still in use as personal sidearms, the Decksweeper was breech-loaded, a simple hinged block with an oversized and oft replaced seal ring of pliant material. A steel locking cam took the load; everything else remained in bronze or brass. The result was a powerful, short-range "no thank you" ideal for gangplanks, alleyways, and the cramped indignity of desperate shipboard confrontations.

What marks its greatest contribution to the development of the larger family of air-arms was in its logistics: ports at the same time had already started to use compressed air in hoists and tools, and with pneumatics taking off it wasn't long before someone figured out how to use air as a consumable: bladders at first, but soon in metal reservoirs to be refilled at port. A crew with charged bottles could deliver repeated volleys without pumps, smoke, or setting the rigging on fire -- in other words, the sort of advantage that decides fights.

By the 14th century, valve-making had long stopped being artisanal wizardry. The "[[Vassals in Lavion#^d0991b|Mirevien]] Repeater" of the era was the first widely copied handgun capable of repeating fire without constant reloading, all through the marriage of a gravity-fed magazine and a much more precise metering valve. The magazine of the Repeater was a slim tube parallel to the barrel, feeding small lead balls down into a chamber when lifted upright. The heart of the gun was a clockwork-simple regulator: a spring-backed poppet and a fixed orifice that delivered a near-identical breath each shot until the reservoir pressure fell below a usable threshold, the signs of which gunners learned to feel approaching the way archers earlier learned the feel of a fraying bowstring. In terms of range it would comfortably hit targets at up to two chains' distance, and a good braced shot could carry up to twice as far.

Materials remained bronze and brass for the smoothbore body, with steel only in the heart. The Repeater did not become battlefield issue just yet, but it was a terrifying skirmish weapon and an assassin's delight: fast, mostly quiet, and not reliant on a man standing about between shots to pump like a penitent sentenced to firefighting duty. Like the Decksweeper before it, it integrated tightly into existing logistics, coupling to the growing pneumatic infrastructure of its time; and while its individual strengths were remarkable, its lasting influence was more in introducing standard fittings, regulated seals, and guild-enforced threads for standardized, truly interchangeable reservoir bottles.

The material revolution of the 15th century -- alumen becoming available at usable cost -- predictably went first to the reservoir, where weight matters and pressure resistance is key. The "Post-Gun" first seen in [[Commonwealth of Principalities|Commonwealth]] garrisons was built around an alumen bottle with a brass neck and steel seat, designed to be topped off at a garrison air post and swapped in seconds. The gun itself was long-barrelled for efficiency and accuracy, with a simple breech that accepted a paper-wrapped ball or, among elite companies, a dart launched from a metal sabot cup. Some variants even used a thin steel liner, but the bore stayed smooth. Still, range improved with better sealants, allowing for massed shots at targets up to five [[Measurements|chains]] away, with individual marksmen reliably hitting as far away as six.

Tactically, the Post-Gun was a wall gun meant for disciplined volleys from fortifications and prepared positions. The bottle system made it economical to train for quickly changing reservoir, almost more so than reloading -- a trend helped by magazines rapidly extending from 6 to 12 or even 24 shots. It was also a weapon you could not field without an entire backdrop of readily available pressure infrastructure, inspectors, and at least one very dull person in the company who took gauge readings very seriously.

When [[Kingdom of Lavion|Lavion]] went through its Imperial Turn, its standard issue was the "Virellic Model 3", a disciplined, reservoir-fed repeater longarm with a robust regulator. The firing cycle of the Model 3 was designed around reliability on campaign -- oversized seal rings, easy access to the valve block, and a regulator that could be swapped as a unit. Steel, though still rationed, increasingly appeared as the bore liner. With sealants improving further, range again leapt forward as pressure increased: a hundred [[Measurements|poles]] became standard engagement range, with specialized pieces capable of hitting enemy officers almost a third of a [[Measurements|league]] away.

The main advancement of the Model 3, however, was not so much in its own form, but in what ammunition it fired. While the standard patterns continued to use a tube magazine, lead balls actually began to fall out of favor by the turn of the 16th century. Instead, ammunitions began to feature darts more and more, though the metal sabot cups of the Post-Gun era were replaced by pliant materials used as obturating sleeves around a flechette, peeling away upon leaving the barrel. With this advancement in munitions, more and more variants started to feature magazines of such sleeved flechettes, which were more easily stackable, more tolerant towards rough handling and chambering than soft lead, and small enough that fashioning them with steel tips became the norm.

A last, parallel development was the spread of air cartridge systems beginning in the late 15th century. These were weapons using pre-charged reusable "capsules", marrying a single-use charge of breath to a sleeved flechette, creating a shell usable independently of the standard bottle reservoir infrastructure. The tolerances were unforgiving, the capsules expensive, but they could be fired from any sort of tube they fit into and with a simple hammer; small wonder that they mainly saw use among saboteurs, the aeronavalier corps, and criminal elements.
