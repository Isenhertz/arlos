Alchemy in the lands about the Circle Sea began as the craft of making things bind, harden, or dissolve at will. Long before [[Aether|aetheric theory]] or [[Scalarism|Scalarist]] analysis, early alchemists were defined by their mastery of gums, glues, spirits and varnishes. They supplied shield-makers, shipwrights, scribes and masons with preparations that resisted water, brightened colour, or made fragile things endure. Over the centuries, this empirical craft matured into a disciplined science of the alchemica, a broad family of organic and mineral-based materials whose properties could be tuned with some precision. By the time of the aetheric revolution, alchemists were no longer merely boiling resins and bones; they were designing specific pliants, insulators and foams for pneumatics, armour, aetheric coils and flight.

The earliest period is defined by the classic gums. Animal glues derived from hide, bone, skin or fish were the first reliable binders. They adhered strongly to porous substrates and were crucial to early woodwork, lamellar harness and bookmaking, but failed quickly in damp. Alongside them, tree resins and pitches, clarified by heat and hardened with ash or mineral powders, provided the first water-shedding coatings. When blended with drying oils from seeds and nuts, these resins could be made less brittle and more suitable for laminating shields, paving temple floors, or sealing hull seams. The development of distillation added solvent-spirits -- vine-spirit, wood-spirit and similar -- which could carry gums and oils as thin films. Early varnishes and sealants, brushed on as liquid and cured to a continuous skin, arose from this combination of gums and spirits. This classical phase of alchemy was dominated by the choice of which glue, which resin, and which spirit to employ for a particular task.

[[Reme]] refined these practices into more rigorous structural chemistry. Its alchemists produced stone-bound gums -- resins and oils extended and hardened with finely ground mineral powders and hot lime. In Earth terms these were early crosslinked polymers, but to contemporary workers they were simply gums that hardened more fully, resisted reheating, and clung tenaciously to wood, stone and bronze. They enabled deep-bellied shields faced with metal to behave as single plates, kept large hulls tight on long campaigns, and consolidated friable masonry. Once cured, such gums did not soften again; they charred or cracked if abused. After the fall of Reme, its recipes survived only patchily in guild traditions, yet the concept of hot lime gums and stone-bound laminants remained central to major works of fortification and shipbuilding.

As warfare and river-borne trade intensified, alchemists turned increasingly to flexible coatings. Harness and armour hung on webbing of linen or hemp, at first treated with simple oils and waxes. To reduce rot without making the webbing stiff and brittle, artisans began to experiment with under-cured resins and the saps of latex-bearing plants. By blending tree resins, drying oils and so-called milk-gums from certain tropical trees, and carefully limiting heat and mineral additions, they produced soft gums that set without fully hardening. These materials formed tough, leathery films that adhered deeply to fibres, shed water and tolerated repeated bending. They are recognised in later scholarship as the first true pliants. They transformed armour webbing, rigging and harness, and were soon adopted in grenades and alchemical vessels as gels and binders for caustic mixtures.

The growth of seaborne power in the Age of Corsairs drove further differentiation. Naval yards began to speak not simply of gum, but of hull gums, rope gums, jack coats and glass coats, each with distinct recipes and expected behaviours. *Hull gums* formed moderately yielding yet waterproof seams that could work with the hull without cracking. *Rope gums* were softer milk-gum mixtures that penetrated rope bundles and locked fibres together while permitting bending. *Jack coats* were intermediate pliants used between layers of textile or lamellae, spreading impact and preventing tears; they were formulated to remain slightly yielding within the thickness of the armour. *Glass coats* were clear or pigmented varnishes polished to reduce fouling and sun damage. In effect, shipwrights and armourers had begun to treat alchemica as matrix materials for early fibre composites.

The spread of pneumatic infrastructure in the late Dark and early Aetheric ages created a demand for airtight seals, as air mains, accumulator vessels and regenerator engines all suffered from leaks and fatigue all too common in ordinary gums. Pressure alchemica answered this need. They were based on milk-gums and soft resins, but loaded with very fine mineral fillers to reduce porosity, applied in multiple thin coats with controlled heating, and tailored to withstand cycling between hot and cold zones. The result was a class of relatively dense, moderately flexible materials with low gas permeability. These were used as gasket faces between pipe flanges, as linings for bellows and diaphragms in regulators and pumps, and as internal coatings for pipes and tanks where condensation would otherwise pit metal. Without these pressure-pliants, city-scale pneumatic systems and long-lived choir engines would not have been feasible.

As experimenters began to work more systematically with Fulminance and Tension, a further class of alchemica emerged: the insulars. These materials were designed to resist the passage of both heat and aetheric charge. Building on stone-bound gums, but driven further toward glassy structures, insulars formed hard, dry, non-conductive layers that did not flow or char readily under stress. They were applied as coil varnishes on wires and windings and then baked, cast as bushings and spacers to keep metallic parts apart, and extruded as protective coats over braided conductors. Their weakness was brittleness; they cracked if forced to bend. For flexible applications, alchemists used them as thin outer skins over more forgiving pliants or textiles.

Alongside these, structural foams and cores appeared in the aetheric revolution. Engineers discovered that by vigorously mixing air into certain resin-oil blends with suitable fillers and then curing them in closed moulds, they could produce rigid foams: low-density materials with good compressive strength but modest tensile capacity. Sandwiched between skins of metal or hardwood, these foams served as light, stiff cores for decks, pavises and airship gondola floors. They improved insulation, reduced weight, and damped vibration. Such cores were particularly important in aerial craft, where every reduction in mass was valuable and where composite floors and control surfaces had to withstand constant stress.

Parallel to the development of foams, artisans advanced the design of stressed-skin pliants and armour webs. These reinforced skins for balloons and lifting cells combined tightly woven cloth with pressure-pliant impregnation and gas-tight insular topcoats, and carried much of the structural load in stressed-skin spars and wing surfaces while also retaining lifting gas. Armourers and engineers began specifying particular brands and grades of pliant, often associated with renowned workshops or academies, to achieve consistent behaviour. Complex curing schedules, with defined temperatures and sequences of coats, became standard practice.

Finally, the rise of aetheric devices and mechanical aids demanded reliable elastics for machine tendons and diaphragms. These alchemica were heavily based on refined milk-gums and certain plant extracts, which enhanced endurance while preserving elasticity. Properly formulated and cured, such elastics could stretch repeatedly with minimal permanent deformation, making them suitable for artificial muscles in aethermatons, prosthetic joints, valve diaphragms and precision regulators. They slowly hardened and lost resilience with age, but their initial performance far surpassed ordinary leather or untreated plant rubber.

Behind all these solid forms lay an equally important family of liquid alchemica: the solvents, thinners and cleaning spirits that enabled gums and pliants to be applied, reworked and maintained. Vine-spirit, wood-spirit, turpentine-like oils and citrus spirits were essential to every workshop dealing with alchemica. Control of their production, trade and taxation became a quiet but significant element of statecraft in realms that relied heavily on aetheric engineering, pneumatics and composite armour.

From the first boiling pots of hide glue and pine resin to the graded, branded pliants of the aetheric revolution, the history of alchemy around the Circle Sea is in large part a history of learning how to harden, soften, flex and insulate matter at will. As scalarist theory matured, these practices moved from closely guarded recipes and guild secrets into a more systematic understanding of how elemental shares could be balanced to produce predictable mechanical behaviour. The resulting alchemica underpinned the revolution of modern Arlosian engineering, serving wherever materials had to bond, seal, bear load, carry pressure, confine charge, or move with controlled elasticity.
