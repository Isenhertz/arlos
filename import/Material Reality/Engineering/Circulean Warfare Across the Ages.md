## Through-line: bronze first, steel where it decides the day

Steel is always rare and expensive; bronze and brass are common. Thus by necessity the mass of tools and arms is the latter, with steel reserved for decisive organs -- springs, triggers, pawls, rifled inserts, bearing pins, and thin edge or wedge work that changes outcomes. Pneumatics and aetheric engines favor bronze for valves, manifolds, and wet service; steel lives where stored energy, elasticity, or precise timing is essential. This material economy shapes every doctrine that follows.

## The Remean Era (11th century BR to 1st AR)

[[Remean Empire|Remean]] war moves on individual and communal perfection. [[Shaping]] makes the soldier the engine: on land their formations close and uncoil with uncanny regularity, while at sea their mighty polyremes are a concert rather than a cacophony. Signature ranged arms are belly-bows of a draw weight that only Shaped bodies could possibly arm them, and heavy torsion frames for ships, walls, and field parks. A few tuned stone-lances appear for long, uncanny shots of tremendous power, but such art does not survive the Fall. When Shaping is proscribed, the weapon grammar that presumed a perfected user retires with it; the surviving artifacts become curiosities and templates for later reverse-engineering rather than living doctrine.

In melee and harness the legions favor cast bronze musculata and scale for their officers and early alchemica linothorakes for the line, all with broad greaves and high-cheek helmets, every edge work-hardened along rivet lines. Sarissas and thrusting pila do the decision work, while short leaf-blades ride as sidearms. Shields are deep-bellied boards faced in bronze and bound with alchemica gums that keep the laminae tight in weather. Steel is present only as needles, pins, and a champion's speartip where a hard bite matters; the weight and volume remain bronze. The overall effect is disciplined shock and hold with a kit built for a thalassocratic power, forgiving fatigue and salt.

## The Age of Enlightenment (1st to 3rd centuries)

With shaping forbidden and orthodoxy ascendant, arsenals pivot to what any unaugmented human can mass and maintain. Slings, javelins, laminated flatbows where craft allows, compact torsion arbalests in towers and alleys, and siege engines of rope and timber define the fighting day. Fire is leashed by rite and practice, so chemistry serves as smoke, quicklime, and pitch rather than powder. Talent flows into order, fortification, and civic logistics.

Melee regresses to sturdy simplicity. City levies carry spears, boat-hooks, and bills that can be hafted from wharf stock; sidearms are bronze hangers with occasional iron or steel axes. Helmets become plain bronze bowls with leather liners and a steel brow strap; jacks are layered linen, most often glued to form a shell that can distribute a blow, sometimes quilted around small bronze squares to form early jack-of-plates. Pavises rise with the crossbow -- tall boards faced in bronze and sealed with gums -- and tower shields return to prominence in street fights. Cavalry keep light bronze scale over rawhide and trade the lance's crush for javelins and pursuit.

## The Dark Ages (4th to 9th centuries)

Doctrine is civic and riverine. Levies carry darts and boat-javelins that double for boarding. Garrison lines favor tension crossbows with laminated laths for reliability; wealthier gates and towers keep torsion frames for confined venues. Early grenades are unsafe jars of smoke, gels, and caustics rather than powder pots. Laminated flatbows thrive, greatbows rule the arc. Strategy is positional and attritional; surprise lies on water with oars, hooks, and nets. Nobles begin to employ pneumatic duelling pistols -- toys that take upwards of a dozen pumps for a single shot accurate only to a few paces, but foreshadowing the rapid advancement of air-arms that is to follow.

Armor craft matures into lamellar and scale on stout webbing, with jacks of small bronze plates quilted into cloth for those who cannot afford full harness. Helmets widen into kettle and brimmed forms to shed missiles from walls and boats. Pike and bill companies dominate, their bronze heads toughened by heavy peening, with steel splitter wedges where a decisive bite wins the clinch. River troops prize cutlasses and boarding axes with thin steel edge slips brazed onto bronze bodies. The era's watchwords are coverage and repairability -- everything can be re-riveted at a wharf forge.

## The Age of Corsairs (10th to 13th centuries)

Trade booms, raiders follow, and navies become policy made timber. Two introductions bend the era. First, the arcannon -- an aetheric impulse thrower reminiscent of the stone-lance of the Remeans -- takes pride of place on walls and ships, steadily displacing petraries from the center of decision while leaving them roles for lobbing alchemica, carcass work, and counter-battery duties. Second, air-arms -- still fussy hand-pumps -- steadily improve into credible soldiery arms over the course of the era. Doctrine is simple: long-range engagements between arcannons, fouling of rigging and decks via slung alchemica, then in the actual boarding large-bore pneumatic "decksweepers" oppose boarding parties seeking to close with hook and blade.

Melee and armor adapt to wet work. Boarding harness is rarely more than bronze scale, more often simple jacks laminated with oil resins that shed brine; gloves and greaves are ribbed for rope handling. Helmets are brimmed bronze pots with padded napes; visors are rare at sea but brow straps in steel are routine. Cutlasses and boarding bills carry brazed steel edge slips or narrow wedge inserts; grapnels and hooks get steel points even when the shanks are bronze. On land, pike-and-pavise blocks anchor approaches to ports while fast billmen and buckler pairs exploit breaches. Cavalry stay light for scouting, harassment and pursuit, wearing bronze scale with mail patches where flex matters most.

## The Golden Age of Aetherics (13th to 15th centuries)

Cities' pneumatics mature into full-blown infrastructure; armies soon learn to lean onto that backbone. Arcannons further standardize, prompting new crew drills, calibers, and carriages; airgun lines emphasize marksmanship, maintenance, and quiet maneuver, introducing pre-charged reservoirs to replace the strenuous handpumps of earlier arms. Specialist marksmen are issued exquisitely tuned miniaturized aetherbalests derived from arcannon principles to reach where airguns cannot. Early air-arm repeaters enter the scene, but overall this category remains a close-range affair, rarely reaching beyond 5 chains for aimed mass fire, so doctrine adapts: assault teams pair shields with grenade slings; defense focuses on the mastery of overlapping arcs of fire.

Harness becomes laminated engineering. Line infantry wear fine bronze scale or lamellar on alchemica webbing, case-bonded with pliants that spread shock and arrest tears; officers add hidden steel ridge frames for rib protection. Pavises and tower shields are fundamental to anchor the line against the opposing side's hail of bullets, and rapidly mature into composite sandwich builds -- bronze facing, pliant core, timber back -- with bronze shoes and shoulders for ground setting. Standard sidearms are still short bronze hangers or seaxes; as armor matures further, steel-point hammers make some inroads. Heavy cavalry reappear as shock wings in selected polities, their bronze barding laced to leather with mail gussets at joints, and their lances tipped with steel wedges; the mass of the kit remains bronze to suit the supply train.

## The 1420s inflection

Organization finally matches tools. Feudal musters remain in law, but standing soldiery largely replaces their impact. Commissions open to any cadets who can read a map and a gauge. Fleet choreography presumes cutters with arcannon swivels, paddle-driven dreadnaughts with 36-pound broadsides, and tacticians look at the burgeoning [[Arlosian Aeronautics#^2ab1b6|aeronave]] industry and begin to wonder about flying artillery pieces.

Issue patterns fix the melee and harness grammar. Cohorts receive standardized bronze scale harness with repair kits of rivets, webbing, and resin sticks; inspection cycles include peening edges and re-bonding laminations. Pike, bill, and short hanger are the triad of close work; steel allotments are booked to bill beaks, hanger edge slips, mail patches for armpits, and the small springs and pawls that make arbalests and airguns cycle. Helmets gain interchangeable bronze face-bars and removable padded aventails to suit street, wall, or field. The watchword is serviceability -- every piece can be stripped and mended at the company forge. The big change in material science is the advent of affordable alumen, which immediately revolutionizes reservoir technology, making air-arm bottles lighter while simultaneously enabling ever higher pressures.

## The Imperial Age (16th century)

Empire points the refined toolkit at people. Airships, long seaworthy gondolas beneath harmonized envelopes, become fighting platforms that marry long durations of loiter with downward-facing arcannon swivels. Heavier-than-air flying boats enter service and immediately become a vessel of air superiority, the fighters vying for control of the skies around the slow airship bombers. Glider crews bring the marines airborne, and boarding and breach in three dimensions call for new weaponry. The Discharge lance enters into service -- short-reach Fulminance throwers whose coils demand rite and whose manuals read like penance.

On the ground and in the yards, constructs first change the economy, then the nature of force. Fluidic logic, pressure braids, and pocket expanders that once powered prosthetics and shop helpers are adapted for war. Brassbacks that never sleep soon march in lockstep, their instruction slates carrying orders they execute without question. Ministries and chartered companies write doctrine to these realities. Strategically, the state favors glittering standing armies, humming fleets, and a civic religion redirected to crown. Tactically, it pays in blood whenever Fire, Fulminance, or Volatility are treated as tame. The century’s signature is jingoism backed by engineering and accountancy, and the cost is tallied on the quay and in the ward.

Melee adapts to air and brassbacks alike. Air-marines wear light bronze scale over padded suits with insulating inner layers against discharge, gripping the decks with hooked soles and fighting with short hangers, boarding axes, and insulated hooks shaped to catch rigging and shear valve lines. Shock troops on the ground carry breaker bills with hooks sized to yank a brassback’s limbs; sapper masks in bronze and mica guard against spirit sprays. Urban formations field tower shields with bronze faces and viewing slots, paired with truncheon-bills for crowd control and anti-construct leverage. Officers and champions carry steel where it truly decides -- a narrow rapier or estoc with a steel core and bronze shell, or a back-sword with a brazed steel edge plate -- but even here the rationing is strict. Armor remains a laminate: bronze outside for weather and arc-shedding, pliant cores for shock, and hidden steel ridge frames where bones need saving.
