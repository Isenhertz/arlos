## A Cautionary Tale told of Ancient Times

*When the Circle Sea was young and the Gyres had not yet tasted the blood of men, there rose in the land of Mer and Hal a man who coveted power before all else. What name his mother gave him we do not know, for all we know of him we know from the sunken steles and cracked tiles proclaiming his rule: Emôn-Dju-Bâ-Mhâhat-Nmu, First-and-Only, immortal sorcerer-king of all he could survey, sovereign of souls, who subjugated the spirit of the Night Jaguar and made its Alvai Kin his own. None could stand before him and spite him; all the world prostrated itself at the base of his ziggurat of obsidian and marble, to bleed in tribute to his glory.*

*It is said that as his dominion over the earth grew, the First-And-Only looked to the sky and felt jealous of the horizon; and the ancients whisper that in a rite of the impurest kind, of blood and sacrifice and sacrilege, he perfected what he had performed when he broke the Night Jaguar -- but this time, his aim was for the Noble Wind herself, to shackle her to his side and thereby claim the heavens as he did the earth.*

*They say the First-And-Only raised a temple so high that it tempted the horizon. In its heart he stacked seven terraces of glassy black stone, each faced with mirrors that bent the sky like a yoke. He there performed a rite of the impurest kind, wherein the lungs of a legion men and women were turned into skin bladders to bottle their breath for unholy trumpets, where the ribs of a legion more were flayed and planed into flute-bones and set to sing in their agony. He named this temple his Ladder, and the winds rose as if to inspect it. Priests that walked among the blood in robes of unstained white cut lines upon the marble and obsidian and seeded them with salt so the air could only find a single place to sit. Then he spoke his own secret name and showed the sky his mirrors.*

*The Wind, who in those times had no name yet, came in glory and contempt, for the queen of tempests refuses all crowns: she descended not to kneel but to cast down the affront that glittered up at her. There is no note in Lur's world meant to bear the cacophonic harmony the First-And-Only had wrought; yet the Ladder chimed with it. So she laughed like a squall on taut canvas, and made to undo the works, but he was as wise as he was cruel, and on his command the breath-trumpets cried, and the rib-flutes sang the sealing song, and the mirrors crossed like a net, to let the First-And-Only cast his brazen collar on the Noble Wind, naming her, and in doing so binding her. Thus did the very sky come to know the yoke as had the earth before her.*

*Bound, she raged. For seven long lifetimes her winds shook the bones of her prison, but her cage was of no earthly make, and she could not undo what had been placed on her. Using her power as he had everything he claimed for himself, the First-And-Only raised his Ladder into the heavens, to watch over his dominion from his terraces of blood and stone, with oiled feet and a cup of wine, and called himself everlasting sovereign of sky and soul. He threw bread to his Hatekin to see them kneel; he threw men to them to see them feed. He took Set-Ahotep-Imekh to his side as lover and as hand, and set him to count the dead. The lieutenant did not falter, for his name was a scythe and his hand the haft of it, and the people called him executioner and adjutant to the immortal.*

*But in the deep watches between moonrise and dawn, when the cords of stolen breath slackened and the flutes remembered they were bones, the Wind leaned from her brazen collar and spoke to the man. She spoke in the language of draft and leak, in the gossip of eaves and alleys, in the whisper that carries a name through a closed door. She did not plead. She revealed secrets, for the wind can carry a secret farther than any runner. And with these secrets Set-Ahotep-Imekh furthered the reign of the First-And-Only, but with each secret the Wind shared the heart of Set-Ahotep-Imkeh hardened against his better, and he began to covet what his lover held. He kissed the First-And-Only with a mouth that promised forever and a hand that weighed the pommel of his knife.*

*On the night of the river's highest laughter, when Mer and Hal broke their banks and wrote new edges upon the land, he rose. He cut the throats of the trumpeters to end their endless note of blood and pain. He shattered the flute-bones of the damned choir, and cast the mirrors that formed the bars of Morwynt's cage into the waters below. The Ladder sighed and came apart like a story told by a liar when truth arrives. The wind fell upon the ziggurat in stripes of pale cloud and dark rain, and the First-And-Only reached for his mile-long name and found only the first syllable left to him.*

*Upon the pinnacle of the ziggurat, the winds rising as the shackles of their prison came undone, Set-Ahotep-Imekh unkinged the unkillable and offered his body to the river, and the river took it as if it were nothing more than a stone. They called him by a new name, Breaks-The-Crown, because names are the oldest tools, and he used his well.*

*Some say the Ladder smote into the lands below, its unholy nature damning the earth into ceaseless motion in the once lush lands of Lu-Pto that are now a sifted, seiving place. Others say the Ladder's stumps still stand at Omt-î-Thes, where later Lesser Kings whispered they could do better, that they could imprison the wind with rites of stone rather than ones of blood and bone. The river remembers, in any case, and so does the sky.* 

*As for Breaks-The-Crown, let us say nothing more, because the lesson is in the unmaking of a king, and not in the man who unmade him.*

*When Morwynt tore free she did not take her shackles off. Pride is not why -- for Air has none of the pride of Fire -- but memory is. She girded herself with her own insult, a collar bright as lightning and heavy as thunder, so that all who look up will see and think twice before believing they could ever command the wind again. Having seen her captor thrown into the waves, she searched everywhere for a sign of his body, that she might find closure; but the First-And-Only evaded her even in death.*

*Furious at his gall and frightened of his possible return, she gathered riders to herself from the cliff edges and the storm fronts, tall and translucent as rain drawn like ink, laughter sharp as a squall, cruel when crossed -- the Windwalkers, heralds and huntsmen of her retinue. With them she rose into a canter, and that canter has never ended. They are the Wild Hunt, and they hunt the shade of the king that shackled their queen. They ride high in the sky, over mountain spires and ship masts, they slip their reins to chase the sun and the long fetch of the sea, they whistle horses into panic and carry sailors home on a breath when they are kind. And when the air falls silent and the leaves hold still as if listening, it is because somewhere the Hunt has struck a trail.*

*So I warn you thus: bind the wind and you will teach her to track you. Think only of lofty heights and you find the very sky come to cast you down. Even now Morwynt rides, bright collar at her throat, not as a slave but as a stalker of her prey. She ranges the ridgelines and the gyres, to hunt the shade of Emôn-Dju-Bâ-Mhâhat-Nmu wherever it settles in men who covet more than is their right, and she will ride until the last echo of his name comes to ground and lies still at her dancing feet. Lightning is her sign, the thunder her command; in the hush after, you can hear the Wild Hunt's laughter as they give chase.*
