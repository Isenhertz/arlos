> # Beniotti (Grand Duchy)
> ![[crest Archduchy of Beniotti.png|Crest]]
>Crest:  *Ermine with a bordure gules, a trefoil argent edged gules over a bar of the same*
>Capital: *Berler, Isle of Galin*

The Grand Duchy of Beniotti sits upon the [[Circle Sea#^e95967|Keystone Isles]], a broken arc of high, dry islands that lean from the northerwestern coasts of Lavion towards the Suurian Bight. The archipelago of five major and several minor islands lies north of [[Kingdom of Lavion|Lavion]] and northeast of the [[Stranu|Stranic]] coast, on that stretch where the Western Gyre brushes the Gates, and it is defined less by land than by that motion. Around the Keystones runs a quiet coastal gyre of their own, a counter-clockwise loop that draws water in from the Western Rim, carries it past the outer islands, and spills it back towards the Suurian Bight and the Gate. The outer limb of this loop offers a friendly push of a few knots to ships bound to or from the Suurian Gate without forcing them into harsher waters; the inner limb is slower, thick with workable eddies between the passes, where pilotage is a matter of reading tides and shoals.

Beniotti sits literally astride this motion. Its harbors look both ways -- inward to the disciplined circulation of the Western Gyre, outward to the long swell and unscheduled weather of the High Sea. Watchtowers crown the saddles of the two largest islands, their lamps and semaphore arms marking not only reefs and passes but the state of the Gate: whether the ocean swell is running fair, whether the seasonal storm tracks favour an outward crossing, and whether corsairs, crusaders, or Commonwealth companies are currently making a nuisance of themselves.

The land itself is modest, ridged limestone and old volcanic plugs, with thin soil that favours olives, hardy vines, and scrub. Rain is a winter visitor, collected in cisterns much like on the Stranic mainland; wells quickly run brackish with seepage and have to carefully lined or reinforced with [[Hexcrafting|hexes]]. Agriculture happens mostly on terraces, but the islands live by sea more than soil. The capital, Berler, clings to a long north-facing bay on the Isle of Galin whose floor drops steeply enough to let ocean-going vessels lie at anchor almost under the city walls, while lighter coasters and fishing boats crowd the shallows between the piled stone piers further out.

### Anish roots and crusader harbors

Like much of the Western Gyre's north shore, the Keystones were first settled by the Anish, who chose the Isles for what they always were: a natural stepping stone between both the Suurian continents as well as the Circle Sea and the Open Ocean, difficult to take by storm and easy to supply by water. Early stone works still stand on the landward sides of the best anchorages -- squat towers with slit windows and stepped platforms that once held early petraries and beacon fires.

In the last centuries before [[Reme]]'s fall and the first of the [[Church of the Light#^97845f|Age of Enlightenment]], the Keystones became a forward base for crusading fleets. Lurist forces pressing south against Remean outposts used the islands as staging harbors, storehouses, and repair yards, knowing that Reme's vast polyremes hated the broken water and narrow passes. Later hagiographers speak of the Keystones as "keys against the heathen", but the old foundations in the outer harbors are strikingly similar to old Empire ruins, telling of the Isles' previous occupants.

When Reme burned, and then again when the Hierophancy shattered, the garrisons thinned. The Keystones did not. The islanders kept their walls and yards and quietly made their own arrangements. For a time there were bishops and banners enough to claim the Isles as direct possessions of this or that Patriarchate. The locals learned early to smile and ring the bells until visiting prelates were comfortably on to the next boat, without ever yielding their own customs.

### Svenic winter and Balboan summer

In the 6th century AR the Isles tasted something closer to conquest. [[Svenu|Svenic]] raiding fleets had for generations been harrying the coasts of Stranu and northern Lavion; now several clans arrived at the Keystones bruised, exhausted, looking for somewhere the winter could not kill them outright. They found that from here they had Paltos and the Cavrel Sound to raid in the south, the coast of An-Suur in the north, and the Bight to the west. For a generation they made the outer islands their winter ground, holding the high ridges and harrying trade.

The story told in the Isles has it that the locals did not beat the raiders in the field so much as in the bed: Svenic captains took local wives, learned the currents, and discovered that a harbor that feeds you was preferable to a fjord that merely fails to kill you. In the end the Isles became many-tongued: Anish in the oldest place-names, Svenic in curses and family names, Lavienese in liturgy, and, for reason we will soon see, Balboan in everyday life. As descendants of those raiders the islanders still keep iron amulets and winter feasts that make Lavienese clergy purse their lips, but they sail under the Duchy's colours.

The second near-loss came from the east. In the late 9th century, at a time when Balboa's famous "Five Generations of Expansion" had just been curtailed by the horrendous loss against Gaviatolis and the Commonwealth had not yet consolidated its coastal councils, a Balboan merchant-prince of the house Beniotti secured charters, debts, and eventually enough hired blades to "rescue" the Isles from a period of disputed succession; a rescue that involved a good deal of burning things down and the abrupt extinction of several island lineages. His reward was the title of Archduke of the Keystone Isles and effective control over their harbors. The dynastic name stuck, and in time the land took its ruler's name: first the Archduchy of Beniotti, now the Grand Duchy.

Balboan law, coin, and counting-houses followed. The Isles acquired finances to rival the Commonwealth's, notarised contracts, and a certain type of lawyer who could argue, with straight faces, that a coastal toll was a canonical duty rather than a tax. The merchant-prince himself rarely left his terrace above the port, but his factors and captains spread through the archipelago, bringing with them Ertrese building habits and the Balboan taste for precise accounts.

### The Age of Corsairs and the rise of the Grand Duchy

The Age of Corsairs turned Beniotti from a provincial prize into a Circulean player. Trade swelled, raiders followed, and the Keystones became the staging ground for both. The Commonwealth's sexagesimal Guilder became the coin of harbors across the Western Gyre, with Beniotti and Balboa rising as twin contenders for naval supremacy, as Lavion lagged in maritime consolidation. Beniotti's cutters learned to ride their home currents hard: out along the swift outer limb to meet convoys or prey, in along the inner limb to slip back to harbor between dawn and noon bell.

Archducal policy during those centuries was a simple, ruthless calculus: toll what you can, escort what pays, ignore what you cannot catch, and never be caught openly aligning with anyone too long. Commonwealth factors, Lavienese chartered companies, and Velkirian mutuals all kept counting-houses in the capital. Each was convinced, on the strength of carefully drafted compacts, that Beniotti leaned primarily toward them.

When King Lurvric of Lavion moved in the mid-13th century to centralise his realm's maritime power and impose order on the motley fleets of the Circle Sea, Beniotti's courtiers saw the axe coming. They answered with ceremony. The Archduke unilaterally elevated himself to a "Grand Duke", arguing that the Isles were too far from Lavion's shores to sit comfortably within its vassal ladder, yet too vital to be ignored. The new style was accepted de facto if not de jure in Virell, and much more warmly embraced in Santra, where anything that complicated Lavion's map was generally approved of.

From that bargain grew the status quo of the Aetheric Age, wherein the Grand Duchy forms as neutral a hinge as can exist in the world. Lavion patrols the approaches to the southern shores; Commonwealth squadrons police their merchant lanes; Beniotti keeps the books of who passes the Gates and under what colours.

### Government: the Grand Duke and the Anchors

Formally, Beniotti is a hereditary monarchy. The Grand Duke of Beniotti styles himself "Lord of the Keystones and Guardian of the Gate", and traces his line back to the Balboan merchant-prince who first welded the islands into a single polity. In practice the Grand Duke rules only in concert with his Anchors, the island council that holds the purse-strings and pilot-books.

The Council of Anchors is a hybrid body. Each of the major islands sends its "Anchor" -- theoretically a landed noble, in reality often the dominant ship-owning clan of that island. To these are added the Captains-Major of the convoy fleets, the Primarch of the High Seas Church, and a delegate from each of the three great chartered guilds: the Pilots' Guild, the Tollkeepers' Guild, and the Guild of Factors and Contracted Correspondents. Together they vote harbour ordinances, customs rates, convoy schedules, and ship levies.

Beniotti's law is a patchwork sewn into coherence by long habit. Port and harbor law looks Stranic on the page for borrowing heavily from Commonwealth statutes on guild deliberation and mutual liability, yet it cites Lavienese precedent in matters of charter, inheritance, and noble rank. Disputes between foreign merchants are often tried under their own consular courts; anything that threatens the Duchy's revenue or neutrality is tried under the Grand Duke's eye, usually with the Primarch and the senior Anchor in attendance.

Foreign policy is conducted by treaty, corridor, and exception. Beniotti holds separate compacts with Lavion, the Commonwealth, and Vel Kiriya, each of which recognises the Isles as neutral pilot and convoy provider at the Suurian Gate. In return the Duchy undertakes to bar pirates and warships from its harbors, at least in theory. In practice, amnesties are regular, definitions are flexible, and the Anchors value a coin in the hand over a diplomatic promise.

### Faith: the Church of the High Seas

Religiously, the Keystones carry more weight than their acreage would suggest. The [[Church of the Light#^d5a57f|Autonomous Church of the High Seas]], the youngest of the Lurist communions, is seated here, raised from the chaplaincies that followed caravels and sloops into the Rim during the Age of Corsairs. Its Primate, popularly called the Sea-Father, holds his cathedra in an old crusader's church above the main harbor: a squat building of imposing but not exactly inviting features whose nave looks inward to the city, and whose west doors open directly onto the Pilgrims' Quay.

High Seas rite tunes its calendar to winds and water. Feast days mark the opening and closing of the cyclone season beyond the Gates, the turning of the Western Rim, and the fortnight in early autumn when the ocean swell. The Church licenses ship-chaplains, keeps orphan houses for foundlings and shipwrecked children, and issues "pilgrimage charts" that double as remarkably reliable sailing directions for captains venturing beyond the Suurian Gate.

In doctrine, High Seas answers to the Patriarchate of the Varsa as court of appeal, and lies within the broad bounds of Orthodoxy. In practice it is a practical, maritime rite with a long habit of looking the other way at small syncretisms so long as tithes are paid and shelters are kept in repair. Even Kharunite notions of recognizing the Light in all elements find a quiet home here, brought west by Vel Kiriyan captains, as do older Anish and Svenic customs of tossing bread and oil to Maregina's servants at dangerous narrows.

The Grand Duke is formally "Lay Protector of the High Seas Church" and funds its chapels and hospice houses. The Sea-Father in turn generally supports the Duchy's neutrality, reminding foreign prelates that without Beniotti's lights and pilots, many of their own missionaries would never come home.

### Society, tongue, and everyday life

Beniotti is a place where few families can count more than two generations of ancestors buried in the same soil. Anish fisher clans, Svenic lineages with their vowel-heavy names, Balboan merchant houses, Stranic factors, Ukili'ata sailing families, and Velkirian agents all jostle along the same quays. The street chatter of the capital is a patter of three counting systems: the old Anish dozenals, the Lavienese sacred numerals, and the sexagesimal numeracy of Stranu and Vel Kiriya, carried in the Commonwealth's Guilder and Velkirian contracts.

With men and women both at sea for weeks, domestic arrangements are usually quite pragmatic. Property and ship-shares are commonly recorded in the names of mothers and sisters as well as fathers and sons, a habit some attribute to lingering Svenic matrilineal customs, others to pure self-interest. The law recognises co-captaincy between spouses and allows widows to inherit command, and no one blinks at a harbor pilot in skirts so long as she can read the chop at the Gate.

Everyday life runs to the rhythm of bells and bulletins. Tide-boards and current charts are posted at dawn in each harbor, chalked by the Pilots' Guild and countersigned by the Tollkeepers. Harbour offices publish daily notices of pressure in the city's mains, sprite-bed reserves, and any scheduled maintenance that might interfere with lifts, hoists, and cranes. Lurday is still the day of sermons and reflection, but market days line up with the arrival of the convoy packets, and every child learns to read at least enough to decipher a posted schedule.

Festivals are almost all oriented towards the realities of a society looking primarily to the sea; Fallow Tables, the Lurist feast of thanksgiving, falls into the time of the year that the last outward-bound convoy of the season comes home from the Gate, all flags flying.

### Economy and arms

Beniotti's true crop is passage. Harbour dues, tolls on convoys, pilotage, and the hire of escorts make up the lion's share of the ducal revenues. Fisheries and salt-pans, terraces of olives and grapes, small smithies making fittings and fastenings for ships and aeronaves, and a modest trade in charts and instruments fill out the coffers. The city also hosts several discreet banking houses whose books keep Guilders, Livres, and Kiriyan tallies, settling accounts between Balboa, Stranu, Vel Kiriya, and the eastern ports without letting any one polity dominate the books.

The Grand Ducal Navy is small but very far from ornamental. It consists mostly of light cutters and schooners tuned to the local gyre and the broken water around the Keystones, backed by a handful of heavier convoy escorts kept in good repair if not always in full commission. Its doctrine is not to stand in the line of battle but to know every reef, rip, and slack in its home waters, and to make those waters unhealthy for anyone the Anchors choose to call pirate. This habit of "selective piracy suppression" hardened into a real anti-piracy tradition in the later Age of Corsairs, when the Duchy, under considerable pressure from Lavion and the Commonwealth, began to sell its squadrons as honest escorts on Gyre and Ocean alike, rather than countenance being called discreet accomplices.

The great exemplar of that turn was Fleet Admiral Aniliki Nua'akali, remembered in chapbooks as the Tigress of Beniotti -- and not merely for the fact that she was a feline [[Beastkin]] bearing stripes. Born into a Beniottian noble house of Ukili'atan ancestry, she married local seamanship to bluewater instincts and made a career of hunting corsairs far into the Open Ocean. Her feud with her younger sister Unaliki, who broke from family and flag to captain the infamous corsair *Silver Sliver*, turned the navy's anti-piracy patrols into high drama. From the 1230s to the close of the Age of Corsairs the family quarrel was the source of tavern tales: Aniliki's natural talent for command made her the best pirate hunter of her age, but the breathless rumors of Unaliki's latest exploit made her the epitome of the freedom-loving scoundrel. Every dock from Saltmouth to the Mer-Hal would happily tell and retell different versions of why and how often the two estranged sisters came to blows in the course of their rivalry, half public posturing and half blood feud. Even today it still feeds tall tales, stage plays, and penny dreadfuls all around the Circulean, but more concretely it left Beniotti with a navy that had real institutional memory of how to strangle smugglers and pirate havens without strangling trade.

In the Golden Age of Aetherics Beniotti added air to sea, dedicating mooring towers on the main islands to allow small patrol craft to hop between them, and the harbor of Berler is a known stop for the pontoon liner connecting An-Mirda with Paltos to the nearby mainland. The Duchy cannot hope to match Lavion or the Commonwealth in tonnage, but its cutters, patrol craft, and watchful aeronaves make it very hard for anyone to raise the black for long within sight of the Keystone Isles without someone in Berler choosing, quite deliberately, to look away.

In times of crisis the Duchy can call upon a militia of mariners and islanders, but everyone involved would rather prevent crises than fight them. The Isles remember too well that when great powers come to blows, keys have a nasty habit of being fought over, not consulted.

### How others speak of Beniotti

In Lavion, Beniotti is sometimes dismissed as "the tollbooth" -- a necessary nuisance whose pilots and tollkeepers have to be paid because cutting directly through the Ring toward the Gate is a fine way to end up as a cautionary tale. Balboans grumble that the Beniotti line forgot its duties to Ertra the moment it tasted independence, even as their own captains quietly appreciate a neutral harbor beyond the Rush. Commonwealth merchants praise the Isles as a civilising outpost of Stranic numeracy and guild law, then complain in the next sentence about how stiff the pilotage fees have become.

From Vel Kiriya's highlands, Beniotti is a remote but valuable staging post -- the last friendly call before the High Sea or the first after it, a place where Kiriyan traders can find chaplains, factors, and notaries from home under one roof, arguing about exchange rates with the clerks who keep the books.

The islanders themselves, stubborn as barnacles, prefer a simpler story. They are the folk who keep the lights burning at the Gate. Without them, so they will tell you over their third cup, the worlds on either side of the Suurian Gates would drift farther apart, and the Gyres would carry their ships forever in circles. And if they earn a modest living for doing everyone the favour of connecting the seas, who could reasonably object?
