## Definition and Character

The Commonwealth of the Princes of the West -- called simply the Commonwealth or "the Principalities" -- is less a nation than a living pact. It is a framework that lets sovereign rulers keep their independence while acting together where common cause demands it. Order here is negotiated, not imposed; precedence is ritual, not absolute; unity is built by charter and custom rather than hammered by a single hand. The patchwork of petty thrones and city polities owes much to gavelkind inheritance, an old Svenic habit by which a father's lands split evenly among heirs. In Stranu this practice seeded a fine-grained map of lordship; east of Stranu it never truly took root.

## Historical Outline

c. 300 BR -- The Light sets Foot in Rimec-Suur. Remean stations along the Suurian Gate and Circle Sea littoral, already too far flung from the empire to see much support, are condemned and razed without much of a fight; the inland tribes convert in uneven waves, by choice or under pressure. Local clergy begin to blend Remean structural habits into Lurist rite, the first, faint strands of what will become Concordian thought.

0 AR -- Fall of Reme. The empire's center is destroyed; provincial outposts lose their tether. City captains and warlords consolidate by necessity, not permission.

56 AR -- The Accord of Salt-Mouth. Salt-Mouth, Arquillan, and Barvella settle shared harbor dues and mutual recognition of shipping rights. Maritime custom takes a first step toward law and will later inform parliamentary procedure.

143 AR -- The Liturgical Contention. Orthodox legates from An-Mirda collide with increasingly localist bishops in Stranu. The dispute foreshadows a western insistence on liturgical self-governance.

201 AR -- The First Concord. Nine principalities bind themselves to mutual aid, arbitration of water law, and diplomatic unity. The court thus convened at Santra is a gathering later remembered as the Commonwealth's first Parliament.

336 AR -- Sack of An-Mirda. Ejitwe the Thronebreaker burns the capital of the First Hierophancy. Western clergy refuse to acknowledge a successor; at Santra a synod articulates the Covenant of Concord, giving formal shape to the Church of the West. Concordian doctrine flowers in Vallafont and Dros-Mera.

367 AR -- The Charter of Santra. The gathering of principalities -- now encompassing most of the peninsula -- convenes to consecrate Santra as permanent seat of both Parliament and Synod, a Free Principality governed by a rotating council of prelates and magistrates. The Synod's autocephaly within the West is fixed in law and custom. This is the first mentioning of working "for the Common Weal of the Princes of the West", and gives the evolving polity its lasting name: the Commonwealth of Principalities is born.

3rd & 4th Century -- The Long Turn away from Gavelkind. The First Concord's water charters carved out indivisible "works of common necessity" and bound estates to keep obligations whole. That in turn pushed succession away from partible customs: in tight terrace basins and spring-towns, patrilineal primogeniture spreads; in the rockfall- and raid-prone marches, tanistry takes hold so the undivided duty falls to the line's most capable adult; in lagoon cities and port communes, elective customs let guilds and councils choose a captain-prince to hold both title and the entire slate of bonds. Cadets increasingly take dowries, stipends, or offices instead of gaining slices of land or water. By the time of the Charter of Santra, these "exemptions to partition" have hardened into local law and habit, and the map no longer splinters further every generation.

448 AR -- The Pact of Steel and Scrip. Border raiding by warlords from the south and southeast spurs a joint resolution for coordinated defense, levy quotas, and pooled finance through merchant leagues. For the first time, cooperation rests on principle rather than personal entourage. This becomes the "land peace" that forces animosities to be arbitrated by Parliament rather than be exacted by feud.

569 AR -- Founding of the Merseal Compact. Several of the Commonwealth's Circle Sea ports unite in a merchant league with semi-diplomatic standing. In Parliament, the Compact's delegates become a counterweight to princely voices.

888 AR -- Collapse of the Second Hierophancy. Schism and doctrinal fatigue lead to Orthodoxy's inability to settle on a single primate. The Commonwealth's concordant institutions absorb the shock and keep civic order through the Interregnum.

Early 900s AR -- The Age of Corsairs dawns. Svenic pilots chart the western currents; Stranic ports thrive. Letters of passage go to privateers and explorers; merchant-princes rise and find their feet on the floors of Parliament.

998 AR -- Founding of Lavion. Genovrast the Great crowns himself king. The Principalities note the danger and begin to moot a rotating High Prince as stabilizing figurehead.

1024 AR -- Elevation of the Concord Guard. Parliament empowers the old Guard of the Concord -- first founded in 201 as a small group in charge of the water laws the First Concord enshrined -- into a standing force to uphold decrees in the Commonwealth, and police Santra and other designated areas of common interest. It is modest in number yet momentous in its historical momentum, building the foundation of a unified constabulary that grows from the Guard.

12th century AR -- The Maritime Revolution. In the middle of the Age of Corsairs, arcannons mature from fickle curiosity to serviceable artillery and, alongside deep-keel hulls, change the economy of war and trade on the Open Ocean. The western harbors -- Salt-Mouth, Vallafont, Arquillan, Barvella -- flourish while the Gyre-side east looks on in envy.

1178 AR -- The Great Disputation. A decade of formal debates in Santra and Dros-Mera clarifies precedence. The High Principate is codified as a presiding office, elected from reigning princes, expressly subordinate to Parliament.

1246 AR -- Maritime Reforms. Parliament harmonizes port dues, declares protected trade corridors, and empowers the Merseal Compact to convene neutral squadrons. Merchant influence peaks; the Chartered Companies era germinates.

1341 AR -- Crisis of the Commonwealth and the Siege of Santra. High Prince Ereador syn Emsar of Velgora marches on Santra to claim a hereditary High Principate. Parliament and Synod refuse; the city endures under guard until a coalition lifts the siege. Emergency protocols are strengthened; rotation of the High Principate is reaffirmed in sharper ink.

## The High Principate

The High Prince or High Princess is primus inter pares, chosen only from sitting sovereigns, for a term of nine years, renewable once by vote. The office is expressly non-hereditary. In ordinance the High Prince merely presides; in practice the dignity of the seat and the breadth of its network give the office real mediating weight in crisis. The charge is service, not sovereignty -- to chair the Hall, to enact Parliament's will, to carry foreign policy in the Commonwealth's name, and to keep peace within the Concord. Speech in Parliament is always addressed to the Chair of Princes, by way of speaking to the High Scriptor that leads proceedings.

## Parliament -- The Ever-Burning Table

The Most High Court of the Commonwealth of Principalities began as an occasional court; it lives now as a near-permanent chamber in Santra. Princes may still speak in person; more often their plenipotentiaries hold the floor. Parliament drafts and ratifies inter-princely compacts, coordinates shared works and levies, adjudicates precedence, registers oaths, and, by near-unanimity, may declare a State of Concord to compel action from member realms. It cannot tax or field troops in its own name; it moves purse and pike through coordinated quotas, subsidies, and warrants.

Factions wax and wane with the century. Concordians hew closest to the Santran Synod and press for moral governance; Old Crowners favor narrow scope and robust local sovereignty; the Guilder League seeks tariff clarity, naval coordination, and uniform law for trade; New Realists argue for brighter statutes and a steadier Concord Guard and are eyed warily by all. None of these schools is a party, but a choreography of seats, city halls, and chancelleries that trade votes when aim and hour align.

Parliament is not a legislature in the modern sense, but a ceremonial instrument of balance. It is composed of envoys and plenipotentiaries sent by each of the 112 recognized principalities -- temporal, spiritual, and municipal -- who speak and vote on matters of mutual concern: trade privileges, port rights, precedence, and questions of faith and law. Decisions passed "in Concord" -- that is, by near-unanimous consent -- carry moral weight even when unenforceable. Parliament's strength lies in its ritual legitimacy, not coercive power, though it has grown some teeth over the long time it has governed.

The High Principate acts as Parliament's presiding officer, arbiter and representative rather than ruler. The High Prince presides over sessions and is bound by oath to "speak for the voices, not over them." Parliament's unwritten customs are as binding as law itself, their observance defended with near-religious devotion. These customs were codified in spirit, if not in writing, after the Crisis of 1341. The memory of that outrage reaffirmed ritual independence as the foundation of the Commonwealth's political faith.

## Santra -- Heart Without a Crown

Santra is the Commonwealth's legal capital and the Synod's neutral seat. It is a free principality ruled by prelates and magistrates in rotation. Its gates are open, its streets orderly, and its neutrality sacrosanct -- a status tempered but not broken by the siege of the 14th century. Here the Synod deliberates, Parliament debates, the High Prince resides when abroad from their home court, and laws are promulgated once passed. In the civic imagination, Santra is the city for all -- a hearth that keeps the Commonwealth's many kitchens warm without favoring any single table.

## Settlement and Ethos

The Commonwealth's settlement rests on three pillars: local sovereignty; common rule-craft; and a churchly order that answers Westward to councils rather than Eastward to a single see. Its law prefers charter to decree and precedent to flourish. In the quarrel between independence and identity the working answer is always the same: build the bridge you need, cross together, and then keep it in repair.
