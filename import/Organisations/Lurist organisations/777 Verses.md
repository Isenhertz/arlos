The Book of 777 Verses is the first and highest part of the Lore of Light, the direct fruit of the Prophet's revelation and the one text held in common by every rite and church of the Communion of the Enlightened. Its numbering is a dozenal count of seven gross, seven dozen, and seven -- or 1099 verses by decimal reckoning.

## Naming

The Prophet never set out to make a book. He composed canticles to be sung and taught in parables to be repeated, and what he left behind was a body of remembered speech held in the mouths of the faithful for the better part of two centuries before Tar-Essari ordered the first copying. The official written canon is younger than the text itself by the whole span of the early Judgeship, and nearly every name the Book carries preserves some stage of that history, or some use to which one part of it is put.

The oldest and plainest title is simply the *777 Verses*, and it is acknowledged in every rite without argument, since the count is the one thing about the Book that no one disputes. The *Book of Verses* is the canonical form, and exists chiefly to mark the distinction from the later *Book of Judges*, and in the east from the *Book of the Road* as well. The *Canticles* is older than both and belongs to the oral era, archaic in ordinary use but kept alive wherever harmonic song and prosody carry doctrinal weight, most of all among the Psalterians.

The Seven Gross Cycle is long enough to be bound on its own, and in that form it is usually called the *Prophet's Path*, with the Teachings and the Instructions following as a smaller companion volume. The east leans readily on the pairing this produces, setting the *Path* beside the *Road*.

The *Seven Verses*, or simply the *Verses*, is vernacular shorthand for the Instructions alone. What it most often means is a physical object: an inscription of the seven displayed at a household niche and honored there alongside patron saints and the remembered dead. Someone who "knows their Verses" is accordingly either a pious person or a person practiced at concealing how they break them, and which is meant depends entirely on the speaker's tone.

The *Enlightened Verses*, or the *Enlightened Words*, begins as a title of reverence for the Book and is commonly stretched to cover the canon entire, *Judges* and *Later Lore* included.

The *Ashen Book* strictly names the fragment recovered at An-Mirda, held in the North to be the oldest surviving form, a claim that passes over the Kharunite insistence that their own version stands plainly older than anything Tar-Essari's copyists produced. Northern clergy and Church Militant officials use the name pars pro toto for the whole canon, with the weight of the burned holy city behind it.

The *Plain Verses* names the Concordian rendering in contemporary speech, a thing unthinkable to Orthodoxy and Antidiscordianism alike, and the name is used as praise and as reproach depending on who says it.

The *Book of the Eternal Light* is the missionary's title, reserved for introducing the Book to heathens who have no reason yet to care how many verses it holds or which of its parts is which.

## Structure

The Book falls into three tiers of decreasing length. The Seven Gross Cycle runs from verse 1 to verse 700, a gross to each cycle. The Seven Dozen Teachings run from 701 to 770, a dozen to each Teaching. The Seven Instructions are the last seven verses, 771 to 777, and the last of them, Verse 777, gives the Ultimoversians their name.

The tiers are not merely a division of the material. They are a progression. The Cycle is the Prophet speaking of what he lived; the Teachings are the same man explaining what it meant; the Instructions are what a person is to do about it tomorrow morning. Length contracts, the voice moves from witness to teacher to plain command, and the reader is carried from the scattered particulars of one life toward a handful of sentences a child can hold.

The sevenfold division at every tier serves recitation before it serves theology. A gross is what two hands count in the dozenal manner, the joints of one hand tallied by its own thumb while the other hand keeps the dozens, so a reciter who reaches the end of a cycle with the second hand unclosed knows that verses have been lost. Seven cycles, seven Teachings, seven Instructions give a keeper of the text three nested counts against which any recitation can be checked.

## The Seven Gross Cycle

The Cycle recounts the Prophet's life in his own words. It is not a biography and does not pretend to be one. It proceeds as a long succession of episodes and parables, apparently independent of one another, grouped by theme and not by time, with no marked boundary between the end of one episode and the beginning of the next. Where the seams fall, and in what order the events of the Prophet's life actually stood, has been argued for the whole life of the faith, and the arrangement of the commentary literature imitates the arrangement of the Cycle itself, gathering like with like and leaving chronology to the reader.

The seven cycles take their traditional names from the concern that dominates each: the Household and the Treasury, Measure, the Stair, the House, the Road, the Forces and the Practices, and the Light. The titles are the work of later hands. The Book supplies no headings.

Lur-Illuhan was raised in an arbiter's household, married, fathered a child, and worked as witness and factor on the steps of the temple-treasuries. He was educated in numbers and letters, and the clients whose marks he attested generally were not. For a time he took part in the corruption then growing among the arbiters, and how long a time, and how deep his part, the Verses never state. He acted against that corruption in the end. He refused to leave the steps of the temple-treasury for seven days, even to eat. There followed a moment of absolute inner peace and rightness, which he received as illumination, and from the need to share the means of it came the itinerant life that occupied the remainder of his years.

Two details of this account carry more weight in later practice than their brevity suggests. The first is what he left behind when he took to the road. The Verses imply that his wife and child stayed; the same words permit a reading in which they followed, and the churches divide along that seam, the West reading renunciation where the Kharunites, the Concordians, and the Southern Reach read a household walking the road together. The second is the friend who bore his lamp, named nowhere in the Book. Tradition identifies him with Het-Zaphar, and the Book itself offers no designation of a successor, which is why the succession to the Prophet had to be settled by the faithful and not by the text.

That the Prophet stood so close to the arbiters made his station easy to inflate. The Theocracy of Eternal Dominion did inflate it, raising a treasury factor toward something nearer to rank, and the centuries after the Sack of An-Mirda walked the exaggeration back again.

The teaching itself is plain in voice and heavy in parable. Nothing in it is ornamental, and the sentences are those of a man trained to set down a contract without surplus. The parables are another matter. They were addressed to people who lived in the fourth century before the Fall on the Anish coast, to whom the workings of a deposit, a weighing, a pledge, and a witnessing were daily knowledge, and the Prophet explains them only as far as his hearers needed. Later ages needed a great deal more, and the commentaries, extrapolations, and rulings that fill the Later Lore exist very largely to say what a half-explained parable requires and at what point a vague obligation has been broken in earnest.

His anger is directed at what people do. He venerated the primordial forces as any Anish man of his day did, and the Book contains no quarrel with them anywhere. The quarrel is with the seal that is sold, the scale that is weighted, the fee charged to the poor for a hearing. The fervor of that quarrel is considerable, and it proved easy for later generations to widen from those who deal falsely to those who practice falsely, and at last to any who do things wrong.

## The Seven Dozen Teachings

The Teachings are the root of the creed. One is Three is Six is All is a formula distilled from their sequence, and each clause of it has its dozen verses.

The First Teaching, verses 701 to 710, treats the One Light. The Second through Fifth, verses 711 to 750, treat the Three: the Heavenly Triad of Body, Mind, and Soul, and the Threefold Practice of Habit, Attention, and Appetite, spread across four Teachings. The Sixth, verses 751 to 760, treats the Six, and its title is ambiguous in the old speech, carrying both the relation to the Six and the Six Relations, an ambiguity the Teaching itself never resolves and which the rites have resolved in their own ways. The Seventh, verses 761 to 770, treats the All: the cosmos entire, and the endless conservation in the Light by which nothing is unmade and everything is transformed.

That the Three alone receives four Teachings is the oldest argument in Orthodoxy's favor when it insists the creed's living weight lies in its first clause.

## The Seven Instructions

The seven closing verses are short, plain, and made to be recited. They are the first part of the Book a child learns and the last part an old woman forgets, and they are the common property of every rite without distinction. Six of them give the conduct a life is to be built on. The seventh gives the conduct required when the other six do not settle the matter.

**771.** Speak plainly, so that whoever hears you needs no one to explain you.

**772.** Do honest work, and let the work stand where your word cannot.

**773.** Restrain cruelty in your hand and in your mouth, and most of all in the hour you are owed it.

**774.** Take no more than your due, and know your due before you reach for it.

**775.** Attend what you have promised, in earnest and without pride.

**776.** Keep the relations of your place, in the direction they are owed.

**777.** Be still and listen well, and let harmony be your guide.

Each of the six opens onto ground the Teachings cover at greater length, and the fit is close enough that the Instructions are commonly used as the headings under which a conscience is examined at night. Plain speech and honest work answer to Habit. Restraint of cruelty and the measure of one's due answer to Appetite. Attendance on what one has promised answers to Attention. The keeping of relations answers to the Sixth Teaching, and carries the same ambiguity into daily practice, since the direction in which a relation is owed is precisely what the West and the East do not agree upon.

## Transmission

The Prophet composed the Verses. Het-Zaphar preserved the original canticles, Hadad-Phez was renowned for the precision of his recitation, and for a century and a half the Book existed in no other form. Tar-Essari ordered the first copying and formalized the Verse-Keepers to guard what was copied.

No complete original survives the Sack of An-Mirda. What remains is fragmentary and held in three places: the Patriarchate of the North has the Ashen Book, Aramoor holds several copies different states of harm, and the Kharunites have their own version which was set down independently, out of their own oral tradition. The Libraferentians collate the variant witnesses of all three.

The loss did not fall evenly. The Instructions and the Teachings are short and in constant use, carried whole in the memory of the entire Communion, and no fragment is needed to restore them. The Gross Cycle is long, heard seldom, and thick with idiom that was already obscure, and it is there that the disputed passages, the contested episode boundaries, and the rival chronologies are concentrated. The question of what the Cycle says is therefore inseparable from the question of who holds which fragment. Where the Northern and Aramoor witnesses agree against the Kharunite, the West calls the Eastern reading corrupt. Where all three agree, no one argues.
